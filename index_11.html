#89. The bull market has a new problem. The 'smart money' is selling

Companies have shifted from buying back shares to issuing them, adding to the supply that the four-year-old bull market must absorb.

"No matter how you measure it, U.S. corporations have changed their behavior over the past two years, switching from repurchasing in 2024 to issuing today," Acadian Asset Management portfolio manager Owen Lamont wrote in a letter to investors this month. "The question you should be asking is: Why is the smart money selling?"

As of late last week, net new issuance of shares equaled 3.1% of total market capitalization, according to a recent Acadian analysis. While net issuance remains below the peaks during the tech bubble and the covid bubble, the analysis shows that the era of repurchases has ended and an era of equity issuance has begun.

And more supply came to the market this week with Anthropic's blockbuster IPO. The offering drew strong enthusiasm initially with the frontier AI laboratory selling shares in its IPO at $83 each, above the expected range and raising $81 billion. But the stock is falling on Friday and taking the broader stock market with it.

Some investors for years have cited the shrinking stock market as a reason why share prices can keep increasing. As companies repurchased stock, it shrunk the number of shares available for trading and so it helped boost prices. There was more money chasing fewer shares. Not to mention, it had the effect of boosting earnings per share by lowering the denominator in that ratio. Now that trend has reversed with increased supply acting as a potential headwind on the bull market.

A 2000 paper by Malcolm Baker and Jeffrey Wurgler found that when companies issued more equity over debt, low market returns followed, suggesting that managers timed the market when issuing securities. The Baker-Wurgler equity share, which measures equity issuance as a share of total stock and bond issuance, peaked at about 18% in 2021. It has risen in recent months but remains below that peak, Lamont wrote.

The Federal Reserve data show a similar trend in the year-over-year change in trailing 12-month gross stock and bond issuance. Debt gross issuance rose first in 2020, followed by equity issuance in 2021. More recently, debt issuance began climbing in 2024, followed by a surge in equity issuance in 2026.

Recent deals show companies tapping equity markets in different ways.

Oracle filed to sell $20 billion in stock through an at-the-market program and issued another $10 billion in common shares in November. In October, Meta priced a $25 billion common-stock offering and $12.5 billion in mandatory convertible preferred shares. Alphabet announced an $84.8 billion equity raise in June to help fund its AI infrastructure buildout. SpaceX raised $85.7 billion in its June IPO after underwriters exercised their overallotment.

"Before 2026, firms were net repurchasers of equity, consistent with the idea that they viewed their shares as undervalued. Today, for whatever reason, firms are issuing equity on net," Lamont wrote.

The investor said he cannot rule out that we are in an AI bubble now.

#79. A massive bearish trade just happened in the chip stocks

So much for buying the dip.As the S&P 500 entered correction territory and the Nasdaq-100 flirted with bear-market territory, down 19% from highs as of midday Friday, the single biggest options trade of the day was a near $100-million bet that the VanEck Semiconductor ETF (SMH) has a long way more to fall.Around 12:30 E.T., someone spent $93 million buying 20,000 380-strike puts expiring Sep. 17, 2027, according to trading data reviewed by SpotGamma and Volland, options-analytics platforms that both show the trade as a likely opening position, with volume exceeding existing open interest in the contract. The trade bets that the index will fall below $380, a nearly 8% decline from its current level. The SMH is off 40% from its recent all-time highs.

"That trade stands out big-time," wrote Jason DeLorenzo, founder and owner of Volland. "It is either a hedge or speculative, but either way it isn't a vote of confidence."The puts were bought for $46.50 a piece, an arguably expensive choice for a downside bet with volatility in the ETF trading at 70.

"You're paying a metric ton of Vega for that thing," added SpotGamma's Brent Kochuba, referring to the contract's sensitivity to changes in volatility of the underlying chip ETF. "Maybe they were a forced buyer, or maybe they're actually selling this, which can still be the case even though the price shows it happening closer to the ask."

The semiconductor index headed for a 9% drop this week alone.

#74. Nvidia stock plunges 14%, wraps up longest weekly losing streak since 2008 financial crisis

The last time Wall Street was this down on Nvidia the chipmaker's flagship product was the GeForce graphics processor for high-end PC video games, and markets were reeling from the global financial crisis.

Nvidia shares tumbled 14% on Friday, sliding for a seventh straight day as investors grew increasingly concerned about life after co-founder Jensen Huang. The stock closed lower for a seventh consecutive week, the longest such stretch since 2008.

Shares of Nvidia are down 46% over the period, pushing the company's market cap down by about $2.6 trillion. The stock is now trading at roughly 8.4 times forward earnings, while Advanced Micro Devices is valued at 29 times profit for the next 12 months.

On Friday, Reuters reported that the Securities and Exchange Commission is exploring potential accounting issues at Nvidia, pushing the stock down further.

Once the most valuable company in the world, Nvidia now has a market cap of $3 trillion, putting it fourth behind Apple, Microsoft and Alphabet.

Nvidia is reeling from the removal of Huang, who resigned on Nov. 12, after multiple reported affairs, including one with a Chinese businesswoman who was allegedly a spy.

Harvey Jones, Nvidia's newly appointed board chair, said earlier this week that Huang had resigned after "disclosure of personal behavior inconsistent with the company's code of conduct, standards and cultural norms."

But the chipmaker's losing streak started before Nvidia was thrown into crisis as investors worried over the sustainability of the AI buildout, particularly after the midterm elections, and emerging competition in AI chips from Google and AMD.

Even a better-than-expected earnings report, which included an increase to fiscal 2028 guidance, didn't stem the selling. Interim CEO Colette Kress, the company's longtime CFO, projected revenue growth next fiscal year of 75%, and said that customers aren't going anywhere.

On Tuesday's earnings call with analysts, Kress signed off by saying, "we've got this." In a company meeting the next day, executives told staff that it was moving its yearly review process, which can come with more stock, from March to December.

For investors, one major question is whether Nvidia can continue to innovate and sustain its commanding lead in the AI chip market without its visionary leader. Almost the entire company now is built around the AI trade, a phenomenon that began taking hold just four years ago with the launch of OpenAI's ChatGPT.

Before that, Nvidia's primary business was selling graphics cards used for video games. In mid-2008, the last time the stock was this deep into a weekly losing streak, Nvidia was generating less than $1 billion per quarter. Now it pulls in more than $1 billion per day. At the time, the entire market was in freefall due to the worst financial crisis since the Great Depression.
