#83. A key report for where inflation stood in October comes out Tuesday. Here's what to expect

Falling gasoline prices provided some relief to consumers in October, but Federal Reserve officials are increasingly concerned that inflation pressures elsewhere in the economy are proving harder to contain.

Prices at the pump declined by more than 4%, according to the Energy Information Administration, providing a respite from a surge that had added more than a dollar a gallon over the past year.

However, the consumer price index for the month, which the Bureau of Labor Statistics will release Tuesday at 8:30 a.m., is expected to show only a slight let-up.

Meanwhile, Fed officials say they are worried that inflation initially driven higher by President Donald Trump's tariffs and the Iran war is proving more persistent than expected, with price pressures emerging in sectors beyond those directly affected by the shocks.

At a time when the central bank is increasingly confident about the state of the labor market, concerns over the cost of living are what are top of mind for the Fed.

"We've been moving up to what historically we'd consider full employment, and the big problem has all been on the inflation side. We go around, we talk to people everywhere in this district, all we hear about is affordability on the business side, the cost," Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee said Monday during an event in his home district. "So we've got to come to terms with this cost-side and inflation-side failure."

The CPI reading for all items is expected to show an increase of 0.3%. Core inflation, which excludes volatile food and energy costs, is forecast to increase 0.2%, according to the Dow Jones consensus forecast. While both would be slower than the respective September monthly gains of 0.6% and 0.3%, they still represent further price pressures.

There will be no annual reading for October because of the government shutdown a year ago, which prevented the BLS from collecting some data and compiling a full index level. Because of the data issue, there isn't a comparable figure for the index.

Bank of America, using an assumed baseline interpolating data for the period last year, estimated the annual growth rates at 3.8% for headline and 2.7% for core, both 0.1 percentage point higher than September and well above the Fed's 2% goal.

Goolsbee said the current run on inflation is defying what normally could be expected from supply shocks and tariffs, with the current situation intensified by the hyperscaler build-out related to artificial intelligence.

"It was supposed to have gone away by now, and we're now starting to see the inflation kicking up in areas that probably weren't from oil prices and tariffs, like a bunch of services. Now this stuff with the AI data center build-out is, to my eyes, starting to look more just like old-fashioned demand overheating," he said.

The massive investment in data centers is creating demand for construction workers, electricity and equipment, potentially adding to price pressures even as AI promises longer-term productivity gains, he added.

Though the Fed in October voted to hold its benchmark interest rate in place, markets are pricing in a better than 80% probability that the central bank will raise rates by a quarter percentage point at its next meeting in December, according to CME FedWatch data.

Goolsbee, who doesn't vote on the rate-setting Federal Open Market Committee this year but will in 2027, didn't commit to which way he was leaning on additional rate hikes, though he said current conditions could warrant action.

"The conventional wisdom there is: Be aggressive up front, to never let the economy overheat, because once it gets overheated, it's extremely painful to get rid of that kind of overheating," he said. "So I think we've got to watch out in that space."

The CPI release comes with economic growth strong — 3.4% in the third quarter for gross domestic product and tracking at 3.5% for the final three months of the year, according to the Atlanta Fed — and the labor market seemingly in solid shape as well. Nonfarm payrolls expanded by a much better-than-expected 165,000 in October, while the unemployment rate edged lower to 4.1%, though inflation-adjusted wage growth has been negative.

That puts the inflation reading squarely in focus.

Bank of America said it sees tariff impacts fading but that goods prices could be pushed higher because of AI demand. Further, the firm is cautious about "sticky" services prices as well as pressures from health insurance and autos.

The bank has been hawkish in terms of Fed expectations and sees a December hike as likely so long as there isn't an escalated stock market sell-off that might dissuade the central bank.

"This will be the final inflation report before the Fed's December 8-9 meeting. If our forecast is correct, we think it would effectively lock in another 25bp hike, all else equal," BofA economist Stephen Juneau said in a client note. "Absent an indisputably soft 0.1% [month-over-month] print, we doubt the inflation data will dissuade the Fed from hiking in December. The bigger risk to our call comes from financial markets."

#75. Hegseth impeachment articles introduced by Rep. Massie

Rep. Thomas Massie, R-Ky., on Monday introduced articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, triggering a vote in the House of Representatives as early as this week.

The unusual impeachment push against Hegseth by a member of his own party comes after Democrats swept to majorities in both the House and Senate during last week's midterm elections. Many Republicans, including Vice President JD Vance, have cited the Iran war Hegseth has led as a reason for the electoral bludgeoning.

Massie, who lost his reelection bid in a Republican primary in May, introduced eight articles of impeachment against Hegseth. They include waging war in contravention of war powers resolutions Congress passed to limit war, extrajudicial killings tied to boat strikes in the Caribbean that purported to target drug smugglers and suppressing free speech by targeting Sen. Mark Kelly, D-Ariz., a veteran.

"Wherefore, Secretary of Defense Peter Hegseth, by such conduct, has demonstrated that he will remain a threat to the Constitution if allowed to remain in office, and has acted in a manner grossly incompatible with his duties and the rule of law," the articles, which Massie read from the House floor, read. "Peter Brian Hegseth thus warrants impeachment and trial, removal from office, and disqualification to hold and enjoy any office of honor, trust, or profit under the United States."

Massie's resolution will force House Republican leaders to put the measure up for a vote by the end of this week. Impeachment resolutions are privileged, meaning the House must act on them, table them or vote to refer them to committee within two legislative days.

President Donald Trump once again defended Hegseth later Monday, telling reporters at the White House he will stand by the secretary even if he is impeached.

"He won two wars. He was in charge," said Trump, who was impeached twice in his first presidential term.

Trump also credited Hegseth with boosting military recruitment, claiming there are "waiting lists of people trying to get in."

Congress has the authority to impeach Cabinet secretaries, though it is a highly unusual move. Republican leadership in the House has not yet laid out how they plan to deal with the Hegseth impeachment, and it is unclear whether they have the votes to dispose of Massie's measure following the election.

The last Cabinet Secretary to be impeached was Homeland Security Secretary Alejandro Mayorkas in 2024 over his handling of the U.S.-Mexico border. Before Mayorkas, a Cabinet secretary had not been impeached since Secretary of War William Belknap in 1876. Neither was convicted by the Senate.

If the House votes to impeach Hegseth, the Senate would be tasked with holding a trial and voting on removing the secretary from office. If convicted by a two-thirds majority of the Senate, Hegseth would be removed from office.

Republicans hold a razor-thin 218-214 majority in the House, meaning only a handful of Republican defections could result in Hegseth's impeachment.

House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries, D-N.Y., reiterated his support for impeaching Hegseth on Monday at his first press conference at the Capitol since last Tuesday's elections. "Pete Hegseth has abused his power, undermined America's national security, and he's the architect of the reckless and costly failed war of choice in Iran," Jeffries said.

Hegseth has appeared to be preparing for an impeachment fight. The secretary put out a video last week defending his actions atop the Pentagon, and the department has led a media campaign to "Stand. Fight. Defend the Republic."

Hegseth on Monday announced he would hold a press conference Tuesday morning at the Pentagon. His most recent press conference way May 5, just over two months after the Iran war began.

#66. Jensen Huang affair puts Nvidia's future at risk, with no clear successor to the renowned CEO

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang's admission of an affair with a purported Chinese spy has raised questions about his future at the company, and what happens if the 63-year-old co-founder, who built the chipmaker into one of the most valuable businesses on the planet, is forced out.

Shares of Nvidia sank more than 4% on Monday, even as the broader market rallied sharply, after Huang acknowledged the affair in a message to staff on Sunday, and said that the board was launching an investigation into whether he was targeted by a foreign intelligence agency. The board said it has "confidence in Jensen," and that it has "no reason to believe that either national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised."

The New York Times first reported that the Chinese businesswoman was a spy for China's Ministry of State Security, according to people briefed on the intelligence circulated in a memo among the Trump administration in the spring. China has called the allegation a smear.

While Nvidia says Huang will continue to lead the company during the probe, pressure is rapidly mounting on the chipmaker, whose technology has powered the first four years of the generative artificial intelligence boom. Leaders from both parties are calling for briefings, and Democrats poised to take control of both houses of Congress are calling for investigations and new legislation "to stop advanced AI chips from fueling China's military," Sen. Elizabeth Warren of Massachusetts wrote in a post on X on Monday.

Huang has outsized influence over Nvidia, and is the sole face of the company with no clear second-in-command. He's also among the loudest champions of AI in the country, is critical to the financing of the broader infrastructure buildout and has a close relationship with President Donald Trump, giving Nvidia a front-row seat when it comes to discussions on AI policy, safety and regulation.

"The issue at Nvidia is that there is no clear successor to Jensen, and we had expected him to stay in this role for at least 10 more years," analysts at Melius Research said in a Monday research note.

Stacy Rasgon, an analyst at Bernstein, told CNBC's "Squawk on the Street" on Monday that, "Jensen is Nvidia. Nvidia is Jensen." He added that Huang "runs the thing pretty much top down," and "sort of pulls all the strings."

Nvidia didn't immediately respond to a request for comment.

Nvidia has long maintained a flat, non-hierarchical organizational structure. Huang has deep involvement in the company's day-to-day operations and frequently serves as its spokesperson in front of world leaders and at industry conferences. In addition to being co-founder and CEO, Huang is also Nvidia's president.

In September of 2025, Huang told CNBC's Becky Quick that he had as many as 60 direct reports. Internal records reviewed by CNBC reveal that, as of this week, he had 33 direct reports, including CFO Colette Kress, influential Chief Scientist Bill Dally, senior vice president of software engineering Dwight Diercks, and Ian Buck, best known as the inventor of Nvidia's CUDA software.

However, relative to some of his founder peers in tech like Meta's Mark Zuckerberg and Elon Musk at SpaceX, Huang has minimal actual control. He owns just over 3% of the outstanding shares and is one of 11 members on a board that has no official chair.

And in the risk factors of Nvidia's financial filings, Huang isn't specifically identified as a key person. Rather, the company says, "we are highly dependent on the services of our longstanding executive team."

"Failure to ensure effective succession planning, transfer of knowledge, and smooth transitions involving executives and key employees could hinder our strategic planning, execution, and long-term success," Nvidia says.

Still, Huang's influence is undeniable, so much so that the question of succession is one that's hardly broached among analysts and investors.

Timm Schulze-Melander, an analyst at Rothschild & Co. Redburn, wrote in a Monday note that Huang could be forced to step back from the CEO role if the board's investigation reveals "something materially more serious" than an undisclosed relationship.

But Schulze-Melander said Huang's potential resignation isn't a foregone conclusion of the investigation, pointing to the importance of his leadership at the company.

Nvidia's board "needs to demonstrate independence and protect shareholders without destabilizing a company whose success is unusually dependent on its founder," Schulze-Melander wrote. "Its initial statement suggests it is attempting to achieve both."

Several analysts said the investigation could lead to governance changes at Nvidia, including putting a transition contingency plan in place.

The Melius analysts wrote that Nvidia has a "strong bench" of executive talent across its networking, hyperscale and high-performance computing businesses, along with "technical expertise" stemming from Chief Technology Officer Michael Kagan as well as Dally, the chief scientist.

"While these folks can keep the engine running, Jensen is one of a kind, and his energy will be needed to maintain confidence in the AI trade," the analysts wrote.

— CNBC's Kif Leswing, Kristina Partsinevelos and Michael Bloom contributed to this report.
