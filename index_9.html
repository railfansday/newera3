#456. Target shares rise 7% as retailer posts best sales growth in five years, hikes guidance

Target on Wednesday reported its best sales growth in more than five years and beat Wall Street's revenue expectations, the latest signs of its turnaround strategy taking hold.

The retailer said its net sales grew 7.1% during the fiscal third quarter. Comparable sales rose 5.6%, compared with StreetAccount estimates of up 3.8%. That growth was boosted by a 5% increase in comparable traffic, Target added.

"[It's] a healthy sign that guests are responding to our changes and choosing Target more often," CEO Michael Fiddelke told reporters.

Digital comparable sales jumped nearly 10% during the quarter.

Encouraged by the growth, Target raised its full-year guidance, upping its net sales growth guidance by 1 percentage point to around 6%. It's also expecting full-year operating income margin rate to be around 6.2%, which includes roughly 1.1 percentage points of benefit from tariff refunds.

The company also said it's now projecting full-year earnings per share to be in a range of $10.65 to $11.40, compared with prior guidance of between $9.90 and $10.90.

"The changes we've made across our assortment and guest experience are adding up," Fiddelke told reporters. "While we're encouraged by the progress, we know there's more work ahead to sustain this momentum and deliver consistent growth over time."

Target shares climbed about 7% in afternoon trading Wednesday.

For the quarter ended Oct. 31, Target reported net income of $1.21 billion, or $2.65 per share, up from $689 million, or $1.51 per share, a year earlier.

This year, the company said it's invested approximately $3.8 billion in capital expenditures, up nearly $1 billion from last year. Because of those returns, Fiddelke said Target is now expecting to invest between $5 billion and $6 billion in capex next year.

On a call with analysts, he added that those investments will go toward more store remodels, technology, supply chain improvements, among other initiatives.

The company said it saw particular strength in beauty, which saw 9% growth, and food and beverage, where sales increased nearly 7%. The retailer added that net sales rose in all three months of the third quarter, with the strongest performance during the back-to-school season.

Fiddelke told reporters the quarter's strength showed "continued progress" toward Target's priorities that it outlined at the beginning of its turnaround.

As it looks to the holiday season, Target said it's marketing thousands of products priced at $5 and under, along with new artificial intelligence-powered shopping experiences to drive engagement. Fiddelke said Target is "encouraged" by shopping behaviors it's seen so far in the current quarter.

The big-box retailer has been showing signs of returning to growth, as it sees stronger sales trends after years of flat revenue. The results come after Target last quarter hiked its full fiscal-year outlook and said it saw "broad-based" strength across its various categories.

It's also lowered prices on more than 10,000 items in an effort to get customers back into its stores, especially as consumer sentiment lags and inflation remains high. The retailer said on Wednesday that it cut prices on an additional roughly 2,000 items during the third quarter.

Target's stock is up more than 70% this year.

#448. Todoist confirms 6% of customers’ details were stolen in data breach

Task management app Todoist said on Tuesday that a hacker gained access to its production systems and stole customer data, including email IDs, password hashes, and account creation dates. The company said around 6% of its customers were affected and blamed stolen credentials for the breach.

Todoist said the attacker used stolen credentials to access the company’s version control system and source code between November 22 and 23. The hacker then changed employee access controls and reached the app’s cloud infrastructure via an employee’s work account.

The hacker downloaded credentials of early Todoist users whose passwords were stored using an older hashing method, but didn’t change any source code, the company said. Todoist said it has blocked the attack path and reset passwords as a mitigation step, as well as disabled the Todoist for Outlook email add-on.

For a small number of people, about 0.001% of its userbase, the hacker also searched through tasks, comments, and attachment names to look for credentials for other services, like API keys and access tokens.

The hacker also pulled Todoist API tokens and Slack integration tokens, and looked for emails, addresses, and attachment file names through the Todoist for Outlook extension if it was connected to accounts and had permission to read emails.

The company said it has sent a notice to those affected and asked them to reset their credentials and change their login details in other places with the same password. The company is asking users to also update API tokens, reconnect integrations, and revoke or replace any access keys, recovery phrases, or credentials they had stored in Todoist comments.

Todoist said it has also reset its internal access structure and how remote command execution works in its cloud environment to prevent such incidents in the future.

Todoist’s founder and CEO Amir Salihefendić apologized to users about the incident on X, and said the company will take a closer look at why it happened.

The to-do app’s data breach is the latest in a burgeoning list of data breaches in which hackers use stolen credentials to access data and systems at enterprises. So far this year, we’ve seen several companies fall victim to this form of attack, including Uber and Klue.

Hims & Hers Health on Wednesday said it will offer five formulations of peptides and longevity treatments compounded in the U.S. as part of the initial rollout of its Peptides+ platform, all of which are not approved by regulators.

Even as it announced the new products, Hims & Hers acknowledged it has more to learn about how best to use the peptides and how effective they are — underscoring the uncertainty surrounding the growing space.

The new offerings come after the federal government eased restrictions on compounding certain unapproved peptides, which are short chains of amino ​acids. Those products have exploded in popularity among fitness and longevity enthusiasts who attest that they help with muscle growth, rapid injury recovery and anti-aging, even though most lack evidence from robust human trials.

Hims in late October launched an early access list for the upcoming peptide offerings, and more than 10,000 people signed up within 15 days, a company spokesperson said in a statement.

None of the company's specific compounded products are approved by the Food and Drug Administration. But Hims said they are authorized by law and formulated by pharmacies "that adhere to rigorous quality standards and are licensed and inspected by state boards of pharmacy."

The products include compounded formulations of sermorelin, a peptide that stimulates growth-hormone release and is marketed for recovery, body composition and anti-aging; glutathione, a peptide promoted ‌for anti-aging and athletic recovery; and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide, or NAD+, a coenzyme that plays a role in cellular metabolism.

The company will also offer compounded versions of vitamin B-12, a vitamin needed to make red blood cells, build DNA, and maintain healthy nerve function, and L-carnitine, an amino acid derivative that helps the body turn fat into energy. The FDA has approved certain prescription forms of both peptides for established medical uses, primarily for diagnosed deficiencies in those compounds.

Hims said it plans to add more peptides and therapies made in the U.S. over time. That includes BPC-157, an unapproved peptide derived from a protein found in human gastric juice that is marketed for healing processes. The company also aims to introduce another unapproved peptide called TB-500, which is derived from a naturally occurring protein in the body and is marketed for broader, systemic soft-tissue recovery and flexibility. Most of the research on both has been conducted in animals, not humans.

To receive the products, members on the Hims platform will start with an assessment of health history and goals with a licensed provider trained in peptide therapies, who can tailor a personalized plan to their needs, if appropriate.

The company said it intends to use its communication platform to check in with patients and ensure they are seeing desired results.

"If they're not, we need to do something about it," Dr. Anant Vinjamoori, Hims chief medical officer, told CNBC. "We need to, you know, adjust the dose when that's appropriate, or in some cases consider other compounds that's maybe a better fit. And of course, there will also be cases when we need to refer people to other care options."

However, Vinjamoori said there's still uncertainty over how to best use the peptides.

"We don't have all of this data that we're used to having with compounds like this," he said. "We have the opportunity to get it right, and I think we see that as our responsibility with a company of our size, our skill, and resources to really lead."

Vinjamoori added that people who have serious illnesses, such as cardiovascular disease, liver disease and kidney disease, will not be candidates for the compounds for now.

Meanwhile, Andrew Huberman, a well known neuroscientist, podcaster and Stanford University School of Medicine professor who recently joined Hims' board of directors, in a statement called compounds "real medical treatments, not just a wellness trend." He said Hims provides safe access through "trusted sourcing and physician oversight."

But health experts raised concerns about the broader mainstream marketing of peptides in the U.S. and accessing them on telehealth platforms.

"With peptides, I think you have to view them the same way you would view any drug that hasn't been rigorously tested," said Joshua Kritzer, a chemistry professor at Tufts University. "Without actual testing, all you have is anecdotal information, and that's just not enough information to put a substance in your body."

Kritzer said the lack of large clinical trials means researchers can't rule out potentially serious, rare complications from peptides — the kinds of risks that have led regulators to reject or pull otherwise effective drugs from the market after they underwent comprehensive testing in humans.

Without that testing, it's also unclear how telehealth firms, physicians and pharmacies can make decisions about what doses patients should take, said Paul Knoepfler, a cell biology and human anatomy professor at UC Davis Health. While some people rely on anecdotal data from real peptide users, "the dosing for these products really has not been established," he added.

Knoepfler said one risk is that injecting too much of a synthetic peptide into patients could trigger "weird immune responses that are really bad for one's health" because the body might recognize it as foreign.

"With the peptides, it's very loosey-goosey," Knoepfler told CNBC. "How does one even know what would be the effective amount for whatever you're hoping it will do, and what amount would have more risks?"

But he said the more contact patients can have with physicians around taking a compounded peptide, the better. Providers, including those tied to telehealth firms, should make sure patients are well-informed about the possible risks and unknowns of a given product and consider their preexisting conditions, cancer history and other medications, Knoepfler said.

That appears to be consistent with the approach Hims & Hers is taking. The company says patients who use the peptide and longevity products can also use its Care+ offering, which pairs treatment with ongoing support. That includes twice-a-year lab testing to track their progress and the ability to message providers trained in peptide therapy around the clock with any questions.

Still, Kritzer argued that having a doctor who only knows a patient from online, essentially mail-order platforms or websites "is not enough supervision to put you on a prescription medication," especially an unapproved one.

Hims' ability to legally sell some of these peptides stems from a major shift in federal oversight by the Health and Human Services Department. It issued a policy in October that says the Food and Drug Administration will not take enforcement action against licensed pharmacies, federal facilities or physicians that compound and dispense six unapproved peptides while formal rules are reviewed.

In practice, that means these pharmacies and other firms can now offer them, even though the peptides aren't yet on the FDA's official list of ingredients compounding pharmacies are allowed to make. It does not translate to formal approval of the specific peptides.

The policy followed a 2023 decision by the Biden administration where the FDA restricted compounding pharmacies from producing popular peptide formulations due to safety, impurity and immune-reaction concerns.

HHS Secretary ​Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has supported broader access to peptides, calling himself a "big fan" of the treatments and arguing that restrictions were driving Americans to potentially unsafe products from overseas. Kennedy has previously described using BPC-157 to treat a muscle injury.

The announcement also comes after Hims in February 2025 acquired a California-based peptide manufacturing facility equipped with advanced peptide synthesizers.

The evidence for peptide efficacy varies by compound.

A 2026 systematic review screening 113 studies, including both small trials on humans along with rodent research, found zero eligible clinical outcome trials proving that administering IV or intramuscular NAD+ improves anti-aging, energy, or wellness endpoints in humans.

There is some evidence for specific uses of certain peptides, such as potential weight loss. A review of 37 L-carnitine trials found participants lost about 2.7 pounds on average, though that analysis does not demonstrate that a compounded injection works the same way.

Meanwhile, vitamin B-12 injections are most commonly used to help people with a diagnosed deficiency rather than preventative care. U.S. survey data shows only 3.6% of adults are B-12 deficient.

Glutathione's evidence is thin, too. IV injections of it are widely marketed for skin whitening, yet not FDA-approved for this purpose.

A small study published in the Journal of Pakistan Association of Dermatologists evaluating IV glutathione showed that the treatment is "not very effective" for skin-lightening, and that it loses efficacy with time.

The FDA also said in August that it received reports of at least 30 patients who experienced adverse events after they received compounded IV glutathione alone or in combination with other ingredients from different compounding pharmacies.

All of the glutathione tied to the reports were from the same wholesale distributor and was labeled as dietary supplement grade, which is not an appropriate ingredient to make injectable drugs. Two of the Texas pharmacies conducted recalls related to elevated endotoxin levels in their glutathione injectable drugs.

These studies and reports don't speak to how Hims' exact combination will perform, but they do highlight the current limits on data studying the treatments and the questions surrounding them.

“When the last one is being produced, that’ll probably reignite things again, and for a few months after that,” he said.

#415. Jensen Huang broke Nvidia’s code of conduct, and his employees may not forget it

When Jensen Huang gave his first all-hands address since returning as CEO, there was an odd emphasis on Nvidia’s code of conduct. He admitted to violating the code, then emphasized that no one was exempt from it. But that “does not mean, the moment you violate a code of conduct, we walk you out the door,” Huang said, according to Wired. “Facts, circumstances, a lot of assessments” still matter. In other words, the code applies to everyone, but breaking it doesn’t automatically cost anyone their job, including, apparently, the CEO.

It’s an awkward stance for a company whose CEO has admitted to breaking its rules. A central part of modern human resources, a code of conduct can seem like a quasi-legal rulebook that lays out unacceptable behavior. But experts say most codes are better understood as a reflection of corporate culture. When an infraction goes unpunished, the main risk isn’t legal but cultural — that employees will feel embarrassed or even angry about the company they work for. That’s the risk Huang now faces at Nvidia.

Matt Plass, chief market strategy officer at LRN, an ethics and compliance training outfit, recently surveyed more than 2,000 workers across different industries for a study on workplace codes of conduct. As Plass sees it, the biggest risk is that employees will become ashamed of their workplace.

“People coming into the workforce now, ethics is incredibly important to them,” he told TechCrunch. “They’re really mindful of the ethical standards of their company. They’re really embarrassed and angry when senior leadership lets them down.”

The consequences of violating the rules aren’t always tangible, but they’re definitely real. Plass gave one example of a company that banned corporate travel to cut costs — only to have its CEO turn up in a VIP box at the Olympics.

“For over a decade afterwards, ‘the Olympics’ was used within that company as an example of the worst thing an employee could do. People would say, ‘Yeah, I shouldn’t have done that, but it’s not like I went to the Olympics.’”

Plass says companies that remove an executive typically do so after an investigation, then try to win back employees. “In most organizations they make a change at the top based on what’s happened,” he said, “and then they will have a hearts-and-minds initiative, to go out to their employee base and their customers and their investors and everybody else, and say, we get it. We’re sorry. We’ve changed. That’s essentially what they do.”

Of course, that playbook is harder to follow at a company like Nvidia, where Huang left without a clear succession plan, and there are serious doubts about whether the company could continue to thrive without him. In cases like that, Plass acknowledges there’s no easy answer.

“I would draw a distinction there between the kind of companies where the CEO is a kind of godhead figure … where the person and the company are almost impossible to separate,” he said. “There it’s much, much harder to deal with.”

In that context, Huang’s stutter-step over the code of conduct is easier to understand. Judging by the employee campaign for his reinstatement, Huang’s return hasn’t created a morale problem so far. But as the coming months put more pressure on Huang and his colleagues, sidestepping the code could end up costing Nvidia something harder to measure — its employees’ pride in the company.

#401. Grand Theft Auto VI proves why efficiency isn’t a virtue

It should have been impossible for “Grand Theft Auto VI” (GTA VI) to live up to the hype. Fans waited 13 years for the newest installment in the crime-adventure franchise, and Rockstar Games exceeded even those inconceivable expectations when the game finally came out last week.

GTA VI launched as the highest-rated game of all time on Metacritic and made $3.79 billion on release day to mark what could be the biggest entertainment event ever – more massive than “Avengers: Endgame” ($2.9 billion) or Taylor Swift’s Eras tour ($2 billion).

The 13-year wait certainly fueled the frenzy, and sales, around GTA VI, but some fans craved GTA VI because of what it represents in the emerging era of AI.

Even the biggest workaholics among us – startup founders – scheduled time off to play the game, including Spiry co-founder Dima Beseda, who told TechCrunch, “I reshuffled my workload to free up time around the weekends and even booked vitamin IV drips to keep my energy up.”

Yet some players felt a sense of anticipatory nostalgia as they wiped away a layer of dust on their Xbox to play the game they’d been looking forward to since they were kids.

“We do have a view that GTA is likely to be the last game of its kind to be made this way,” Ben Feder, managing partner at TIRTA Ventures and former CEO of Rockstar parent company Take-Two Interactive, told TechCrunch.

That’s because GTA VI was created entirely without generative AI.

“We’re kind of at this technological moment where this way of developing has sort of reached its natural frontier,” Feder said. “I don’t think you can say that games like this will always be around.”

As millions of people immersed themselves in Vice City, the tech industry witnessed another long-awaited moment: Anthropic became the first major AI lab to go public. At Nasdaq HQ, Anthropic executives gathered to celebrate the Claude maker’s $81.3 billion IPO. At virtually the same moment that Anthropic broke the record for the largest IPO ever, GTA VI was also breaking records with the highest-rated and best-selling game launch of all time.

But when the Anthropic team rang the bell on Thursday, the sound also signaled the end of an era in video game development, and perhaps even in the arts at large.

The AI industry operates with efficiency as its north star. The promise of this technology is that we will be able to automate so much work that we can expand the capabilities of humanity, avoiding needless time spent tinkering with ultimately fruitless ideas. Instead, a prompt goes into an LLM, and a response is spat out.

This technological revolution exploded into the world with some soul-crushing side effects: children are inundated with mind-numbing slop on YouTube, lawyers cite nonexistent cases in court, and tech companies like Meta lay off tens of thousands of people as part of a corporate-branded “Year of Efficiency.” But against the backdrop of the AI industry’s first major IPO, Rockstar has unveiled a game that about 5,000 people labored over for more than a decade.

The final product is breathtaking, looking so lifelike that it can be hard to distinguish gameplay footage from real life. These sprawling open-world games are usually made possible by procedurally generating new landscapes and objects, but Rockstar has taken the time to painstakingly craft every detail of the world, even developing 30,000 different nonplayable characters with unique appearances and personalities.

The video game industry is already grappling with the threat of AI. Google’s Project Genie, which launched earlier this year, allows users to create and wander around 3D worlds generated from prompts to an LLM. The stock prices of several video game companies faltered around the time of Project Genie’s release, perhaps because of investors’ uncertainty about the future relevance of video game developers.

“The notion that somehow new tools would allow an individual to push a button and generate a hit, market a hit, and bring it to many millions of consumers around the world, it’s a laughable notion,” Take-Two Interactive CEO Strauss Zelnick said at the time. “It’s just never been the case with entertainment.”

Zelnick’s fellow tech CEOs see different future, though. On a Meta earnings call this year, CEO Mark Zuckerberg promised that, “soon, you’ll have an AI that understands you and [can] even generate great personalized content for you.”

Elon Musk even went as far as proposing that a game like GTA VI could be made with AI.

“You won’t even have to ask,” Musk wrote on X. “AI will figure what video game you’d like best.”

This is the paradox of “Grandthropic” week. GTA VI is so impressively vast and captivating because its developers could make mistakes, experiment, and rework ideas, even if that meant delaying the game (twice). GTA VI was incubated with the exact inefficiencies that investors hope AI companies like Anthropic can help eradicate.

“Plenty of fantastic art — video games and elsewhere — almost by definition has to be created slowly, and there’s something to having that kind of inefficiency around it,” Feder said. “Creative output requires deletion, right? Getting to kind of a masterpiece oftentimes requires an enormous amount of deletion. And when you’re talking about a game of the kind of complexity we’re talking about, that kind of deletion ends up being very expensive, and it is by definition not efficient.”

GTA VI reeks of its own humanity – every small detail is loaded with intention, but it’s this impressive precision that keeps the world abuzz, triggering the kind of monocultural moment that has become increasingly rare in a media landscape that revolves around highly personalized social media algorithms.

The game forces the player to slow down, which is either frustrating or genius, depending on your sense of humor. People waited more than three presidential terms for the next installment in a high-octane action franchise, only to download a 212 GB behemoth of a game in which your earliest missions involve driving around doing errands and putting up drywall to make your girlfriend happy. Don’t worry — there are still car chases and shootouts — but in a moment when AI feels suffocating, GTA VI is such an artistic achievement that it is reaching audiences that don’t even like violent video games.

The ultimate fantasy of GTA VI is no longer to role-play as a criminal — the fantasy is just taking your time to relish the mundane and explore an expansive world that developers were given the time and space to perfect.

#398. A month with the iPhone Duo has changed how I use my phone

For years, the definition of a premium smartphone depended on its cameras, processors, and how light it felt when you stowed it in your pocket. The iPhone Duo challenges that concept by asking consumers to pay more for a fundamentally different smartphone experience, even if it means sacrificing what they’ve long thought of as high-end features.

In a little under a month of using the Duo, I’ve found that the trade-offs I was initially concerned about matter far less to me than I had expected. What’s been more interesting is how the Duo’s foldable form factor has changed my everyday habits, including how I interact with apps, which apps I use, and how I organize and personalize the phone itself.

I also easily forgot a number of my initial concerns about switching to the Duo. The apps, for the most part, are now optimized for the Duo; I’ve reacquainted myself with Touch ID; and I don’t miss the Pro Max camera on most days.

That’s not to say the Duo doesn’t have its shortcomings. I still miss that telephoto lens sometimes, and I’m still longing for Face ID — though not as much since Apple shipped a face unlock feature in the latest update. But after a month of using the Duo, I’ve found these compromises have had less of an impact on my day-to-day experience compared with the benefits that the new form factor brings.

I was surprised by how much I’ve grown to appreciate the Duo’s multitasking capabilities. Initially, I didn’t think I’d regularly use it to view two apps side by side, given the still fairly limited screen space even while the phone is unfolded, especially compared with an iPad. But I’ve found myself snapping apps to both halves of the screen more often than I had imagined.

For starters, Apple has made this side-by-side mode incredibly intuitive and easy to use — you simply drag the app over to the left or right side of the screen and drop it into the target box that appears.

I have regularly taken advantage of this feature to do all sorts of things.

I really enjoy keeping X open as I read through the news, which allows me to switch back and forth between the article I’m reading and real-time reactions to it. Sometimes I’ll keep email or Slack open on the side while I’m working on something else so I don’t miss an important message or notification.

And much like the overstimulated members of Gen Z (which I am not), I sometimes like scrolling social media while engaging with some other media off to the side.

Case in point: I passively watched Netflix’s “Love is Blind” reunion while reading the gossipy tweets and Reddit posts about the main characters on the other side of the screen. This sort of TV watching behavior used to be called second-screen viewing, but now it’s morphing into something new. Everything is happening on one screen, so should we call it “Duo screen viewing,” perhaps? It makes me long for even more interactive TV experiences where commentary and content are combined.

I’ve noticed another shift in my behavior since adopting the Duo as my main device. When I’m out and about, the Duo feels more like an (arguably heavier) iPhone mini. For those who still miss smaller iPhones, the Duo is the next best thing. Despite its weight, the phone feels small. You can cover it with one hand; you can type with a thumb; you can reach the top and bottom easily; and you can even fit it into your front and back pockets. The Pro Max now feels unbelievably strange in my hand — how was I ever comfortable wrangling this giant rectangle?

The Duo’s small screen works well when you’re on the go, especially for short interactions like responding to a quick text or pulling up directions to a destination. It’s not as great for mindless scrolling, which I appreciate, as it forces me to engage with the real world instead of killing every moment of boredom or unease by opening up apps.

But when you’re home or somewhere with time to kill, you can unfold the screen and enjoy your apps super-sized. It’s the best of both worlds.

I find that I instinctively unfold the phone when I’m preparing to watch videos, because the larger screen is best for that. It’s also unfolded if I’m shopping, as it’s more convenient for looking at products, zooming in to examine the details, or complete checkout forms.

Generally, I also unfold the Duo when I’m actively using apps. This setup has also allowed me to rethink my Home Screen layout: With the Duo closed, I prefer to see just widgets on the small screen with key information like weather, time, battery life, and so on. Meanwhile, the only app icons on the Home Screen are those in the dock.

In the weeks since its launch, most of the apps that weren’t yet optimized for the Duo have been upgraded to support its foldable form factor. In mid-November, Apple said there were over 200,000 Duo-ready apps, and in my experience, that certainly feels true. A majority of my so-called “daily drivers” — with the glaring exception of key Google apps like Gmail and Drive — have dropped the black band on the side of the screen that was frustrating early testers of the phone.

Most apps now take advantage of the available space, whether that’s on the Duo’s small front screen or the wider, unfolded one.

I’ve also found it interesting to see the different interpretations developers have had for how to best optimize their app for Duo.

Some have used the form factor as a way to bring in some sort of novelty: there’s a note-taking app that opens and closes like a notebook, with pages you can write on with the Apple Pencil; a retro Walkman-like music player; a tiny typewriter; a gaming handheld; a game where you close the phone to make grilled cheese.

As much as I love the whimsy of this group of apps, I appreciate how my everyday apps feel better to use on the unfolded screen. Spotify, at last, feels less cluttered when it has more screen real estate. A number of note-taking, list-making, and calendar apps also feel somehow more usable when spread out.

The Netflix app is actually fantastic with its support for multiple viewing modes, which include playing video on the closed outer display, the “laptop” orientation with its big player controls, and the “tent” mode.

Other developers have similarly reimagined how their app would work if it had originally been designed for the Duo’s small and larger screens.

One good example of this is Sofa, a handy utility for organizing the books, movies, TV shows, podcasts, albums, and games you want to check out. When the Duo is closed, its navigation is tucked off to the right side in a vertical bar, while the main screen focuses on the items you’ve saved. But when the Duo is unfolded, the number of items displayed on the screen also expands into a two-column layout, with more of your items featured on the screen and your lists on the left side. It doesn’t feel like two different apps, but like you’ve opened up the app itself and given it more space to spread out.

In terms of your daily social media scroll, apps like TikTok and Instagram have been updated to be perfectly usable on the Duo, whether the phone is open or closed. When closed, TikTok now stretches the video to cover the screen, instead of showing the ugly black bar. Scrolling short-form videos on the Duo, while unfolded like a slightly smaller iPad mini, has become my preferred way to watch.

Here’s another surprise: I’ve spent less time with my Kindle, and am instead reading books on my Duo. Unfolded, the Duo is a decent enough replacement for an e-book reader, albeit it can be a bit more stimulating as it’s not an e-ink device. However, the matte inner screen isn’t quite as bright and distracting as your typical glossy OLED display. There’s less glare and softer reflections, but there’s also something about the display that just feels calmer than a smartphone, allowing you to more fully engage with a book’s text.

Unfolded and held vertically, reading on the Duo feels a lot like reading on the standard Kindle, at least in terms of hand-feel. And putting your phone into the “Do Not Disturb” focus mode can help you not be distracted by incoming notifications and messages while you read.

You can also unfold the device while keeping it in landscape orientation, with the screens bent slightly inward like a real book. For even more fun, you can turn on Kindle’s page-turn animation, which makes it seem as if you’re turning the page in a physical book. And I love how you can “performatively read” using an app like Book2Life or Actually Read — they display the book’s cover on the Duo’s outer screen as you read the text on the inner screen.

E-book readers will likely find they can carry one less device when they’re out and about, because the Duo can stand in for whatever Kindle or other reader you typically keep in your purse or bag. You can save the Kindle for other times, whether that’s at home, on long travel days, or when you don’t want to worry about sand getting into your $2,000 phone.

On the big-screened iPhone 17 Pro Max I used to carry, I tended to keep a PopSocket of some sort on the back because the phone was both heavy and large, which made it hard to hold for longer periods of time. The Duo is similarly heavy, but its shape makes it far easier to hold in one hand without the extra help.

Folded, you can do the usual one-handed hold where the bottom edge of the phone rests on your pinky, your other three fingers cradle the back, and your thumb is free to navigate the screen.

Unfolded, the Duo’s like using a small Kindle or iPad mini, where two hands are required. You also don’t need a PopSocket for balancing the phone on the side, because it can be propped up like a tent or opened like a tiny laptop. Or, if you prefer, Apple and others offer Duo cases with a built-in stand. There’s something about the Duo that seems to have kicked off a new iPhone personalization trend, at least judging by what I’ve been seeing on social media.

You can buy cases with and without built-in screen protectors, and some use adhesive to better stick to the outer screen, while others don’t. Some cases let you customize the band around the outer screen with a different color than the case that protects the rest of the phone.

Then you have cases that cover the hinge, while others leave it exposed. There are cases that offer holes where you can attach straps, wristlets, and charms. Some of those have attachment points at the bottom and others on the side.

Essentially, there are a lot of options for customizing how you want to carry the Duo and how you want to express your own style.

I’m also now starting to see whimsical cases that come with attached pens. (Thank goodness — most of the pen cases so far have been kind of plain!)

I also believe there’s something about the Duo that particularly appeals to women. Maybe because it’s small, or maybe it’s because you can attach a strap to its side and carry it like a little purse. Or maybe because, like a purse, you can deck it out with chains and charms — a choice that’s grown increasingly popular as young people have embraced whimsical, retro tech as a way to express their individuality and sense of fashion.

The Duo easily lends itself to this trend, perhaps even more than a regular iPhone. It becomes an accessory you want on display, not one buried in a bag.

Before switching to the Duo, I spent a lot of time thinking about what I’d be giving up by moving away from the Pro Max.

I’m surprised to find that, a month later, I only think about what I’d have to give up if I switched back to a traditional iPhone. It’s not just the extra space of the unfolded screen I’d miss — it’s all the little habits I’ve developed around having different screen sizes for different purposes.

The Duo makes me pause and think about how I want to use an app. That small moment of deliberation, before I even open the phone, has helped me break some of my more mindless smartphone habits, particularly around social media.

That’s not a benefit I could have anticipated if I’d judged the device by its specifications alone.

#351. Why the UK is dithering over what to do about e-scooters

Why the UK is dithering over what to do about e-scooters

When I step out of Southampton Central railway station, it takes me just seconds to spot an e-scooter. In this city, these devices have become a normal part of the transport ecosystem, along with e-bikes.

"It's just the most convenient way to commute from home to the station and back," says Keith Reed, who I speak to as he arrives at the station to catch a train to London. Keith says uses rental e-scooters twice a week. His alternative option would be his bike, but he'd then have to worry about where to leave it.

Around the city, there are parking areas for bright orange rental devices everywhere - but they're not necessarily a permanent feature. Southampton hosts one of the numerous government-backed trials around England. This one has been going on since 2021.

A study suggests around half of users of e-scooter do so in conjunction with another mode of transport - like Keith, who rides to the railway station

It also doesn't take long to find e-scooters which aren't part of the trial, but instead are owned by individuals. I see a man riding one along the pavement outside a parade of shops. He doesn't want to stop and talk.

These privately owned e-scooters are illegal to ride on public roads across the UK, but they've proliferated regardless. One expert told me the situation was a "Wild West".

Among people I spoke to in Southampton, the same concerns came up time and time again: "They're a blinking nuisance," says elderly pedestrian Fred. Another shopper, Martin, says he's "very concerned" whenever he sees them racing through precincts: "An accident's almost inevitable."

The endgame of the trials is to reach a decision on whether to make permanent hired e-scooter schemes such as the one Keith uses - and whether to bring in legislation that would legalise and regulate privately owned e-scooters too.

But six years since the trials started around England, there's still no conclusion - and as the UK government has responsibility for road traffic legislation across Great Britain, officials in Scotland and Wales say they are waiting on Westminster to act.

So why is it taking so long for the government to decide what to do, and what are the consequences of delay? What does the evidence tell us about how safe these things are?

And why are people using them in the first place - are they here to stay as a useful part of the transport offer, or will they end up being viewed as a pest on our streets?

You'd recognise an e-scooter if you saw one. The Department for Transport's (DfT's) definition of an e-scooter includes no pedals, two wheels and a maximum continuous power rating of 500W.

And so far, the trials around England appear to have proved popular. According to DfT research, fom January 2022 to May 2024, 36.9 million trips were made across 30 trial areas outside of London. On average, nearly 19,500 e-scooters were available to hire every day.

Dr Yasir Ali, a reader in Transport Modelling at Loughborough University, says e-scooters are "complementary" to other modes of transport, rather than replacing them. Due to factors such as range and being exposed to the elements, they are less suitable for longer trips, rural areas with less infrastructure or in bad weather. They work best as "a first- and last-mile solution" in urban areas, to reduce reliance on cars for short trips.

Shared mobility charity CoMoUK's 2025 report suggests that for more than half of the people using rental e-scooters, the most common use was travelling to work or school. Its analysis of DfT data suggests the majority of people using hired e-scooters are male and under 35.

"Sometimes it's a bit difficult to take the bus from dot to dot", says Yan Zhang, who works at Southampton university and doesn't drive, "so for shorter distances, I'll take the scooter."

Like Yan Zhang, e-scooter users are less likely to have a car, CoMo's report says - this is partly explained by official trials' concentration in urban areas

Around half of respondents to CoMoUK's online survey used it in conjunction with another mode of transport - such as riding to a railway station, like Keith.

Earlier evidence from a national evaluation of the trials published in 2022 found that although some users would have driven instead had they not had access to an e-scooter, the biggest "modal shift" - meaning switch in ways of getting around - to e-scooters was from walking. Given walking is an active mode of travel, this has raised questions over how significant the public health impact of e-scooter use really is.

And the usage of privately owned e-scooters isn't reflected in this research. The DfT has said between 760,000 and 1.2 million people in England aged 16 and over own one - despite the fact that it remains illegal to ride them in public.

In the eyes of the law, e-scooters are classed as motor vehicles. Outside of the trials, they can't be insured, and thus can only legally be ridden on private land, with the owner's permission.

Parking bays for e-scooters and e-bikes are available across Southampton

The trials themselves are bound by strict rules. The devices' top speed is limited. Businesses who operate the pilots must insure them. Users must have a full or provisional driving licence, and they must not ride on the pavement. Helmets are recommended but not mandatory.

Richard Dilks, CEO of CoMoUK says "mass-scale use of something that is inherently low-carbon, low-impact, low-cost" is a "good thing" but "we also don't have rules of engagement about how they should be ridden".

And as a result of the delays in introducing regulation, Loughborogh University's Yasir Ali says "we are increasingly in a situation where the law and reality have diverged", with "the disadvantages of e-scooter use without securing the full benefits of regulation".

Back in Southampton, I meet a lady pushing a black e-scooter, who doesn't want to give her name. She says nobody told her when she bought it that it was illegal to ride on the streets.

The Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency has a unit that carries out surveillance of retailers to check they're complying with the law. But earlier this month, an investigation by the Press Association news agency discovered some retailers were advertising them for commuting purposes.

Police tell me they regularly see children illegally riding private e-scooters. Margaret Winchcomb, deputy executive director at the Parliamentary Advisory Council for Transport Safety (PACTS), says some are being specifically marketed at kids.

Police say they will seize privately owned e-scooters that are ridden illegally

So what can police do? Forces regularly launch crackdowns where they seize illegally used devices, and often crush them. Each year, awareness campaigns run urging parents not to buy their kids an e-scooter for Christmas. But enforcement is a challenge.

Insp Matt Boiles from Avon and Somerset's road policing team says problems arise even with the legal trial devices - for example, people riding them after drinking alcohol, which counts as drink-driving. Riding with another person on the back is another common issue.

But officers see private e-scooters being illegally ridden in public places "all the time, every single day" and they can be really hard to catch - it isn't always safe for police in a car to pursue someone on an e-scooter, he says.

Insp Boiles despairs over parents buying them for children: "We do have parents attend a police station quite a lot, getting very, very upset that their child's Â£500 e-scooter has been seized." But "the legislation is really, really clear that you can't have it back".

There's a potential risk of fire if unregulated devices with lithium batteries are left to charge unattended. Most train operators have banned e-scooters on board for this reason.

But there's also the fact that riders are unprotected in the case of falling off or being hit by motorised vehicles. And - along with e-bikes - they can end up involved in collisions where pedestrians are hurt. Many trial e-scooters in the UK are restricted to 12.5mph, but privately owned scooters' speeds aren't.

Some coroners have even raised concerns. In 2022, a coroner in London called for action after the death of 14-year-old Fatima Abukah on a privately owned scooter.

According to the DfT, in 2024 - the most recent year for which figures are available - 1,390 casualties were recorded in collisions that involved e-scooter in England, Scotland and Wales, three more than in the previous 12 months. The vast majority, 1,096, were e-scooter users themselves.

Young boys and men appear to be over-represented in the stats. Of the casualties, 302 - the most of any age and sex category - were males aged 10-19.

Six deaths were recorded in 2024, unchanged from the previous year. Five of these were riders and one a pedestrian.

Although the numbers aren't conclusive, it is thought more accidents are happening on privately owned e-scooters. Winchcomb says police statistics "aren't reflective of the number of injuries".

Nonetheless, campaigners believe there is enough evidence to show that regulation is urgently needed.

Carly Calland's 14-year-old son Jacob died in March 2025 of a catastrophic head injury. He was a passenger on an e-scooter that was involved with a collision with a car.

If privately owned e-scooters are legalised for public use, Carly believes, there should be mandatory helmets, a ban on carrying passengers and penalties for parents that allow children to ride illegally.

"If Jacob was wearing a helmet that day, he would still be here," Carly, from Wythenshawe in Greater Manchester, says.

Carly is not against e-scooters. They "are really good for people to get to work, and they are eco-friendly", she tells me, but "they just need to be used in the correct way."

What happens if the current situation goes on? Her answer is emphatic: "More deaths."

The last evaluation of the trials in England was in 2022. New findings are expected by early next year.

In recent years, successive British transport secretaries have supported the idea of a new law to legalise and regulate the e-scooters available for sale. E-scooters were mentioned in the 2022 Queen's speech, but nothing materialised.

"We're going from the kind of weather activity which takes out an airport, and which the system kind of copes withâ¦ to a situation where, with very little predictability, it would be possible to lose all of the London airports from real-time operation quite swiftly and with little notice".

Such a scenario, he thinks, would make the disruption for passengers caused by the incident in June seem relatively minor â and could even put lives at risk. But how likely is it?

For controllers at National Air Traffic Services's (NATS) main control centre in Swanwick, Hampshire, thunderstorms are a major preoccupation. When I visit, it is a hot and humid day, there are ominous dark clouds in the sky, and heavy, thundery downpours are hitting the surrounding region.

In Terminal Control, the part of the building responsible for flights heading to or from airports in London and southern England, those same storm cells are being monitored continuously. Their progress is tracked on large radar screens, while at the same time weather forecasts are carefully scrutinised.

Air traffic tends to come in waves during the day. Right now, at lunchtime, it is relatively quiet and the storms have yet to affect major routes. But with activity expected to intensify in the afternoon, staff are already preparing for what could be a tricky few hours.

Stormy weather can shrink the available airspace, meaning traffic has to be restricted and rerouted. If planes are unable to land at one airport, for example due to high winds, they may need diverting to another. But that can create its own problems; if too many unscheduled aircraft land at an already busy airport, it might not have sufficient infrastructure to accommodate them all.

Heavy rain and storms can make flying harder

Planes need fuel and somewhere to park. Passengers need food and facilities. Ultimately, if there isn't space, it can simply refuse to accept all but emergency traffic.

This is where the scenario set out by Atkinson comes in. He warns of a situation where traffic is diverted from Heathrow, Gatwick, Luton and Stansted at the same time, leaving aircraft coming in across the Atlantic and a large number of European flights all looking for somewhere to land at the same time, and some with dwindling fuel reserves.

This would at the very least create serious disruption for passengers, who might end up hundreds of miles from their destinations. But it could also, he thinks, create "a very serious risk event, such as an aircraft running out of fuel in the sky".

Aircraft are legally required to carry enough fuel to take them to an alternative airport if necessary, and to allow them to fly for an extra 30 minutes on top of that.

Not everyone shares Atkinson's concern. Former airline executive Andrew Charlton, who manages the Swiss-based consultancy Aviation Advocacy, thinks the risk is low.

"You've always got to imagine the worst-case scenario", he says. "But I also think we need to keep that worst case in context.

"Yes, it's possible to imagine it. But I'm also fairly comfortable with the ability of this industry to react well and make good decisionsâ¦ of all the things that wake me up at 3amâ¦this is not the top of that list."

Thunderstorms are caused by what is known as convective activity, where moist air close to the ground warms rapidly and rises quickly. As it rises, it expands and cools, allowing the moisture it carries to condense, forming large clouds. More heat means more convective energy, potentially creating instability. In addition, for every degree the Earth warms, the atmosphere can hold about 7% more moisture, increasing the probability of heavy rainfall.

It is not clear whether this means we will actually get more thunderstorms in the UK and across Europe, as so many variables are involved beyond the simple matter of energy and moisture. Some parts of the world may even get fewer. However, studies have shown that higher latitudes, particularly in the northern hemisphere, will see the biggest increases in favourable conditions for storms to form, and those that do may be more violent. According to the UK's Met Office, "while we may not see more thunderstorms overall, the ones we do experience could be more impactful".

Where possible, pilots will avoid flying through convective weather. Even though aircraft are designed to cope with severe conditions, storms can produce intense turbulence, capable of damaging the structural frame of the plane, as well as throwing people around the cabin if they are not strapped in. Hail can also be a big problem, potentially cracking windscreens or causing engine problems. So, however much inconvenience it may cause passengers, crews will invariably put safety first.

New technology helps pilots navigate tricky flying conditions

Onboard weather radars can help aircrew to see up to 320 nautical miles ahead. They also have access to live weather forecasting resources online. But thunderstorms can move quickly and, according to Prof Guy Gratton, an expert in aeronautics at Cranfield University, these systems are essentially "tactical rather than strategic". In other words, although they enable pilots to see storm cells on their screens from a relatively short distance away, and request a diversion, they do not allow for detailed long-distance planning.

The result is that unplanned diversions often happen late in flight, which is likely to mean burning more fuel. If storm delays do become more common, says Andrew Charlton, then aircraft will have to load more fuel as a matter of course, in case there is trouble brewing ahead. "Aircraft are going to have to fly further, and they're going to have to factor in the assumption they're going to fly further," he says. For an airline, fuel is one of its major costs â and if the fuel bill goes up, he says, it is inevitable passengers will pay more.

More intense storms would be likely to make air traffic management in busy regions harder and potentially lead to more delays and cancellations. However, it is possible some of this could be mitigated using new technologies.

Both Heathrow and Gatwick, for example, have moved away from mandating minimum distances between incoming aircraft in favour of a system which continually calculates spacing times based on wind speeds and aircraft types. In practice, this is meant to allow controllers to reduce the distance between landing aircraft, particularly in high winds, and get them on the ground more quickly. According to NATS, it has successfully improved landing rates and reduced delays by a significant margin.

However, there could be other effects from more severe storms as well. Heavier rainfall, for instance, increases the likelihood of flooding. In a worst case, this can paralyse airport operations, as happened in April 2024 when torrential downpours hit Dubai International Airport. It briefly had to close its runways, while disruption lasted for two days. More than 1,200 flights were cancelled, affecting tens of thousands of passengers.

Passengers were delayed during the floods in Dubai

But even if runways and taxiways themselves are clear, and aircraft are able to fly without any difficulty, passengers may struggle to catch their flights if roads, railways or car parks are flooded.

Protecting against such risks can be expensive. Gatwick Airport, which experienced severe flooding over the Christmas period in 2013, has spent tens of millions of pounds on mitigation strategies in recent years â although those figures are dwarfed by the more than Â£2bn it is expecting to invest in its current expansion plans.

The problem of clear-air turbulence is also a real one. It cannot be seen, and is becoming increasingly common as the climate warms.

This is when air moves violently at high altitudes with no clouds or other visual cues to help a pilot avoid it. For passengers, it usually only means a little bit of bumpiness, but occasionally it can be a lot more serious. On 1 March 2023, for example, a Lufthansa flight from Austin, Texas to Frankfurt encountered what the airline described as "brief but severe" turbulence.

Those aboard described how passengers and flight crew were thrown around the cabin as the plane suddenly lost altitude. After it made an emergency landing in Washington DC, seven people were taken to hospital.

While still rare, this kind of turbulence is already much more prevalent than it used to be and is likely to become even more so.

"You know the projections are pretty dire: doubling, trebling or worse in the amount of severe turbulence on many busy flight routes around the world," says Paul Williams, a professor of atmospheric science at the University of Reading. He says incidents over the North Atlantic have increased by 55% since 1979.

Nevertheless, he remains confident solutions can be found, including improvements to forecasting and making use of laser-based technology.

There is another climate-related threat to aviation, which may prove challenging to overcome: rising sea levels. When Hurricane Sandy arrived off the US East Coast in October 2012, it provoked a storm surge that flooded the city. The two runways at New York's LaGuardia airport were left under water for several days, preventing it from reopening even after the winds had subsided.

Significant flooding that would have affected the New York City area once in every 500 years in pre-industrial times is now experienced every 25 years or so â and could occur every five years by the middle of the century, according to one major study. That makes LaGuardia, built on reclaimed land next to Flushing Bay, appear highly vulnerable.

LaGuardia could be at greater risk of flooding and future closures

It is not alone. With some 40% of the world's population living within 100km of coastlines, many airports have been built on low-lying areas near the sea. According to research by Newcastle University, 269 airports around the world are already at risk of flooding due to rising sea levels, and that total is expected to rise. Among those potentially most exposed are the likes of Amsterdam Schiphol, London City, Bahrain International and Newark Liberty International.

Many airports do already have some degree of flood protection, especially larger ones. But stopping the flood risks from rising over the next century means expensive investment in flood defences, raising runways, or even moving airports. It could cost up to $57bn (Â£42bn) according to the Newcastle report. Such costs are ultimately likely to be met by taxpayers or by travellers.

The aviation industry is often criticised for its own contribution to climate change emissions. It faces a forbidding challenge in attempting to meet a target of being carbon neutral by 2050, while also accommodating growth that is expected to see the global aircraft fleet double in size over the next two decades.

"We need to make sure we have enough space at air terminals for people whose flights are being disrupted and delayed, that we have enough hotel rooms and parking space and so on."

#344. Rejecting cheap fabrics, higher prices: Why shoppers are asking for better clothes

Clothing isn't made like it used to be, and more shoppers are taking notice.

The makeup of apparel imported into the U.S. has shifted in recent years. In 2010, about 60% of clothing was predominantly made from cotton. Now, that total is around 45%, while predominantly synthetic fabrics such as polyester and nylon comprise the majority of imports.

Apparel prices generally declined over the two decades leading up to the pandemic, according to the consumer price index. Since 2020, they have soared to record highs.

It's starting to change habits for consumers who are paying more for food, gas, rent and other staples. As shoppers shell out more for clothes, they're increasingly making sure the garments are worth the cost.

Consulting firm AlixPartners' 2026 Consumer Sentiment Index, which surveyed more than 9,000 shoppers, found that consumers are scrutinizing labels more closely and rejecting cheap materials sold at a premium.

"If it's not a natural material, a retailer brand who passes it off as it is, they're really becoming very low on consumers' minds," said Sonia Lapinsky, head of fashion at AlixPartners.

Fast fashion brands like Forever 21 and H&M exploded in popularity during the early 2000s and 2010s, bringing trendy clothing to shelves quickly at low prices. Then came ultra-fast fashion, with brands like Shein and Fashion Nova pumping out new garments at even quicker speeds and lower price points — made possible in part through the use of inexpensive, synthetic fabrics.

"We got to a point where the pendulum just swung too far in that direction. And we've actually begun to see a reversal," said U.S. Bancorp BTIG consumer analyst Janine Stichter. "Quality matters a lot more ... over the last three or four years than it has in the previous 10 or 20."

But quality, which shoppers often tie directly to material, can come at a higher cost.

The average unit price of apparel imported to the U.S. between January and August was $2.64 per square meter for garments that were predominantly synthetic fiber, like polyester and nylon, compared with $3.67 for items that were largely cotton, according to data from the U.S. Commerce Department's Office of Textiles and Apparel.

When comparing those 2026 figures to 2010, synthetic fiber apparel import costs have climbed around 11%, while cotton apparel costs have risen 21%.

"If you're appealing to a more value-sensitive customer, you either reduce the quality, or you negotiate with suppliers to try to share some of the cost or you maybe absorb the cost … and perhaps hope to crank a higher volume of product to make up for it," said Bernstein Research senior retail analyst Aneesha Sherman.

Though polyester is cheaper, experts point out that's not necessarily a bad thing. It can help clothing hold its shape and wick away sweat.

Sherman believes the athleisure boom is a primary factor in the shift toward polyester. She said that from 2015 to 2025, sports apparel was growing at a 4.9% rate in the U.S., versus 2.6% for all apparel and footwear, according to Euromonitor data.

"There probably is some quality trade down as well happening there," Sherman said. "But I think both these trends are conflated."

Nonetheless, backlash to synthetic materials is growing. Gen Z has even repurposed the word "polyester" as an insult to mean generally low-quality or fake.

Concerns have also grown about the environment and human health surrounding petroleum-based clothing, as more research indicates microplastics can leach from polyester clothing when washed.

It's led consumers to reexamine what materials are in their clothing.

Apparel prices have risen coming out of the pandemic, as demand outpaced supply and allowed companies to raise prices, said BTIG's Stichter. Tariffs and the Iran war followed, raising costs for businesses — and prices for consumers.

Petroleum is the basis of many synthetic fibers, and oil prices have skyrocketed since the start of the Iran war.

The same factors that helped drive up prices of clothing — Covid, tariffs and war — also affected clothing quality.

"Companies had to move around their supply chains in the post-Covid period," said Stichter. "They had to find ways to take costs out, and sometimes that meant using less expensive fabrics."

Those factors compounded a more-than-decade-long shift away from manufacturing in China.

Sheng Lu, a fashion and apparel studies professor at the University of Delaware, whose research focuses on the economics of the global textile industry, said China's established history with clothing production means it has higher-skilled workers, state-of-the-art-equipment and better material management compared with emerging manufacturing destinations such as Bangladesh, Sri Lanka and Cambodia.

"They mostly serve the value market and the mass market. And I think the reason why they're not ready to make higher-quality luxury and premium products really reflects the kind of skill sets you can expect when you source from these countries," said Lu.

AlixPartners' Lapinsky said these countries that manufacture at a lower cost sometimes have to still ship the raw materials from China, which can extend the timeline to make clothing. That means taking earlier bets on trends and then discounting if products don't sell well.

"If you have to mark down that product because the consumer doesn't want it as much, your net margin might actually be worse than if you were to just invest in quality in the first place," said BTIG's Stichter.

And as material costs rise for synthetic fibers, Stichter also said she believes it could further influence companies to use more natural fabrics moving forward.

Companies prioritizing quality are winning in this environment.

Ralph Lauren's stock is up around 85% over the past two years. Around 90% of its apparel is primarily made of cotton, leather, wool, viscose, cashmere or silk, according to its 2026 sustainability report. In its fiscal 2026, the company said it sold more products at full price and reduced markdowns.

Meanwhile, direct-to-consumer clothing brand Quince, which centers its brand identity around natural materials, reached a $10 billion valuation this year.

The brand jumped 16 spots to No. 3 for best resale value in online consignment retailer ThredUp's 2025 report. Organic cotton retailer Pact jumped 20 spots on that list to No. 8, illustrating the potential role materials are playing in brand recognition.

Alternatively, the 2026 AlixPartners report, which ranks sentiment for brands in different retail categories, found shoppers are viewing some fast fashion brands less favorably. Both Shein and Zara dropped three spots — to fifth and seventh place, respectively — in terms of sentiment in the young adult specialty category.

Shein passed higher tariff costs onto shoppers in 2025 and then blamed a sales slowdown on those higher prices. The ultra-fast fashion retailer saw its year-over-year share of U.S. apparel, accessories and footwear card spending decrease in the fourth quarter of 2025 and first two quarters of 2026, according to Consumer Edge.

Shein did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

Though Zara fell in AlixPartners' Consumer Sentiment Index, it has continued to grow sales and profits year over year. Even so, Lapinsky said worsening consumer views are something to watch, even if the company is doing well.

"What we found over the years with this consumer sentiment index is that sometimes it will be a bit of a leading indicator of what's to come," said AlixPartners' Lapinsky. "I'm not saying that this is going to be true for Zara, but I think that's one thing that we'll be watching as we look in the upcoming quarters and into 2027."

#339. Europe's car makers are in crisis. Will the threat of war rescue them?

Europe's car makers are in crisis. Will the threat of war rescue them?

It's green, bulging and intimidating. Ford's latest vehicle looks like a pick-up truck that has been through an Incredible Hulk transformation. Based on its popular Ranger series, this beefy number parked outside the front door of Ford's Dagenham plant can carry a load of two tonnes, tow up to four tonnes and is also shouldering the hopes of the 2,000-strong workforce that still makes engines here.

Inside the factory, the three-litre diesel engines that will power this camo-painted beast drift down a production line that has seen output cut from 90,000 engines a year to about half that over the last decade.

Ford is hoping that a pivot towards military vehicles can help fill the void created by what the company's UK boss calls the most challenging environment since the invention of the motor car. Once mighty, the European car industry is starting to look puny and is hoping surging defence budgets as Europe re-arms can help it flex its industrial muscles once more.

Ford is part of a bid for the British Army's Light Mobility Vehicle (LMV) programme

Ford is part of a joint venture with defence specialists General Dynamics and Ricardo bidding for a Ministry of Defence (MoD) contract to supply 9,000 vehicles over the next five to seven years to replace the Army's ageing Land Rover-based fleet.

Lisa Brankin, chair of Ford UK, says it is a chance to showcase their abilities to respond quickly to defence needs: "As a manufacturer you look at every opportunity that comes at you and this is a great opportunity that we would love to take advantage of."

This is not the first time Ford has busied itself with defence work.

Ahead of World War Two, Ford's factory in Dagenham, east London, was the largest car plant in Europe.

When war broke out, civilian car assembly stopped completely, and the factory was converted entirely to military production. Between 1939 and 1945, the Dagenham plant built 360,000 vehicles for the Allied war effort.

Ford workers in Manchester manufactured 34,000 Rolls-Royce-designed Merlin engines, which powered Spitfires and Hurricane fighter planes.

Eighty years later, the UK and European car industries are hoping that the engines of war can help defend against what one supplier told the BBC was a "terminal decline".

As Europe feels compelled to commit hundreds of billions to ramp up defence spending in the face of the menace from Russia and US reluctance to be Europe's protector, car makers that are under commercial attack from Chinese rivals have taken notice.

So can the rearmament of Europe save an auto industry and a supply chain facing crisis?

Ford is far from the only car company that sees defence as a growth industry - and one that can utilise the growing overcapacity at car plants across Europe.

French car maker Renault has signed a strategic agreement with defence giant Thales to produce military drones - targeting an output of up to 1,000 units per month. The French military and Directorate General for Armament want to tap into Renault's mass-production capabilities to bypass traditional, slower defence supply chains.

Meanwhile, Volkswagen has agreed to sell an under-used factory in Osnabruck, western Germany, which will become a military manufacturing hub in a joint venture with an Israeli-based defence investor.

Jaguar Land Rover (JLR), which makes the Land Rover, is also bidding for the same contract as Ford - as the Army retires its existing Land Rover-based fleet by 2030. JLR has also established a new dedicated business unit to support its global military ambitions.

It makes sense for under-utilised auto manufacturing capacity to switch to defence, says Mike Hawes of the Society for Motor Manufacturers and Traders (SMMT) trade association.

The UK car industry and its suppliers are heavily reliant on a few big manufacturers â Nissan in Sunderland, Toyota in Derbyshire, BMW in Oxfordshire and the biggest of them all by value of output, JLR at multiple sites in the Midlands and Merseyside.

And Hawes says the supply chain is very vulnerable.

"UK automotive output has been in decline over the last eight or nine years," he says. "We're probably half of what we were 10 years ago. Now, that's obviously going to hit the supply chain because they're not making the same number of parts. So they've got capacity. They may be quite dependent on one particular manufacturer as well."

So they will welcome "the opportunity to broaden their customer base and potentially move into defence", he says.

"JLR is the critical mass in the UK automotive manufacturing space," he says. "It is the glue that holds the whole of the sector together. Because remember, when you're making volumes for JLR, they're significantly higher than any other car maker in the UK.

Signatories to the letter said the redundancies were "the first visible crack" in a UK automotive supply chain supporting about 183,000 manufacturing jobs.

Across Europe, the industry is facing what Sigrid de Vries, director general of the ACEA auto industry manufacturers' association called "a perfect storm". Car makers are spending billions to shift to electric vehicles and insist that government sales targets for EVs are outpacing consumer demand.

And the EVs people are buying are increasingly from the biggest new threat to the industry â China.

In the early 2000s, Western firms saw China as a land of opportunity. The country's rapidly growing middle class had plenty of money and a seemingly insatiable appetite for cars, including the most profitable upmarket models. It was a lucrative business â the Chinese market once accounted for half of Volkswagen's profits.

However, it didn't last. China wanted a home-grown car industry and lavish state funding was ploughed into making the country a leader in high-tech businesses, including electric cars.

Today, the Chinese market is overloaded with brands, both foreign and domestic, and is characterised by cut-throat competition. Chinese car makers have focused their attention on foreign markets, and have used the transition to electric vehicles as a pathway to gaining market share. The likes of BYD, Chery and Geely are moving aggressively into Europe.

For European brands, all of this could not have happened at a worse time. The loss of the steady stream of profits from China, and the emergence of Chinese rivals on their home turf, has come after they invested heavily in producing electric vehicles (EVs). But EV sales have not increased as quickly as expected. Executives admit they have struggled to match the low production costs and development speed of the Chinese insurgents.

The result is that European manufacturers are now rushing to cut costs, while wondering what to do with expensive factories capable of producing millions more cars than they are able to sell.

Sigrid de Vries says car makers are well placed to help Europe rearm.

"Many of the capabilities that defence needs are needed for and also delivered by the automotive sector," she says. #329. My hometown shows that high streets have to change or die

My hometown shows that high streets have to change or die

The empty building that used to be the home of Marks & Spencer in Aberdeen is one of the first things you see when you arrive off the train and come into the city centre. The sign has gone, though the shape of the lettering can still be made out; the windows are blacked out and the doors boarded up.

This is my hometown. As a teenager, I spent hours on Union Street, its main thoroughfare, nicknamed the Granite Mile after the local silver-grey stone. It was the place to try on all the latest fashions from the likes of Clockhouse and Chelsea Girl.

The street used to be packed with shoppers from across the north-east of Scotland.

"I come to Union Street to take a bus somewhere else... there's nothing to shop for nowâ¦ it's horrendous," complains Marie Sim, a lifelong Aberdeen resident.

It's a similar story across the country: High Streets, once bursting with shops, hit first by the rise of out-of-town retail centres and then by online commerce.

But Aberdeen is in the thick of an ambitious turnaround plan. The port city has essentially declared war on its empty shop problem, taking an unusually hands-on approach to filling vacant units and bringing long-neglected upper floors of buildings into use.

The Marks & Spencer flagship store in Aberdeen closed in August, 80 years after it opened there

On top of that, the council is pouring tens of millions into trying to make the city centre a destination again. Like many other places, it's leaning into the "experience economy", with a new indoor food and drink market and a major redevelopment of the seafront.

High Streets are integral to how we feel about where we live. The sight of vacant shops and graffiti can quickly become a vicious circle, deterring shoppers, discouraging investment and leading to anti-social behaviour.

Politicians of all stripes have been trying for years to breathe new life into town centres - and Andy Burnham, who has made a focus on "place" part of his political identity, is the latest. He argues they should become "symbols of Britain's renaissance".

But can it be achieved? And if they're to survive, do town centres need a fundamental rethink?

One in eight properties on British High Streets are currently vacant, according to Green Street, the property and retail analytics company, a number that's stayed stubbornly high for years.

"If there was a silver bullet, it would have been fired years ago," says Jackie Sadek, a former government adviser on urban regeneration, who has spent nearly four decades working in town centre renewal.

One of the biggest obstacles is fractured ownership. Shops and buildings are often owned by dozens of different landlords, sometimes through offshore companies or complex structures, each with their own interests and priorities. That can slow decision-making and make large-scale regeneration difficult.

But even with empty units that are available, you need to find someone willing to take them on.

One in eight properties on British High Streets are currently vacant

The cost of running a High Street business has also risen; employment costs are higher, and there's the burden of business rates.

Firms need customers. Footfall on many High Streets has been in long-term decline as shopping and services move online, and this enormous structural shift has yet to play out fully.

Two-thirds of town centre experts think there is still 20% to 40% too much retail space, according to a survey by retail body Revo and real estate consultancy Lambert Smith Hampton.

In Aberdeen's boom years of North Sea oil, from the late 1970s through to the early 2010s, it didn't have to work hard to fill its hotels and restaurants and get the cash tills ringing because of the wealth the energy industry brought to the city.

I remember weekends with friends as a teenager, spending whatever cash we had in the fashion chains and shoe shops. Later came the pubs and bar-hopping, and trips to the art deco Capitol Theatre (now office space), where I saw Duran Duran.

But by the end of 2022, Union Street had hit rock bottom. Aberdonians had long deserted the High Street for a shopping centre, which opened in 2009 next to the railway station.

"They'd fallen out of love with their city," says Bob Keiller, head of Our Union Street coalition, a community-led group formed after an emergency summit to reverse the decline.

The first thing it did was listen to what residents wanted, receiving thousands of submissions. Restoring a bit of civic pride and filling empty units became the focus.

It's been a co-ordinated effort, involving letting agents, the council, landlords, businesses and a big team of volunteers, led by Keiller, a former FTSE 100 CEO, who isn't getting paid. He has a map on his office wall, detailing the status of every property, unit by unit, with colour-coded Post-it notes.

They audited the street, tracking down absent landlords, and logging what was empty and why. Volunteers offered to give the empty properties a clean and a lick of paint, to make them more attractive to let. They also spruced up the streets, picking litter, weeding, and painting bins. A small team still goes out once a fortnight.

Bob Keiller heads a community-led group set up to help revive Aberdeen's Union Street

There's a website where all the available properties are collated; crucially, all of the financial incentives are also listed. These can include council fit-out grants (where a new business gets money to decorate premises), and a two-year discount from business rates.

In 2023, 57 of the 198 units on Union Street were empty. As of today, 33 have been filled, with a further 14 in the process of being let.

But it can feel like a game of whack-a-mole because since the initiative started, another 20 units have become vacant. "The key thing is, we're filling quicker than emptying," Keiller insists.

Keiller's operations director, David McKeith, describes it as putting an arm round the shoulder of those they can help and being a "wasp in the ear" of people who just say no.

At one end of the office, a small, young team produces social media content, linking visitors and residents to a new Our Aberdeen app, showing what the city can offer.

For Keiller, the "small wins" - like tackling graffiti - are needed to help change public perception.

"We've realised the psychology is not to do it once but be prepared to do it two or three times before people that do these things eventually say, 'there's no point in putting it there because somebody's going to paint over it.'"

Attention is also turning to the often-forgotten upper floors of buildings along the once-iconic Union Street. In a separate initiative run by the business group Aberdeen Inspired, experts are getting inside those vacant high-up spaces to identify the barriers to bringing them back into use.

Aberdeen sought the help of a High Street troubleshooter, Iain Nicholson, who's worked with dozens of towns and cities through his Vacant Shops Academy.

Out of more than 1,000 empty units he helped audit nationally, on average more than half didn't have a letting agent actively marketing the property. He says some councils don't even know who the landlords are.

Empty shops, he says, won't all be filled by the market alone. Towns and cities need to encourage a broader range of occupiers, from arts and culture to leisure, health and well-being.

He says what's striking about Aberdeen is its joined-up approach, and the fact people "rolled up their sleeves" and overcame differences. "That's not something you can yet say about everywhere."

Every town and city faces its own mix of challenges.

Nicholson also helped Crewe in Cheshire. As a smaller place than Aberdeen, it is focusing on arts, culture, and community, with a new women's centre opened this month.

And English councils are being encouraged to take a more interventionist role.

They can now force an empty commercial unit that's been vacant for at least a year to be offered to tenants through a High Street Rental Auction, even if the landlord doesn't want to let it.

A noodle bar (left) is one of the new businesses in the area. Parts of the city centre have been undergoing construction work (right)

It's early days but the mere prospect of losing control of a property is delivering some results. Bassetlaw Council in Nottinghamshire, an early adopter of the scheme, says more than 30 shops across the district have been let or are currently under offer as a result of the policy.

But filling empty units is only part of the challenge. The bigger question is what town and city centres are actually for.

Changing habits mean we won't need so many shops. Instead, it's argued, town centres will need more people living and working in them, with new homes and office space alongside services that can't be done online. With an ageing population, for example, bringing NHS health centres into the heart of a town could be a big opportunity.

In Aberdeen, the building that used to house BHS - a department store shuttered in 2016 - is being converted into an entrance plaza for the new food and drink market.

The physical appearance of Union Street is changing too. Like many Victorian shopping thoroughfares, it was built with a wide road (probably to give space to horses and carriages). Now parts of the pavements are being widened to make it a more pleasant place to spend time, though many Aberdonians are weary of the disruption, and of new traffic restrictions.

Money is a big barrier. Aberdeen's new indoor food and drink market is costing Â£40m, funded by the council and the UK Levelling Up Fund. In other places, regeneration schemes can struggle because the cost of redevelopment outweighs the likely financial return, making support from the public sector essential. And the economics are becoming more challenging.

"Higher interest rates, rising costs of construction and other issues are impacting the viability and delivery of new housing and big redevelopment projects," says Steve Norris, a director at Lambert Smith Hampton. He says that big projects usually require cash from both the public and private sectors.

He wants to see "more creative ways of funding".

Part of Burnham's plan is to give local leaders more freedom to invest in town centres and drive regeneration, building on the approach he has championed in Greater Manchester. But bigger projects take years.

Volunteers have played a role in the redevelopment of Aberdeen's Union Street

Making it easier and cheaper to open and run a High Street business is one of the more immediate solutions being called for, with business rates a particular concern.

Burnham has promised further rates reform. All eyes are on this month's Budget to see how far he will go. All eyes are on this month's Budget to see how far he will go in England. Business rates are devolved in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The chief executive of Next, Simon Wolfson, argues that the bigger challenge is not business rates, but planning reform.

"The most important thing that the government can do for high streets is let them develop," says Wolfson, who's also a Conservative peer.

Back in Aberdeen, there are some signs of traction.

Lauren Reid and her mother Rose moved their fashion boutique to Union Street in 2024. A council grant helped with the refurbishment after they decided to make the leap.

Can they make the sums add up? "We're getting there," she says.

She feels the same about the future of her street. "I always say Rome wasn't built in a day."

Lauren Reid and her mother Rose moved their fashion boutique to Union Street with the help of a council grant

Right now the area feels like a construction site - but another gaping hole will soon be filled. Robert Keane is a local property developer who in 2023 bought the Trinity shopping centre, once home to Debenhams. The space is now set to be re-occupied by a national leisure brand. A notoriously tiring stairway leading to the Trinity from the train station is also being replaced by an escalator.

"We saw a real good opportunity, because it was dying," he says.

He reckons he can make a go of it, cutting the cost of parking and overheads, resetting rents and being more nimble than previous far-flung owners.

It was a bold move given Lambert Smith Hampton suggests that more than one in 10 UK shopping centres is ripe for demolition.

But he's betting the dynamics of Aberdeen are finally changing.

"I think by 2027 it should look and feel like a different city."

That is certainly the hope. Yet walking along Union Street today, past the boarded-up shopfronts and roadworks, it can still feel like a city caught between its past and whatever comes next.

#315. Ex-GM CEO on Mary Barra succession planning: 'It's going to be tough'

Former General Motors CEO and Chairman Dan Akerson doesn't mince words when asked about the challenges the automaker faces in finding a successor for GM CEO Mary Barra: "It's going to be tough."

He should know. The 78-year-old businessman and U.S. Navy veteran helped with his own succession planning and recommended to GM's board in 2013 that Barra replace him as CEO. She has held the top job since Jan. 15, 2014.

Akerson said Barra will be hard to replace, citing her success and prominence as the first female CEO of any major automaker and the ongoing challenges facing the automotive industry, such as artificial intelligence, vehicle digitalization, and the rise of China and autonomous vehicles.

"It's going to be very, very dynamic," Akerson told CNBC during a phone interview. "It's going to be a big change."

Akerson declined to discuss potential internal candidates such as GM Chief Product Officer Sterling Anderson, CFO Paul Jacobson or President Mark Reuss, but he said the eventual successor needs to be dynamic and, if from the outside, "adaptable."

His comments to CNBC follow a Wall Street Journal report on Tuesday that GM is working with an executive search firm to identify possible outside candidates to succeed Barra, in addition to internal prospects. GM declined to comment on the report or Barra's succession.

Akerson, who came to GM as an outsider from The Carlyle Group in 2010, said selecting an outside candidate might be risky if there are already executives within the organization who have similar leadership and technical expertise.

"Is there anybody in the organization that has that background that you know better day to day over a period of time? You may be taking a risk by bringing in an outsider," he said.

The prospect of an outsider leading GM would be unusual in the auto industry, where major automakers have generally promoted internal CEO candidates, apart from periods of exceptional disruption such as the Great Recession.

Stellantis, BMW, Toyota Motor, Nissan Motor and Hyundai Motor have all appointed internal executives to their CEO positions in recent years.

Barra has said several times that her successor and her longevity in the role would be determined by the board.

A notable influence is likely Hewlett Packard veteran Patricia Russo, GM's longest-serving board member who has been on the board since 2009 and leads its CEO succession planning as independent lead director.

GM's CEO succession planning is done "at least annually," according to the company's yearly proxy filing. That process includes Barra providing the board "with recommendations for, and evaluations of, potential CEO successors and discusses developmental plans for these successors," according to the filing.

That is followed by board members engaging "with CEO candidates and senior management talent at Board and committee meetings and other forums to enable directors to personally assess candidates."

Another advantage, Heinrich says, is that "since we cut the cells into smaller areas, we can fill out special sizes more easily. You don't have to adhere to the regular pattern size."

Learoyd believes this would lower costs and give "the whole construction industry much more confidence in using the product".

Since pizza locations hit a peak of 83,035 nationwide in August 2019, more than 9,600 pizzerias had closed their doors as of July 2026, according to data from Datassential.

In 2025, sales at U.S. pizza restaurants fell 0.3%, according to Technomic data. Sales at independent pizzerias slipped 0.4%. Chains, which account for about two-thirds of the domestic pizza category sales, saw revenue shrink 0.3%.

Papa Johns reported that North American same-store sales declined 2.5% in 2025. Pizza Hut's U.S. same-store sales fell 5%. Among the three pizza chains that were publicly traded last year, only Domino's posted annual same-store sales growth at its U.S. restaurants, with an increase of 3%.

It isn't just diners who have lost their appetite for pizza. Since March 2020, Papa Johns stock has lost about two-thirds of its value. Even shares of Domino's, which dominates the category, have seen gains of only about 2% in that time. For comparison, the S&P 500 has climbed about 150% over the same period.

And Yum Brands sold Pizza Hut this year, offloading the laggard of its portfolio to LongRange Capital and Yum China after about two decades of stagnating sales.

"Pizza Hut has been a detractor for YUM ... Going forward, YUM won't be tripped up by pizza category dynamics (discounting, etc.), translating to stronger growth and higher concentration in higher growth industry segments and markets," Wells Fargo analyst Zachary Fadem wrote in a research note in September.

Pizza chains now have significantly more competition for diners' dollars than they did in 2020. Third-party delivery platforms, which grew in popularity during the pandemic, offer a wide variety of cuisines beyond the traditional pizza and Chinese. As a result, Domino's and Papa John's have to compete with other fast-food chains, like McDonald's and Chick-fil-A, for DoorDash and Uber Eats orders.

As pizza has grown more expensive, a pie seems less appealing to budget-conscious diners. The average large cheese pizza is now almost $17, according to the 2026 Pizza Power Report from PMQ Pizza, a trade publication.

Plus, frozen pizza is another convenient option, with a lower price tag. Retail sales of single- and multi-serve frozen pizza rose 2.9% in the 52 weeks ended Nov. 7 compared with the same period in 2022, according to NielsenIQ data.

"When the American consumer is focused on the least expensive eating occasion, the items that are in my pantry, my refrigerator and my freezer win every single time," said David Portalatin, a food and food service industry advisor for Circana.

Convenience stores like Casey's General Stores have also won over customers with pizza at a low price point.

"We believe that [Casey's] has an advantage over [quick-service restaurants] and dollar stores, which have fewer options to navigate the persistent inflationary environment, while [Casey's] can offset food pressures with margins," Bank of America Securities analyst Sara Senatore wrote in a research note published in June.

She added that geographic overlap of locations suggests that Casey's is stealing share from Pizza Hut.

The biggest pizza chains have also been slow to adapt to shifting consumer attitudes toward nutrition. For example, of the four biggest chains, Papa Johns is the only one to even test a pizza crust with added protein as more diners prioritize it in their diets. And the test was only in one Atlanta restaurant — for a single day.

Pizza's nutritional content more broadly is another drag for the category. Its carbohydrate and fat content, from its dough and cheese, respectively, can make it less appealing for consumers on GLP-1 drugs, which have skyrocketed in use over the last several years.

Spending on pizza quick-service restaurants has fallen 22.2% per GLP-1 household after six to eight months using the medications, according to a 2026 survey published by PwC.

To be sure, pizza's recent struggles do not mean an existential crisis for the category. Total pizza servings are down only about 1% this year, Circana's Portalatin said.

And chains are trying to reverse that downturn in servings. In August, Domino's launched the Domino, a customizable Detroit-style personal pizza to attract solo diners. In November, Papa Johns unveiled a new turnaround plan focusing on improving food consistency and restaurant operations. And Pizza Hut is running a holiday-themed meal deal.

"Pizza's not going anywhere," Portalatin said. "The American consumer loves pizza."
