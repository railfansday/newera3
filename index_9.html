#471. A transcription firm used by police, prosecutors, and tech giants exposed customer data online

A transcription company that caters to law enforcement, government departments, and Silicon Valley tech giants has resolved a security lapse after leaving a large amount of internal data, including customer transcriptions and their source materials, exposed to the web.

The data security lapse at San Francisco-based Tigerfish exposed transcriptions of police interviews, body-cam footage, and customers’ raw audio and video footage. The exposed data also included invoices of the company’s customers, which include police departments and prosecutors’ offices.

According to Anurag Sen, a good faith security researcher who found the data, the files were located on an Amazon-hosted (S3) cloud server that was openly listed and accessible to anyone via an easy-to-guess web address — no passwords needed.

The security lapse underscores how simple flaws or misconfigurations can result in sensitive information getting exposed online, especially as AI makes it easier to uncover weaknesses that can put companies and their data at risk.

Sen discovered the exposed data online in October and alerted the company to the issue, but did not hear back. Sen later provided details to TechCrunch in an effort to flag the incident with the company for resolution.

Sen, 30, is no stranger to TechCrunch. For the past decade, he has searched for sensitive datasets exposed online with the goal of notifying the companies and organizations that their information is open to the internet. Like many security researchers, much of his efforts are driven by a duty to secure the internet, and he describes his work as an “unpaid air-traffic controller.”

From his island home in Indonesia, Sen has uncovered sensitive U.S. military emails spilling from a compartmentalized Department of Defense cloud server; databases exposing reams of customer records; the inner workings of a country’s national license plate surveillance system; and public systems that inadvertently sent out streams of SMS codes used by people around the world to log into their online accounts.

Given Sen’s propensity for finding often highly confidential information online, we verified the source of the leak and the data, and contacted Tigerfish.

Soon after making contact, Tigerfish secured its exposed S3 cloud bucket.

“We’ve blocked all public access to the bucket you identified, including directory listings and individual files,” Tigerfish president, Adam Goldberg, said in an email. “We’ve also verified that all of our other S3 buckets are blocked to public access.”

“We’re continuing to review and strengthen our storage security more broadly,” added Goldberg.

The company said that Sen’s email disclosing the data leak was caught in its spam filter.

It’s unclear if the company has notified its customers. Tigerfish did not respond to a follow-up email.

Discoveries of exposed datasets have consumed a large part of Sen’s early cybersecurity career so far. He scours the web daily using automated scanners and specialist search engines to identify databases, cloud storage, or other sensitive information that likely shouldn’t be left unprotected online.

The modern web runs on absolute trust, he said, and operates under the notion that the world’s data is kept secure. But he described how his findings reveal the reality of how “fragile” the internet is behind the scenes.

Despite the recent focus on how AI is accelerating people’s ability to discover vulnerabilities, Sen said that many of the data leaks and exposures that he finds come down to basic human errors.

“An engineer spinning up a test cloud database on a Friday, disabling authentication to test an API, and forgetting to shut the door before heading into the weekend,” was just one example, he recounted.

Sen’s findings over the years highlight the ongoing problems caused by such security mistakes, just as the new wave of vibe-coding and AI-generated development is creating new security problems due to insecure and unchecked code deployed to the web.

Similar to Amazon’s recent efforts to make it difficult to make S3 buckets public without going through clear multistep warnings, newer vibe-coding platforms like Lovable and Supabase have also made changes to their platforms to prevent user mistakes.

“Everyone often fears shadowy hackers,” Sen said. But he argues that the real crisis is leaving sensitive systems and databases “wide open to the public internet without a password.”

#462. Chips can survive the space environment — can their financing?

The push to build data centers in space faces plenty of obstacles, from tricky engineering to a dearth of rockets that could take chips to orbit. But what about paying for the chips?

The data center ecosystem currently depends on debt financing, with cloud providers borrowing to build out data centers and then paying back the loans with a share of the revenue.

Those deals typically use the chips as collateral — if the data centers default on their loans, the lenders can take possession of the chips and resell them. That backstop makes it cheaper for companies to finance their chips. Even older chips retain value — according to Compute Credit Index Research, some H100s are selling for about half of their original cost basis from 2023, while some H200 configurations are selling for the same price they did when introduced in 2024.

But you’re not going to repossess chips installed in a satellite orbiting 300 miles above Earth at 17,500 miles per hour. The Financial Times is reporting that lenders behind SpaceX’s $40 billion chip financing deal asked that none of the chips purchased be used for the company’s orbital data center plans. It’s not clear if they succeeded in getting SpaceX to agree with the provision.

That may not matter to the company in the near term — SpaceX’s ability to deploy compute at terrestrial ground stations has proven lucrative, and on the company’s most recent earnings call, CEO Elon Musk detailed how he was dispatching engineers from the company’s rocket team to accelerate that build-out by taking on challenges like casting turbine blades for generators at faster than existing suppliers.

However, open questions remain about how easy it will be to find land and power to support the data center build-out. Many potential projects face delays and local opposition, while markets wait to see the state of compute demand and the ability of its buyers to turn it into revenue.

Musk, meanwhile, began saying at the beginning of 2026 that he believes space will be the “cheapest” place to operate data centers in two to three years. That’s unlikely until SpaceX can fly its Starship rocket in a fully reusable configuration. In its most recent flight test, the company failed to recover the booster stage but did succeed in landing the orbital stage of the rocket back at its launch facility in Texas. It’s not clear, though, when the second stage will fly again or how expensive it will be to prepare it for a second trip to space.

Even with Starship, however, SpaceX — as well as startups like StarCloud, Cowboy Space Corporation, Sophia Space, Orbital, and others — will need to buy chips. If the already daunting economics of space data centers weren’t challenging enough, it looks like they’ll be spending more to buy them than their terrestrial counterparts.

That might be an advantage for Google, which is focused on deploying its own TPU chips in space through Project Suncatcher. SpaceX and Tesla are working together to build their own chip foundry, Terafab, in the U.S., but that project has yet to break ground and will require years and billions of dollars before it starts producing semiconductors at scale.

#459. Super-hot rock geothermal could end data center energy woes

Geothermal startups are in a race to see who can tap the deepest, hottest rocks. The winners could power the next phase of the grid, data centers and all.

The broader markets got their first taste of enhanced geothermal earlier this year when Fervo Energy went public. But this week was all about super-hot rock geothermal.

Geothermal has become a darling among investors, tech companies, and even the Trump administration. The basic technology has been around for decades, but advances in drilling techniques borrowed from the oil and gas industry has supercharged it. The U.S. has plenty of potential, too, with up to 57 terawatts of generating capacity, more than enough to power the next wave of AI data centers.

An emerging class of geothermal energy, known as super-hot rock, could help the industry unlock much of that potential.

The secret to super-hot rock geothermal is what happens to water at extreme depths. As it gets closer to the superheated core of the Earth, both temperature and pressure rise significantly. When they get high enough, ordinary water turns into a supercritical fluid — a sort of gaseous-liquid state that can carry tremendous amounts of energy. A single super-hot rock well could generate 15 megawatts of electricity or more.

Super-hot rock geothermal is inching closer to commercial reality. Quaise Energy said on Tuesday that it had landed Google as a customer. The hyperscaler said it would pay more than $500 million to secure 50 megawatts of power from a Quaise project in central Oregon, which will tap into searing heat three miles deep.

Google is paying a premium for the amount of power Quaise will deliver, but that’s in line with the company’s strategy of getting in early with potential suppliers. For instance, it was an investor in and an early customer of Fervo.

Meanwhile, on Monday, Khosla-backed Mazama Energy announced it had figured out how to keep its drill heads cool as they tap into super-hot rocks. And last week, XGS Energy was given a grant by the Department of Energy to develop and refine materials, well designs, and models to improve reliability at depths where super-hot rock exists.

XGS is dipping its toes in the super-hot waters, while Mazama and Quaise have made it their core focus, though they’re taking very different approaches.

Quaise’s technology sounds like science fiction: Starting at about 2.5 miles deep, the company will swap out its conventional drill bit for one powered by a gyrotron, bombarding rock with millimeter-wave radiation and turning it into ash. The hope is that the technology can make drilling under super-hot conditions cost-effective, something that’s challenging with traditional metal drill bits, which wear out faster at those temperatures.

Mazama, on the other hand, thinks that traditional drill bits will work just fine if they can be kept cool.

The startup’s newly announced technology, Carbon Chill, uses carbon dioxide in place of water-based drilling “mud” to keep the bit cool. At those depths and temperatures, the carbon dioxide turns supercritical, allowing it to hold significant amounts of energy. Just above the drill bit, the CO2 flows through a valve that looks similar to a rocket nozzle. As the gas flows through the valve and expands on the other end, the expansion rapidly cools it, allowing it to absorb significant amounts of heat. The CO2 also removes drill cuttings on its trip to the surface, where it’s filtered and reused.

In both cases, Quaise and Mazama are betting that their technology could leapfrog today’s state-of-the-art commercial geothermal power plants. By going deeper and hotter, they’re hoping to get substantially more power out of each well. Given that drilling accounts for about a quarter to a third of a geothermal power plant’s total costs, drilling fewer wells could make a project more competitive.

Investors have been betting that the technologies will soon prove themselves. Quaise has raised more than $270 million from investors, including Prelude Ventures, Collaborative Fund, and Engine Ventures, with Vinod Khosla stepping in as an angel investor, according to PitchBook. Mazama has raised $171 million from investors, including Khosla Ventures, Doerr Capital, and Bill Gates.

As with any new technology, there’s some risk that the new techniques won’t pay off. But early results suggest they will — Mazama drilled its second well 80% faster than its first, and its CO2-cooling tech cut drilling time another 20%, co-founder and CEO Sriram Vasantharajan told TechCrunch.

That, plus Google’s vote of confidence in Quaise, suggests the race to super-hot rock geothermal is, well, heating up.

#455. AI claimed a solution to the 167-year-old Riemann hypothesis — and a human did, too

It’s a big week for Levent Alpöge: The mathematician saw his employer, Anthropic, become the first publicly traded frontier AI lab. He also appears to have solved one of the most important problems in mathematics.

“The precise timing is horrible,” he wrote in an essay announcing his findings and describing how he messaged Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei last Sunday night to ask how to announce that he had found a Lean-compilable solution to the Riemann hypothesis, which dates back to 1859 and is among the Millennium Prize math problems.

The hypothesis conjectures that there is a hidden structure underlying the appearance of prime numbers. If the claimed result holds true, we’re entering sublime-mysteries-of-the-universe territory.

Maybe the best way to understand how exciting this is to the mathematics community is to watch the experts puzzling through the result in a chat on Zulip. Mathematicians like Chris Birkbeck, a number theorist at the University of East Anglia, are posting thoughts like this …

The cohomology bound is proved by what looks like an automorphy-lifting argument. It genuinely uses the existence of Galois representations attached to regular algebraic, polarized cuspidal automorphic representations of GL_n over CM fields, along with base change, Jacquet–Shalika, Clozel’s purity, Bernstein–Zelevinsky, the main theorem of CM, Deligne–Ribet and more.

For my part, I tapped out of mathematics at college calculus, so I can’t help decode exactly what that means.

But that comment was followed by 20 readers reacting 🤯.

It will be a while before the mathematics community feels like it has a handle on the proof, but most appear confident that the formalized Lean result will hold. Lean is an an open-source coding language used to express mathematical ideas as code, allowing proofs to be formally verified. Several participants in the Zulip chat, including MIT mathematician Andrew Sutherland, say they have successfully confirmed that Alpöge’s solution can be compiled successfully.

Alpöge appeared in his essay to feel a bit guilty about releasing the proof without a truly human-intelligible explanation, but his obsession with the problem since childhood made him feel he “could not live” if he waited to release the finding.

Alpöge described a “stream-of-consciousness jaunt through the experience of finding all the pieces of the argument through incredible confusion with Claude” with more than a hint of AI euphoria, describing how the model “flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line.”

However, for all that action to mean anything, we’ll have to wait for humans to be able to understand the result and explain it to each other, something Alpöge has committed to pursue.

Alpöge and a collaborator, NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster, also released another important proof related to the Navier-Stokes equations, problems that helped kick off the race between frontier labs and mathematicians to solve outstanding problems this summer.

Meanwhile, Ravi Vakil, a Stanford professor and the president of the American Mathematics Association, posted a proof of the General Riemann hypothesis written by Stanford postdoc Alessandro Masullo, noting that “it is all human, with no AI. The hope was to bring Alessandro to Stanford for a year to polish the writing and then announce it after running it by enough people. Now it seems best to put out the current draft.”

Without being expressed in Lean, it is too early to say whether the proof checks out, much less if anyone understands it.

In a brief introduction to his proof, which he said is the result of 12 years of work, Masullo wrote that “if any message at all this work is meant to send to the mathematician who feels discouraged by the pace and tone of the current climate, it is this: we are still here. We are very much alive. We are not going anywhere.”

#446. Experts warn: Nearly everyone in AI is susceptible to a honey-trap scheme right now

Even as the fall-out for Nvidia over Jensen Huang’s alleged honey-trap scandal is still unfolding, Silicon Valley has yet to reckon with a new reality: many more people among them are at risk.

“Nobody briefs founders on counterintelligence,” Aaron Brown, a former senior CIA officer and current founder of the software company Lumbra, told TechCrunch.

That’s because historically honey traps have been associated with politicians, the military, and national intelligence — the people “behind clearances and a workforce trained to expect an approach,” from spies and other attackers.

But now many of the most valuable secrets “sit inside companies building chips, models, and infrastructure,” Brown says, adding that a spy “wants what the CEO can touch. The roadmap, the export decisions, the customer list, who gets the technology. The CEO is the shortest path to all of it.”

Huang has been accused of falling for just such a honey trap by having an affair with Alice Huang. She’s a former executive of a Singapore neocloud who’s been accused of being a Chinese spy and of smuggling billions of dollars’ worth of restricted Nvidia chips into China.

After the Huang allegations broke, jokes flooded the internet about which tech CEO will be next, but it’s not just CEOs and founders who are at risk. The WSJ reported that OpenAI started warning its employees about outsiders trying to cozy up to them in search of company secrets. Anyone with access to sensitive information — from board members to executive assistants — can be the target of a honey trap.

And typically, the attackers aren’t lovers. Friendship is actually the oldest honey-trap scheme around, Brown said.

Honey traps gain access to their targets as investors, customers, recruiters, and consultancies. Their requests look legitimate on paper and don’t need their targets to fall in love. “They need you to feel comfortable talking,” Brown said. “They last longer because nobody’s looking.”

What’s interesting, too, is the irony. So many tech founders are moving to automate and digitize so much of human life and yet remain vulnerable to the oldest bug around, a person who preys on the human need to be understood by others.

“Why fight through a firewall when you can get close to the person who can open doors, influence decisions, or make requests nobody wants to question?” Perry Carpenter, a criminologist and chief deception strategist at KnowBe4, told TechCrunch.

Once targets realize they have been compromised, the threat can escalate: attackers now have a means of blackmail.

Another irony: AI has made honey-trapping much easier and cheaper. Planning a scheme used to take months or years of research, crafting a persona, knowing the target, and executing a plan. These days, it’s easy to use AI to research a target, generate fake social profiles, fake backgrounds, even fake voice calls.

“AI can help sustain an entire fictional backstory,” Carpenter said. “The technology helps set the stage, but the deception ultimately plays out through human relationships.”

The AI industry “became a national security industry faster than its culture caught up,” Brown said.

“Part of being honey-trapped is not knowing,” he added. “It doesn’t feel like manipulation. It feels genuine, even lucky.”

Carpenter said a good first step is looking for unusual patterns. Note, for instance, “coincidental encounters that happen a little too often,” he said. There are also obvious signs, like persistent curiosity about work and colleagues, or requesting secrecy about meeting up, and pressing for introductions or information.

Bigger red flags include if someone’s requests are getting bigger, pushing the boundaries of what you usually would be uncomfortable doing. “A simple gut check,” Brown said. “Would this person still be around if you lost your job tomorrow?”

It also helps to try and understand when emotions might be influencing a decision. Carpenter suggests putting up a clear boundary between one’s personal and professional life. Within companies, it also helps to “eliminate executive exceptionalism,” he said, the idea that the rules don’t apply to people in power at the company.

Brown added that company culture plays another role, too: Are employees comfortable with raising concerns, challenging unusual requests, and escalating possible threats? “One of the most dangerous security cultures is one where nobody feels comfortable questioning the CEO,” Carpenter said.

“Founders need to take risks, and they need to explore business opportunities,” Brown said. “Paranoia doesn’t help. Recognizing you’re a target does.”

#435. Is selling an AI version of your face to this microdrama startup the future of Hollywood?

For the most part, the content offered by the microdrama startup Shortical is indistinguishable from the rest of its industry. The company produces soapy, melodramatic storylines — almost always risqué romances — that, in bite-sized two-minute episodes, compel viewers to stay glued to their phones so as not to miss the latest twist in a tawdry, cliff-hanger-happy saga.

The primary way that Shortical — which was founded last January — makes money is through subscriptions. Typically, the first 7-10 episodes of a micro-drama are free. After that, if the user wants to finish the series, they have to pay a small monthly fee which unlocks access to all of the site’s content.

Over the past year, the company has churned out a gush of popular live-action romances like “Crossing the Line with My Neighbor,” “Accidentally Married My Boss,” and “Spoiled by My Billionaire Sugar Daddy.”

Lately, however, the company’s homepage is covered in AI-generated content. The reason isn’t exactly hard to figure out: the production overhead for AI content is substantially less than live-action. The profit potential, meanwhile, is substantially higher, if you can draw an audience.

Its latest microdrama premiered in Los Angeles last week. The series, called “Inevitable,” stars Aki Avni, an Israeli film and television star who has been visible in American TV shows for years (he had a stint on Fox’s 9/11-era thriller “24” years ago) but is now trying something largely unheard of in modern Hollywood: licensing his face to Shortical for the sake of its AI content.

The project, which is wholly AI, used Avni’s face to create a digital avatar of him. In “Inevitable,” he plays himself – in a very meta plot in which he — the actor Aki Avni — licenses his face to a film studio, after which thriller-like antics ensue.

This is Shortical’s first foray into such a licensing deal, but it likely won’t be their last.

In a phone interview, Avni told TechCrunch that he found the project both nerveracking and exciting. “Look, it’s a new era for artists,” the actor said. “AI is a new kind of art. It’s a new format. It’s always great to be the pioneer in something.”

Guy Shimoni, the CEO of Shortical, emphasized the creative potential of the new tool. “One of the things that AI does is it enables smaller teams to be able to produce these movie-scale productions,” Shimoni said. “When we were doing mobile-first live action, we were limited by budget, but now we can serve them with epic fantasies. We have kingdoms and dragons. We have superheroes. We have werewolves.”

But AI-generated romances involving werewolves — who, exactly, is watching this kind of thing?

Apparently, a lot of people. While China boasts the biggest microdrama audience globally, Shimoni says that most of his platform’s viewers are actually in the U.S. The industry’s viewer demographic skews heavily female. Many viewers may watch them to wind down at night, getting minor dopamine hits as they drift off to sleep. Many of the AI-generated programs that already exist on the company’s website appear to have millions of views.

Now, Shortical is betting on the creation of a new generation of creators — those who, like Avni, are willing to surrender their likenesses for compensation. “LICENSE YOUR FACE” Shortical’s website advertises, explaining that interested parties can submit their social media handles to the company, which will then decide whether to offer them “a contract setting out the terms” of using their persona in the company’s AI-generated content.

A company rep told TechCrunch that this “offering is still in its early days and hasn’t yet resulted in any signed contracts.” They added: “The series with Aki is the first time Shortical has used a real person’s likeness, and it was done through a direct agreement with him rather than through the platform. Shortical hopes the series will draw more attention to face licensing and plans to promote the offering more actively following this launch.”

Much of this trend has already taken hold in China where the microdrama industry originated, where AI-generated content is big, and where face-licensing is already a trend. Although the trend may not be a particularly profitable one for creators. A recent report in Rest of World found that Microdrama firms were paying people “between US$15 to US$700 to license their facial likenesses.”

How much money is Shortical offering for its face-licensing deals? The company isn’t saying. As for if Shortical will offer royalty-based compensation for creators — like the residual system in television, “It’s a case by case [basis],” Shimoni answers vaguely.

A Shortical representative further explained to TechCrunch that the company didn’t want to “share a specific figure” for Avni’s particular contract. However, the person said, he came “on as a partner in the project, and his compensation was in line with industry standards for a comparable non-AI acting role.”

For other actors, however, the deals may be quite different. Plus, many creatives are understandably unsettled by signs of automation creeping into their industry. Until recently, microdramas seemed like a safe, entry-level gateway into the entertainment industry, where young actors could gain experience on sets and hone their craft. Now, companies are downsizing and producing content that doesn’t even require real actors.

Jennifer Cooper, a longtime fan of the industry and founder of the consultancy firm Vertical Drama Love, notes that there’s a lot of distrust of AI — both from audiences and within the microdrama industry itself.

“Everyone’s been impacted by AI,” she said. “A lot of us have either lost work to AI or can see that it’s devastated this industry — [taking microdramas] from something that was a rare thing in the world, that was in growth, and providing work, particularly for young people…[and now] you know, it’s been decimated.”

Questions of consent with AI likenesses have also dogged the industry (some creators claim they have had their likenesses stolen), which is something that Shortical is obviously trying to address with its new face-licensing endeavor.

Yet these licensing deals could also help to gig-ify the industry, creating a scenario in which creators pay a distributor for the chance to platform their content (or their face), then receive payouts based on the audience engagement they’re allowed to generate. Shortical recently partnered with a company called Payouts.com, which describes itself as a “payout infrastructure platform powering creator and gig economy disbursements at scale.” The plan is to create a system by which creatives can seamlessly interact with Shortical’s platform and get paid.

Cooper believes the push towards AI in the industry is driven by broader financial trends. Most of the microdrama business is led by startups, and VCs love to hear a growth game plan involving automation. “For anyone to get investment at the moment in microdramas, they’ve got to mention the word AI,” she says. “It’s like AI equals gold in everyone’s eyes.”

Despite all the investment in this space, Cooper seems skeptical that there is actually a huge audience for this kind of content. “Some of it does get views. A lot of it doesn’t,” she said, of the AI content. “I think it’s being pushed on an audience more than there is an audience demand or wish for it.”

#372. AI is changing software engineering — and some developers miss the struggle

When Gemma Rodgers began her computer science degree in 2002, software engineering was seen as a future-proof and lucrative career. It appealed to people who enjoyed getting into the weeds of code, tracking down bugs and building programs from scratch.

It's an industry that has always moved fast, Rodgers, a software developer based in the U.K., told CNBC. But the arrival of artificial intelligence has accelerated the pace, with AI tools now part of the daily work of every software engineer she knows. In a 2026 Stack Overflow survey, 83% of software developer respondents said they use at least one AI tool.

As AI coding tools help engineers write, debug and test code faster than ever, some are mourning the job they used to have. Rodgers has always loved the sense of accomplishment that comes from piecing together different parts of code to find a solution, particularly when she's spent days, or even weeks, trying to fix a stubborn bug.

"The sense of joy and fun that I used to experience by having a challenge and then having to work that out, it's been removed somewhat," she said. "Now you can sometimes do it in a few prompts, and it's great — but it's a breadcrumb of joy, not a full meal of joy."

Rodgers is not alone in feeling nostalgic about the not-so-old days. Software engineers have taken to social media to describe missing their old way of working, when they could get into a flow state with code for hours at a time.

Still, AI tools can be useful and have lowered the barrier to entry significantly. "It's becoming an industry where a lot of people have been able to thrive who wouldn't necessarily have been able to thrive 10 years ago, five years ago, even three years ago," Rodgers said.

It's also important that the core skills engineers have traditionally learned on the job are not lost, she said. AI tools and internet services can go down unexpectedly, and engineers still need to know how to investigate problems and work through solutions themselves.

"The sorts of skills that we were taught years ago are kind of being lost because [engineers are] having to just churn work out," Rodgers added.

The job itself is changing, too. James Broadhead, chief technology officer of AI decision-making company Taktile, told CNBC that engineers who might previously have spent three weeks building a prototype can now generate 10 in an afternoon. The hardest part of that, he said, is "having the judgment to decide which of those are any good."

"The [AI] agents are spitting out so much code that [engineers] haven't read it all anymore," Broadhead said. "It used to be you'd build up a sense of comfort and awareness of how the whole code base was structured, but now the whole thing changes all the time."

Joshua Wöhle, CEO of London-based AI training company Mindstone, told CNBC he sees a growing divide between workers who know how to use AI effectively and those who don't.

The industry used to talk about "10X engineers," he said, who were dramatically more productive than their peers. Now, AI is widening that gap even further to produce "100X engineers."

Wöhle believes companies will increasingly have to decide whether to help employees catch up with AI or prioritize those who can get the most out of it. But he also understands why some engineers might struggle to adapt.

The people who once excelled at the job were often those who loved writing and working through code, he said, and those are not necessarily the current top performers.

"That's the grief that is very, very real," Wöhle said. "The thing you love doing is no longer the thing the company should be paying for, to a degree, because it's no longer adding the value to the company that it used to."

But not every engineer sees the move away from hands-on coding as a bad thing. Chris Sees, a software engineer of more than 20 years and CEO of London-based virtual office company Hoxton Mix, told CNBC that AI gives developers more time to tackle interesting problems. He views AI as a capable junior engineer that still needs supervision.

"The important thing is knowing what to delegate and when human judgement is needed," Sees said. "We'd be silly to think AI gets everything right, and I know I get a lot of satisfaction out of proving and spotting where it has gone wrong."

Even so, Sees worries about what happens to the next generation of engineers if AI takes over the routine coding and debugging through which they traditionally learned. Those tasks are "where you learn lessons and build scar tissue," he said, and companies need to make sure AI becomes part of the learning process, rather than a shortcut.

For Rodgers, what matters is having control over how she uses the technology. As a self-employed developer, she can use AI to automate the parts of a project she finds tedious, while keeping the more interesting problem-solving for herself.

She believes there's something important about working through a difficult problem rather than immediately outsourcing it to technology. Friction and discomfort, she said, are vital parts of the human experience.

"As humans, we need to feel accomplished in something and part of accomplishment requires challenge," she said. "Unless we are able to work that through in so many different aspects of life, then you never get the full force of the good feeling on the other side."

Mundane but essential equipment, used by everyday businesses, has become a favorite target of cyber saboteurs in recent weeks.

Guy Segal, CEO of cybersecurity firm Sygnia, told CNBC that his company handled 285% more incidents in October than a year earlier, with demand forcing it to turn away some businesses seeking emergency assistance.

"It's going to get worse before it's going to get better," Segal said.

The recent cyberattacks targeted refrigeration systems across hundreds of European supermarkets, remotely altering temperature settings and forcing retailers to discard spoiled food.

France has since accused a cyber unit with links to Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps of the attacks, an allegation Tehran denies.

Fredrik Blix, a cybersecurity researcher at Stockholm University, told CNBC that thousands of industrial control systems remain accessible over the public internet, sometimes despite manufacturers' warnings.

Segal said that basic cyber protection measures such as multi-factor authentication and basic network segmentation are more important than ever.

He said risks to major civic infrastructure like the electrical grid were front of mind and so "usually pretty well protected."

"There are always those civil supply chain components that are way, way less protected. Could be supermarkets, could be gas stations, could be… the screens on the Tube," Segal said, referring to London's underground rail system.

The supermarket attacks showed "this is the new normal, and definitely it's a wake-up call for every company out there," Segal added.

Segal said that inexpensive AI tools mean technical expertise and resources are no longer needed to launch attacks. He described one recent incident in which clients suffered breaches within six hours of a vulnerability becoming known, as perpetrators used AI to act faster — a process that might have taken weeks a year earlier.

Segal, whose firm helps companies respond to breaches and strengthen their defenses, warned that businesses without prearranged incident-response support could struggle to secure emergency assistance, as overwhelmed cybersecurity firms prioritize existing clients.

In one case, he said, Sygnia had to turn down a company because no response teams were available. Segal added that the company in question had already been turned down by ten other providers.

Jacob Silverman, CEO of risk advisory firm Kroll, told CNBC earlier this month that the company was operating at full capacity in its incident-response business amid what he described as "the most rapid uptick in threat activity that we've ever seen."

Segal argued that retailers should treat cybersecurity risks with the same seriousness as fire safety and food hygiene, including when outsourcing maintenance.

When Ahold Delhaize stores in Belgium and Luxembourg were attacked on Nov. 2, it exposed how different independent store operators had responded to the warnings following the earlier attacks in France.

Retailers had been warned about the vulnerabilities following earlier attacks in France, and those that implemented the recommended protections were spared, CEO Frans Muller told investors in an earnings call earlier this month, but smaller operators with fewer technical resources had struggled to make the necessary changes.

Coop, whose stores are owned by regional consumer cooperatives or operated by franchises, told CNBC that public attention on the recent attacks was the main driver of security improvements. It said the vulnerability at the affected stores had since been addressed.

#328. An early breakdown in the polar vortex is threatening already depleted gas stocks

An unprecedented early breakdown of the stratospheric polar vortex and a record-breaking El Niño event has put energy traders on alert this winter, with analysts warning the weather and climate phenomena could have an outsized impact on Europe.

Meteorologists are currently observing a record early breakdown of the polar vortex, a large-scale circulation of strong winds that typically keeps extremely cold air over the North Pole.

Changes in the strength and shape of the polar vortex can influence weather patterns much lower down in the atmosphere, potentially bringing in colder weather to parts of Europe and North America.

This year, however, the collapse of the stratospheric polar vortex, or SPV, is taking place at the same time as an unusually powerful El Niño, a climate event that can boost global temperatures and disrupt rainfall.

The collision of these two events raises the risk of a prolonged cold spell that could lift heating demand and force European countries to draw down already-low reserves faster. That would leave the continent more reliant on imported liquefied natural gas (LNG) at a time when supplies from the Middle East are disrupted.

To be sure, Europe's natural gas storage levels started the winter at their lowest level in more than a decade.

The International Energy Agency warned earlier this month that low European storage levels and limited storage options in several LNG-importing countries in Asia and South America could intensify the competition for cargoes and put upward pressure on prices during periods of high demand.

Simon Lee, a lecturer in atmospheric science at the U.K.'s University of St Andrews, said the combination of a sudden stratospheric warming (SSW) with an El Niño event is not necessarily unusual, but meteorologists had been expecting a polar vortex breakdown in February — not as early as November.

The early SPV disruption is an "extremely rare" event that could influence winter weather in northwest Europe and the eastern U.S., Lee said, increasing the chance of colder conditions although likely not to the extent that a polar vortex event caused parts of Niagara Falls to partially freeze in January 2014.

"Geopolitics and weather are the two key volatility drives for energy and now they seem to be happening at the same time — and that's always the biggest risk for markets," Florence Schmit, senior energy strategist at Rabobank, told CNBC.

For months, meteorologists expected an El Niño event would result in a milder winter in Europe and relatively muted heating demand, Schmit said, which was broadly seen as welcome news given ongoing concerns over the continent's tight regional supplies for gas and crude oil.

The prospect of a colder-than-expected winter due to an early breakdown of the polar vortex could be "incredibly difficult" for Europe to navigate, Schmit warned. She noted that the region's gas storages have seen "very, very strong withdrawals" over the last two weeks, "which is quite unusual for this time of year."

"If you continue to see the same storage withdrawals over the next couple of months, that means energy prices will spike again — and they won't spike because of geopolitics. This time it will be the weather driving that spike and that volatility," Schmit said.

European gas prices have soared to levels not seen since the 2022 energy crisis in recent weeks, notching 103.9 euros ($113.99) per megawatt hour in late October. European benchmark Dutch TTF futures for December delivery stood at around 87.28 euros on Friday afternoon.

"We have never seen an early breakdown of the polar vortex and a super el Nino in the same winter so we have no historical precedent on how this will impact global gas demand," David Lewis, principal research analyst of gas and liquified natural gas at Wood Mackenzie, told CNBC by email.

El Niño is commonly associated with milder winters, while a SSW event typically leads to colder temperatures and higher snowfall, Lewis said, noting this could materialize in December and January within Europe.

"So far, the start of the European winter heating season has been quite cold but more importantly has seen low wind speeds across Northern Europe," Lewis said.

"The drawdown in stocks in the first half of November has been amongst the highest on record. With inventories starting the winter at their lowest level on record, any further deterioration in temperatures or wind speeds will have an outsized impact on European balances and lead to higher prices across Europe," he added.

#309. Retirees face a looming deadline for required withdrawals — missing it could trigger a tax penalty

Every year, millions of Americans must take required withdrawals from retirement accounts by Dec. 31.

Amid the busy holiday season, it's easy to miss the year-end deadline for required minimum distributions — a mistake that can trigger a hefty IRS penalty, experts said.

"One of the most common RMD mistakes is waiting until the last minute," said certified financial planner Alex Canellopoulos, director of wealth at Vista Capital Partners in Portland, Oregon.

Most retirees must start RMDs from pretax accounts at age 73. The withdrawal is based on the previous year's account balances on Dec. 31, your age and an IRS "life expectancy factor."

The first annual RMD deadline is April 1 of the year after you turn 73, and future withdrawals are due by Dec. 31. If you wait until April 1 for the first one, you'll need two RMDs that year.

RMDs also apply to certain inherited IRAs if the original IRA owner reached RMD age before their death.

In 2023, more than 14.8 million taxpayers age 70 or older had a traditional individual retirement account, according to the latest IRS data. The average year-end market value of the accounts was about $330,000.

Deferring RMDs until the last minute raises the likelihood of mistakes, such as overlooking certain retirement accounts or making withdrawal calculation errors, Canellopoulos said.

Some 7% of Vanguard investors at RMD age missed their yearly withdrawal in 2025, up slightly from 6.7% in 2024, according to a Vanguard analysis of about 400,000 RMD-age retirees with IRA balances published in December 2025.

Among those investors, the average missed RMD was $10,500 in 2025, Vanguard told CNBC.

Investors who don't take RMDs by the annual deadline may owe an excise tax to the IRS, worth up to 25% of the amount they should have withdrawn. For example, the excise tax could be up to $2,625 for a $10,500 missed RMD.

If investors miss a required withdrawal, they could reduce the penalty to 10% if they "timely corrected" the RMD within two years and file Form 5329, according to the IRS.

In some cases, the IRS could waive the penalty entirely due to "reasonable error" if investors take "reasonable steps" to fix the shortfall, according to the agency. Investors can file Form 5329 with a letter of explanation to request the waiver.

"If you don't ask, you're not going to get it," said certified public accountant Tim Steffen, director of advanced planning at financial services firm Baird. "I've never heard of anybody being denied the waiver."

Investors who give to charity may be skipping a specific donation strategy that can reduce or eliminate their annual RMDs, according to Lawrence Pon, a CFP and CPA based in Redwood City, California.

They can use a so-called qualified charitable distribution, or QCD, Pon said.

A QCD has a dual tax benefit: It doesn't count as taxable income and may also satisfy at least part of an investor's yearly RMD, Pon said.

The move requires a direct transfer from an investor's pretax IRA to an eligible nonprofit organization. They can donate up to $111,000 in 2026. While QCD eligibility begins at age 70½, transfers won't start counting toward RMDs until an investor reaches their RMD age.

Generally, taxpayers only get a tax break for large charitable donations if they itemize deductions on their tax returns. But QCDs still provide a tax benefit for charitable gifts when investors use the standard deduction.

QCDs "provide a real benefit if you are not itemizing [tax deductions]," said Pon.

While there's no tax deduction for QCDs, the transfer won't increase a taxpayer's adjusted gross income, or AGI. Raising AGI can cause other tax consequences, such as higher premiums for Medicare Part B and Part D.
