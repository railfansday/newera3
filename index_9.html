#453. Sequoia shutting down its Scouts program marks the end of an era

Buy One Founder Summit Ticket, Get a Second for Free. Register now.

Founder Summit begins Nov. 4, 9:00 a.m. ET. Get your pass savings & deals before it starts. REGISTER HERE.

Sequoia this week announced a broad overhaul in a memo posted to X, a move that includes closing its London office and shutting down its scout program. Observers warn that it would be a mistake to read too much into the London closure, since the British capital has a thriving AI scene. On the other hand, the decision to end scouts says more about where venture investing is headed.

That’s partly because 17 years ago, Sequoia pioneered the scouts model, in which select founders and operators write checks on the firm’s behalf. Amid increased competition for deal flow, many VC firms followed suit. But a consensus now seems to be emerging that the model has reached its limits in its current form.

Not everyone was sold on the model in the first place. “They pioneered the scout program but [to be honest] it never made sense to me!” VC Sheel Mohnot wrote on X this week. “The scouts themselves don’t really care that much about the investments, you can still miss (like they did with Uber), not sure what it gets you. I suspect other firms will shutter theirs too,” he added.

To be clear, Sequoia didn’t make a case against scouts in the memo. Instead, it framed its decision as part of a broader need for more focus. But its goal of “putting more energy behind fewer people and things” is likely to resonate with others who’ve questioned the ‘spray and pray’ approach to scouting.

Rival firm Andreessen Horowitz (a16z), for instance, rolled out a global scout network aimed at spotting opportunities in the U.S. and also in smaller European hubs. But a16z partner Gabriel Vasquez told TechCrunch earlier this year that the firm had revamped its program to double down “on the best people.”

Under this new approach, Vasquez explained, scouts get more resources but are also expected to be more active, such as by co-hosting dinners with the firm. “They get more budget, but we have reduced the number,” he said.

This also seems to indicate that it is VC firms that have been driving an overhaul of the model. For founders, being a scout is attractive: it brings them closer to firms they may want checks from someday, or even a partner role with, and being seen as someone who can write a check can raise their standing among other founders.

Some scouts will undoubtedly mourn the end of Sequoia’s program. Among them is Station F boss Roxanne Varza, who wrote on LinkedIn that she was “sad” to hear that the program was getting cut off, and “hope[d] that other funds [w]on’t follow suit.”

Varza, a former Sequoia scout from 2020 to 2025, told TechCrunch that she had no information beyond what has been shared publicly, but observed that becoming a scout had often been a stepping stone for aspiring investment managers.

“I cannot name all of the incredible scouts that have gone on to join or build funds, but there are quite a few. And the scout programs may have helped them get there — allowed them to build an investment track record, allowed them to build relationships with various funds directly, and more,” she wrote to TechCrunch.

According to Varza, the program’s impact went beyond the individual level, instead helping the entire ecosystem become more mature. “I believe they have helped to foster the investment culture and early-stage investment landscape that we now see in Europe, for example,” Varza said.

While others may agree with Varza about the role these programs have played, they may still have run their course for two reasons. First, seasoned operators may get more financial upside as angels. When it’s their own money, they get to keep the returns, too. Second, leading VC firms are now big and well-known enough that the best founders tend to come to them, so they worry less about missing deals, except at the very earliest stages or in niches and geographies where they have less reach.

Still, the pull of those early and niche deals is why scouts programs are much more likely to get revamped than to disappear entirely. “While Sequoia may be cutting the program, venture partner-style deals are likely to continue,” said Varza in her email to TechCrunch. “Scouts was just a way to scale the format. I don’t imagine Sequoia would end all forms of venture partners, but they are full of surprises.”

In October, Amazon announced its refreshed Kindle lineup, its first significant design overhaul in years. The highlights included a new edge-to-edge design that eliminated traditional raised bezels, thinner and lighter Paperwhite and Colorsoft models with better displays, and page-turning accessories like a Bluetooth remote and a magnetic cover with page-turn buttons.

However, given the impact of the global memory-chip shortage driven by the AI boom, the new Kindles also debuted with pricing that had increased by $40 to $50 across the lineup. That makes it more difficult for the average consumer to consider buying one of the more premium devices.

For that reason, the Kindle I most wanted to get my hands on was the 6-inch entry-level device, which starts at $169.99 for the ad-free version. With the price hikes, this is the Kindle that will remain the most affordable to new e-reader buyers.

The question I weighed was whether or not the basic Kindle is even worth buying at this price when there are arguably better Kindles on the market. The answer, after a couple of weeks of testing, is yes — if you can live without the bells and whistles and prefer a smaller, lighter device.

On the 6-inch Kindle, there’s no warm light to reduce eye strain, no color display, no auto-adjusting brightness, no option to use the page-turn cover, and it’s not waterproof. Obviously, because of its size, the screen is smaller, as is the battery. Battery life is 6 weeks compared with the Paperwhite’s 12 weeks or Colorsoft’s 8 weeks.

Still, even this basic Kindle has seen improvements of its own, mostly from a physical design perspective.

The plastic 2026 Kindle is 11% lighter and 14% thinner than the 2024 model, weighing 140g versus 158g and measuring 6.85mm thick versus 8mm. While this seems like a small change, the 18-gram difference is noticeable and particularly welcome for a device you’re likely to carry in your purse or bag. Plus, it makes it easier to hold for hours on end when you’re really binging your way through a book.

(The aluminum version, meanwhile, is slightly heavier at 162g, or 4 grams more than the 2024 Kindle, though it still benefits from the slimmer design.)

And despite being the “starter” Kindle, the 6-inch basic device still received the new flush-front screen update. This makes it more comfortable to hold in your hand, especially for those long reading sessions. Amazon explained that you don’t have to worry about no longer having a bezel to rest your thumb upon, either, as the new Kindles have been updated to detect a dead zone in the capacitive touch that will readjust to account for the fact that your finger is there.

In practice, that doesn’t mean you can just press your thumb down anywhere on the Kindle’s screen as you hold it — doing so would register this as an attempt to highlight or look up a word. Instead, it means you can rest your thumb or fingers on the outer edge of the Kindle’s screen (the colored border) without triggering an unintentional tap, even though some of your finger will be touching part of the Kindle’s capacitive screen.

In terms of the software, the new basic Kindle is a bit snappier than its 2024 predecessor. This is most noticeable when opening books, which Amazon claims to be 30% faster.

If you’re coming from the 2019 Kindle Basic, the 2026 version is an even bigger upgrade, as you gain USB-C, more storage (from 4GB to 8GB to now 16GB or 32GB), the new flush screen, and the screen resolution jumps from 167 ppi to 300 ppi. It’s faster, thinner, and lighter, too, and the battery lasts weeks longer.

Another perk about the basic Kindle beyond its small size (this thing fits in your jeans’ back pocket!), is that you can now pay a little more for an upgraded version with an aluminum backing instead of plastic and more storage. The new $189.99 aluminum option comes in Seagrass and Alpine Sky hues, and ships with 32GB of storage. Meanwhile, the $169.99 plastic model offers 16GB of storage.

However, even if you go for the less expensive option, you can at least now pick a fun color other than black (aka Graphite), as Amazon has introduced a cheery purple option called Ube. (I almost wish I’d gone for this one, just for the whimsy of it, even though I prefer the feel of the aluminum.)

I should point out that you can use the Kindle Click, the new Bluetooth remote for page turning, with the basic Kindle, but I’m not sure why you’d need to, if not for some accessibility-related reason. The 6-inch Kindle was already the smallest, and now it’s lighter and easier to hold, too. (But perhaps I just don’t get the appeal of the remote? I’ve barely used the remote with my Kobo either!)

The big thing to consider here is whether you want to spend this much on a Kindle that lacks a warm light. While this is something of a personal preference, for some readers, no warm light is a dealbreaker. The warm light shifts the color tone of the Kindle’s display from a cooler bluish-white to a softer amber glow, which is meant to help reduce eye strain and improve reading conditions in low light or at night.

Whether you need this feature depends on where you like to read and how long your sessions tend to be. If you read nightly in bed with little light, you might prefer paying extra for a $199.99 16GB Kindle Paperwhite. (That version only comes in Graphite. The 32GB Paperwhite can come in Seaglass, too, but is $249.99).

If you often read by the pool or on vacation, you also might want to upgrade to a waterproof Kindle.

But if you can live without the extra features and want the best purse- or pocket-sized device, the 6-inch Kindle is still a viable option.

#438. Parents want safer phones for kids. These companies are answering the call.

Founder Summit begins Nov. 4, 9:00 a.m. ET. Get your pass savings & deals before it starts. REGISTER HERE.

As parents become increasingly concerned about the potential dangers of unrestricted smartphone access for children, a growing number of companies are developing devices specifically designed for kids.

Today’s kid-friendly phones typically feature touchscreens, cameras, and communication capabilities similar to regular smartphones. However, the software on these devices is heavily modified to remove or restrict access to web browsers, social media, app stores, and other distractions.

Parents can usually manage these devices through a companion app, allowing them to approve contacts, monitor locations, set screen time limits, select available apps, and, in some cases, receive alerts regarding cyberbullying, explicit content, or online predators.

In addition to these modified devices, some companies offer a more minimalist approach, creating devices that focus solely on calling and texting.

There are also home phones designed specifically for children, like Tin Can and the recently launched Pinwheel Home.

Here’s a look at the major players shaping the kid-phone market.

The Bark Phone is probably one of the most popular kid-specific smartphones on the market.

Built on Samsung Galaxy hardware and powered by Bark’s monitoring software, it only allows calls and texts with approved contacts. Bark continuously scans texts, emails, photos, and supported apps for signs of cyberbullying, grooming, suicidal ideation, sexual content, and other potential concerns, then sends alerts to parents.

A big standout feature is that parents can gradually unlock web browsing, apps, and other features as their child matures. The phone also includes GPS tracking and screen-time management.

Current pricing for the standard model is $240, plus a required wireless plan beginning at $29/month. Higher-tier plans add internet access and unlimited texting.

Gabb is a well-known name in the kid-friendly phone category. Unlike Bark, however, Gabb focuses less on monitoring content and more on eliminating online risks altogether.

Its phones don’t include social media, a browser, or an app store. Instead, children get calling, texting, and a carefully curated selection of preloaded apps, including a camera, calendar, and calculator. The company also touts its “worry-free” music streaming library with millions of songs.

Additionally, parents can view location and manage certain device settings, while optional services such as “Gabb Guard” help block spam calls and unwanted texts.

Current phone pricing starts around $159.99, with cellular service starting around $24.99 per month.

With Pinwheel, parents approve every app, control contacts, schedule screen time, create custom routines, and monitor location history.

One standout feature is the ability to create different “modes” throughout the day. For example, limiting the phone to calling and navigation during school hours before unlocking additional features after homework.

Pinwheel phones start around $119, while the Caregiver Portal subscription begins at $14.99 per month without cell service. (Cellular service can be purchased separately through participating carriers.)

The company recently launched two landline phones for $68 and $79. It also offers a smartwatch at $160.

Launched in 2022, Teracube offers a tailored version of Android known as Thrive OS. This enables parents to approve every app download, filter web browsing, set app-specific and overall screen-time limits, monitor location, and create customized routines for school or bedtime. However, because it has more traditional smartphone features, it’s often recommended for older children.

Thrive currently sells for $99, with plans starting at $35 per month.

Unlike the other products in this roundup, Ooma MyPhone isn’t a mobile phone at all. This kid-focused home phone is designed as a modern replacement for the traditional household landline.

The service allows calls only between approved contacts through its Trusted Circle feature, offers scheduled Quiet Hours, provides address-based 911 service, sends emergency alerts when 911 is dialed, and lets parents review call logs through an online portal. There are no apps, internet browsing, texting, or social media.

The phone costs $99.99, while service starts at $7.99 per month.

For $100, Tin Can resembles a landline phone with a distinctive tin can design, which is the inspiration behind its name.

This device does not require a traditional phone jack, but connects via Wi-Fi. Only approved contacts can make calls, effectively preventing spam or unauthorized communications. Parents can easily manage contacts through a user-friendly companion app.

Tin Can offers a free plan for calling other Tin Can users, as well as a $9.99 per month plan that allows calls to all approved contacts.

A newer entrant in the space, Waffle recently opened preorders for its screen-free devices: Waffle Home ($119), a landline phone, and Waffle Go ($169), which looks like a portable walkie-talkie or kids’ toy with three simple buttons that can only make voice calls and listen to voice notes.

Parents can approve contacts via the companion app, as well as see location and control when devices can call.

TextNow, the Canadian telecommunications company, recently launched its new mobile phone for kids. It’s called Yello! and while it looks like a normal touchscreen smartphone, it doesn’t have an app store, social media, or unrestricted web access.

Parents can see their kids’ location and battery life, as well as control things like the games they play and who they have in their contacts.

Yello! costs $149.99 upfront, with a $19.99 per month plan.

Ann Mettler, president of Catalyse Europe, a Brussels-based clean-energy think tank, and a former vice president of Breakthrough Energy, said developing the technology should be a strategic imperative for Europe.

"Europe is a resource-poor geography and we are very dependent on China. The world is in competition for technological supremacy — this is a zero-sum game," Mettler told CNBC, speaking in a personal capacity.

"Fusion is a silver bullet because the promise is abundant, clean, limitless energy."

Fusion energy seeks to replicate the way the sun produces power, using super-heated plasma to fuse two light atomic nuclei into one heavier one, releasing vast amounts of energy in the process.

There are two main approaches: one uses high-powered lasers, while the other uses powerful magnets to confine the plasma. Proxima is developing a type of magnetic device called a stellarator, which is designed to hold the plasma in place for long periods.

The agreement with Kyoto Fusioneering will expand existing work on next-generation gyrotrons — devices that generate high-power microwaves used to heat the plasma.

Proxima's agreement with 141-year-old Fujikura will explore plans for industrial-scale manufacturing in Germany of high-temperature superconducting, or HTS, tape, a specialized metallic tape that is superconducting at relatively high temperatures and can carry electrical current with virtually no resistive losses.

The tape is wound into coils to form powerful magnets that confine and control the heated plasma inside the stellarator.

Francesco Sciortino, CEO and co-founder of Proxima Fusion, told CNBC that Fujikura, which employs over 50,000 people worldwide, was a leader in manufacturing high-temperature superconductors, having built its reputation in optical fibers. "They think this tape has the potential to be the next optical fibre," he said.

He said Proxima, which is studying plans to build a manufacturing facility in Lower Saxony, hoped to make around 5,000 kilometers, or 3,100 miles, of tape annually by 2030.

The 140 million euro ($160 million) facility would be financed partly by the proceeds of a $468 million funding round in July, which valued Proxima, whose backers include Alphabet-owned Google, at $2.7 billion.

Proxima expects to need around 20,000 kilometers (12,400 miles) of the tape for its "Alpha" fusion demonstrator and a further 40,000 kilometers (24,900 miles) for its planned "Stellaris" fusion power station.

Sciortino said that establishing production of the tape in Germany was crucial as it would help Europe create the expertise and supply chains needed to turn its fusion research leadership into industrial capability.

This is where Europe has historically struggled, according to Catalyse Europe's Mettler.

"Every geography wants to turn leadership in science into industrial leadership and this is where Europe has traditionally been not so good — turning that scientific leadership into euros," she said.

While Europe had industrial giants to be proud of, disruptors tended to be more successful in frontier technologies, according to Mettler.

"If Europe is first to develop this technology, it will give us a huge advantage, but key to that will be building the supply chain," she said.

Sciortino said he remains confident Proxima will be the first company to turn on a pilot plant — but stressed the key to profitability would be making that plant as simple as possible to operate.

"You can have complexity in the design, manufacturing and operation of the plant," he said. "We can take care of the complexity in the design and manufacturing, but our view is that there needs to be simplicity in the operation. We are aiming for net energy gain during the early 2030s."

But Proxima has some well-heeled competitors. They include U.S.-based Commonwealth Fusion Systems, which is also backed by Google, and Helion Energy, as well as Canada's General Fusion. China has a number of state-backed players, most notably China Fusion Energy Co., while Tokyo-based Helical Fusion leads Japan's push.

"The supply chain in fusion energy needs to be built years before — people often think of frontier technologies as a switch that can be thrown," Mettler said. "That is not the case here."

#358. Inside the card-grading empire shaping the price of Pokémon cards

Collectors may have more grading options than ever, but many still turn to Professional Sports Authenticator, or PSA — even as the industry leader works through a backlog of millions of cards.

Demand for grading — an act of verifying a card’s authenticity and condition — has grown as collectors increasingly treat trading and vintage sports cards as alternative investments.

PSA reported a backlog of 7.2 million items on Oct. 20, down from 12.4 million in July. The company suspended cheaper submission services in June to ease the backlog before reopening its Value service on Oct. 29.

Even so, some sellers would rather wait for a PSA label than send their cards to a competitor, dealers told CNBC.

“It really all comes down to secondary market values,” said Geoff Wilson, founder and CEO of CardsHQ, which operates one of the world's largest sports card shops.

PSA-graded cards typically command higher prices than equivalent grades from competitors, Wilson said, giving collectors and investors an incentive to choose PSA in hopes of a stronger return.

The financial difference can be substantial.

A 1999 Base Set Charizard graded PSA 9 sold for about $3,440 on eBay on Sept. 29, compared with roughly $1,890 for a CGC 9 copy sold the same day, according to CNBC's review of PriceCharting records.

That pricing gap reinforces PSA's lead and gives its decisions about fees and submission services considerable influence over the resale market.

PSA had not responded to CNBC’s detailed questions at the time of publication. In published updates, the company has attributed service restrictions and pricing changes to demand exceeding capacity, and outlined investments intended to reduce the backlog.

Grading companies verify a card’s authenticity, assess its condition and seal it in a protective plastic case, known as a slab. PSA uses a 10-point scale, with its highest grade, Gem Mint 10, reserved for cards it describes as "virtually perfect."

Like a credit rating in bond markets, a card grade gives buyers and sellers a common benchmark for assessing condition.

That can reduce friction in transactions by giving both sides a reference point based on past sales of the same card at the same grade.

That capability has helped open the trading-card market to more global participation and made cross-border transactions easier, according to an unnamed spokesperson for Singapore-based submission dealer Oxley Grading.

The spokesperson attributed PSA’s position as the industry’s “gold standard” to its early start, brand recognition and extensive history of completed sales.

“They've just built up such a strong branding and a lot of sales records,” the spokesperson said.

They estimated that roughly 90% of the graded cards offered to Oxley Grading for purchase carry PSA’s label.

The dominance creates a self-reinforcing cycle: buyers seek out PSA-graded cards, giving sellers an incentive to use the company. The abundance of PSA slabs in shops and online marketplaces further reinforces the brand's visibility and demand.

For a seller choosing a grader, that established resale demand can outweigh a competitor’s lower fees or faster turnaround times.

PSA sits within a larger collectibles business.

Its parent company, Collectors, also owns rival grading brands Beckett and SGC, pricing-data platform Card Ladder, the PSA Vault and financial services. The vault links storage with selling through external marketplaces, including eBay.

Together, those businesses span much of a card’s commercial journey — grading, pricing, storage, sales and even financing against collectibles.

Collectors' three card-grading brands accounted for roughly 71% of August grading volume among the major firms tracked by industry analyst GemRate. PSA alone represented about 65%, while the next-largest player, CGC, represented about 28%.

Collectors' consolidation has also drawn legal scrutiny.

A proposed class action filed in April challenges Collectors’ acquisitions of SGC and Beckett, alleging that the deals unlawfully reduced competition in card grading. The allegations have not been proven in court.

Collectors has defended the Beckett acquisition as a way to strengthen a trusted brand while preserving collectors’ ability to choose the grading service that best suits their items.

“We believe that strong, well-supported brands help grow access to the hobby,” Collectors CEO Nat Turner said in the company's 2025 acquisition announcement.

For sellers who prioritize PSA's resale premium, changes to its fees and submission services can alter the economics of grading a card.

PSA raised fees across several existing tiers in February, including Value Bulk from $21.99 to $24.99 and Value from $27.99 to $32.99. It also lengthened estimated turnaround times for some services, saying the changes would help balance submission demand with grading capacity.

The suspension of cheaper tiers in June further narrowed affordable options, while higher costs have made customers more selective about which cards they submit, according to Oxley Grading's spokesperson.

Meanwhile, Wilson said the restrictions have particularly affected collectors and dealers who buy cards ungraded, send them to PSA and then resell them at a higher price.

For cards worth a few hundred dollars or less, that strategy has become harder to justify, he said.

“You're seeing more raw cards bought and sold at card shows,” Wilson added, using the industry term for ungraded cards.

PSA has acknowledged that customers are waiting too long.

In an Oct. 28 update, the company said submissions in April and May were 2.5 times the volume received in the same months a year earlier. It also announced shorter turnaround estimates for Standard and Priority services, while saying new Value submissions would be processed behind existing orders.

“We have added an incredible amount of capacity, but we still have work to do,” Ryan Hoge, president of PSA and grading at Collectors, said in the statement.

For Wilson, those investments in capacity are part of PSA's appeal.

“They've made the collector experience better by doing that,” he said.

The leading graders' dominance may leave collectors with little room to push back.

Collectors' preference for PSA's resale value can make switching graders less attractive, the Oxley Grading's spokesperson said, adding, "PSA's lead comes from resale demand and liquidity, and other labels are still building that."

Overseas customers can face fewer practical grading options as many major graders are based in the U.S., Amber Chuah, operations manager of trading-card platform TCG Marketplace, told CNBC.

The complaints she hears most often involve long waits and disappointment when cards receive lower grades than customers expected.

Challenging a grade can require another round of shipping and waiting, discouraging some collectors from disputing the result, she added.

"If you're really keen on selling, that's a lot of time."

PSA did not respond to CNBC's questions about the sources' claims.

PSA’s terms allow customers to request a paid review of disputed grades, with customers responsible for grading and applicable shipping fees. The company also offers defect notes for eligible submissions to provide more detail on how a grade was determined.

CardsHQ's Wilson said some variation between assessments is an inherent challenge in a process that involves human judgment.

Wilson pushes back against the suggestion that PSA’s position leaves customers without alternatives.

“First of all, there are alternatives,” he said, pointing to CGC, Beckett and smaller grading firms such as TAG.

CGC’s grading volume in August was more than double its level a year earlier, according to GemRate, showing that rival graders are attracting business. Beckett also recorded strong growth, although it is owned by Collectors.

Oxley Grading's spokesperson said there may be room for lower-cost services aimed at collectors who care more about a card’s artwork, sentimental value or preservation than maximizing its resale value.

Competing for sellers focused on the strongest financial return remains more difficult.

Building comparable resale acceptance would require substantial investment, with no guarantee that buyers would ultimately value a newcomer's label as highly as PSA's, the spokesperson said.

For Wilson, broader acceptance in the resale market is key to shifting that balance.

“If the market as a whole starts to respect the value of the other grading companies' cards more, then collectors will increasingly submit to those companies,” he said.

#290. Why a wave of bomb threats is proving so difficult for Europe's aviation authorities to contain

A spate of bomb threats against passenger flights has forced diversions, emergency landings and military escorts across European airspace this week, stoking fear among passengers and heaping pressure on airlines.

Authorities have described the more than two dozen threats as malicious hoaxes, and no actual attacks have been carried out. But the incidents have highlighted the huge impact that even false claims can have on a tightly scheduled aviation network.

"This is really quite exceptional, I've never seen this before in my whole career," Wilfried Covent, head of security firm Covent Consulting with a specialization in aviation, told CNBC by phone.

"We've had bomb threats in flights and towards airports, but usually maybe a single copycat, one or two max. What we see now is a systematic attack on airlines and airports."

An email sent to European broadcasters on Friday claimed to be from the leader of the group behind the plane hoaxes and also said bombs had been placed in their buildings, prompting evacuations. No bombs were found at the sites.

"What happened this week is just a small part of what awaits Europe in the near future," the message said, according to Reuters.

The news agency said it could not verify the sender's identity, where the emails originated or whether they were linked to the threats against flights.

Covent said the "criminals" — who many security analysts say are likely to be state-backed actors working in coordination to disrupt public life for Europeans, but have not been identified — were well aware that the "weakest point of aviation is targeting it in-flight."

"With a threat on an airport or a general threat to aircraft, you can assess the situation without fully disrupting operations. When the threat is called on a specific flight number that is in the air, even with a hoax, that triggers international regulations which ensure you don't ignore it — it means a quick landing, evacuation and searches by specialist forces," he told CNBC.

"For the context of an assessment, you cannot say that 20 times before it has been a hoax so this time it is a hoax, the safety of passengers and crew is priority number one. The criminals know that."

Responsibility for dealing with hoax attacks is split, international agencies told CNBC. Assessing security threats and implementing aviation security measures primarily falls to national authorities, working in coordination with airport operators, airlines and law enforcement.

Air traffic control will also be involved in finding aircraft with an appropriate place to land, while the European Union Aviation Safety Agency, or EASA and U.K.'s Civil Aviation Authority more broadly set aviation safety rules and set how airlines prepare their crews to respond to security threats. Within the European Union, the European Commission is involved in aviation security policy and coordination between member states.

The operational decisions also depend on where the threat arises. Joji Waites, director of flight safety, policy and regulation at the British Air Line Pilots Association, said airline assessors grade a threat as red, amber or green, but that an amber verdict would never be given if the plane is airborne.

If assessors judge a threat credible, the crew is informed and the captain takes command of decisions for the aircraft, guided by company procedures. On the ground, that may mean an evacuation, while in the air the priority is to land as soon and as safely as possible.

As well as the fear and disruption to specific flights, each diversion can then affect the next flight scheduled to use that aircraft, the crew's working hours and passengers with connections.

The network effect can be significant. Philip Baum, a visiting professor of aviation security at Coventry University, told AP that a single diversion could cost an airline as much as $250,000, including replacement crews, missed connections and disruption to the rest of the day's schedule.
