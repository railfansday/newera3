#439. Tesla to wind down Cybertruck production in Texas next year to ramp Cybercab, Musk says

Tesla CEO Elon Musk on Friday said the electric vehicle maker will "be winding down" production of the Cybertruck at its Texas gigafactory around the middle of next year.

Musk, who hailed the Cybertruck as the future of the pickup truck segment, said in a post on X early Friday morning that the end of production at the massive facility would make room for manufacturing of its Cybercab robotaxi.

The Cybertruck is exclusively produced at the company's plant in Texas alongside the Tesla Model Y and two-seat Cybercab, which Musk has touted as a crucial product for the automaker moving forward.

Musk did not say whether the company would produce the Cybertruck at a different facility, but he later responded to a user on X who called the update an "end of an era" and said the Cybertruck was the "best vehicle Tesla has ever made."

"True, but the Cybertruck fleet will be around for a long time," Musk said.

Tesla began Cybertruck deliveries in late 2023. The company did not immediately respond to a request for comment from CNBC.

The Cybertruck and its polarizing design gained a niche following, but it did not achieve mass-market sales, despite Musk's pre-launch prediction that annual sales would eventually total 250,000.

"Trucks have been the same for a very long time," Musk said when the truck was unveiled in 2019. "Like 100 years. Trucks have been basically the same. We want to try something different."

While Tesla does not break out its sales by model or region, Cox Automotive estimated sales of the Cybertruck last year at 20,237 units in the U.S., down 48% from 38,965 in 2024.

One big buyer of the Cybertruck last year was Musk's aerospace and tech conglomerate, SpaceX, which spent $131 million buying up the angular steel trucks at Tesla's suggested retail price, according to a SpaceX financial filing.

The distinct look of the vehicle made some owners a target of rude gestures and more serious road rage, one owner told CNBC last May, after Musk became the biggest funder of Donald Trump's presidential campaign and led the so-called Department of Government Efficiency, an effort to slash the federal workforce and regulations.

Wired wrote in an October 2025 feature story, "There is likely no object that feels more emblematic of US president Donald Trump's return to the White House than the Tesla Cybertruck." The magazine interviewed owners who described being spit on and sworn at due to their choice of vehicle.

The Cybertruck's specifications also fell short of those Tesla advertised early on, and in some cases fell short of those offered by leading fuel-burning pickups. There have also been many quality and production problems associated with the vehicles.

The 2024 Tesla Cybertruck was the subject of 11 voluntary recalls, according to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration website. Issues ranged from a "trapped" acceleration pedal risk that could cause unintended acceleration, to software defects and an inverter fault that could result in sudden loss of drive power.

#436. Israel’s VCs see opportunity for challengers in Nvidia’s turmoil

Former Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang’s resignation has been received with worry in Israel, the company’s “second home,” as Huang described it in 2023. The company’s importance flows both ways, as the semiconductor giant has provided a major boost to Israel’s economic forecasts in recent years.

Intel, Microsoft, and many homegrown startups also conduct a sizable share of their R&D in Israel. But owing to Nvidia’s stronghold on AI given the centrality of its GPUs for training LLMs and using them, many in the country are wondering how much of a reshuffle will result from the company’s leadership crisis.

As with everything at Nvidia, Huang drove the company to acquire Israeli chipmaker Mellanox for $6.9 billion in 2019 and use the company as the base for its largest R&D facility outside of the U.S. Nvidia also made plans to build a multibillion-dollar campus in northern Israel.

“Jensen brought Nvidia to Israel with the acquisition of Mellanox, so there is a strong positive sentiment towards Nvidia and Jensen in Israel’s tech community,” Israeli repeat cybersecurity entrepreneur Pavel Gurvich told TechCrunch.

Beyond the 6,000 locals Nvidia employs, the bigger question is whether the company will remain as committed to Israel with someone else at the helm. As a result, his line of succession is being observed just as closely by those who hope the company continues to do so as by those who don’t want it to happen.

The latter have already gathered that they may be in for disappointment. In a 2023 interview that is now doing the rounds on X, interim CEO Colette Kress had declared that as Nvidia’s CFO, she typically started her day “with Israel meetings, and only then continue[d] to California.”

Kress may not remain in charge in the long run, but the many names floated for the role include two Israelis, Apple’s Johny Srouji and Nvidia’s Michael Kagan — although they’re not at the top of the still quite tentative list.

Regardless of who gets chosen, Huang’s successor will likely have less of a grip on Nvidia and the tech industry as a whole. But investors don’t think that would cause demand for Nvidia’s product to fade away.

According to Yanai Oron, a general partner at Vertex Ventures Israel, “slower decisions at Nvidia could give competitors an opening, but if that uncertainty also slows AI spending, they could end up with a bigger share of a smaller opportunity.”

Nvidia has been responsible for a vast share of the spending in the global AI industry. Under Huang’s leadership, the company used its cash reserves to massively invest across the spectrum, both in companies and in its own next-generation chips.

Such an empire may not be doomed to fall with its architect’s ouster, but it does seem less likely to become a full-on monopoly. According to Adi Levanon, a founder and managing partner at Selah Ventures, “[Huang’s] departure doesn’t dismantle Nvidia’s technology or ecosystem, but it could give competitors more room to shape what comes next.”

And now that companies globally are deploying increasingly demanding AI agents into their workflows, there’s been a lot of interest — and some venture capital — flowing into developers of AI-specific chips and inference software that could compete with Nvidia’s.

“These challengers now have a window to prove a different approach delivers better economics; a leadership change alone won’t win them the business,” Levanon said.

#433. Microsoft’s latest email outage turned into a full-blown global cloud crisis

Microsoft is slowly recovering from its daylong outage after much of the company’s systems were hit by a cascading software issue.

The problems, which started Thursday, were initially flagged as affecting the company’s services in the U.S. and the Americas, but quickly spread around the world, as engineers struggled to recover downed servers.

What began as an Outlook email and Teams video conferencing outage soon developed into a full-blown meltdown affecting the company’s cloud services for both consumer and business email, files, and documents. The outage also knocked out the company’s Copilot AI tools and Defender security screening software, and prevented system administrators from accessing their internal Intune dashboards and features. Some users reportedly experienced issues with accessing their cloud-based Windows computers.

Microsoft hasn’t yet said what caused the issues, only saying the outage involved “a large number of servers being unexpectedly unavailable.” The company said it continues to investigate the cause.

The outage affected a broad range of industries, including TechCrunch’s own email. We’ve reached out to Microsoft for comment about the incident.

As of Friday morning on the U.S. East Coast, Microsoft’s Outlook email service had begun to recover and some emails have been trickling in. The recovery comes after engineers initially could not find the cause of the issue that was causing servers in Microsoft’s data centers to fail.

Doug Madory, the director of internet analysis at Kentik, a company that monitors internet traffic worldwide, said in a post on Bluesky that traffic was returning to Microsoft’s services after about 18 hours of downtime.

Microsoft said in its latest status update on Friday that there was “no evidence of malicious activity associated with this incident,” and later noted that there was “not related to cyber or AI / agents.” That may be some solace for the millions who went without their email and online services yesterday, but the company is likely to face questions about its inability to swiftly recover from an outage of its own making.

A Microsoft spokesperson did not respond to our questions asking if the company has the technical means to detect malicious activity, such as logs, or if Microsoft has a way to check what happened.

“We’re seeing encouraging recovery progress, with service availability and email delivery performance improving across a growing portion of affected infrastructure,” said Charles Lamanna, Microsoft’s executive vice president for Copilot, agents, and platform, in a statement on Friday. “Our teams are reviewing what happened and how we can prevent similar issues going forward.”

The last major incident affecting Microsoft services was in September, when a dayslong outage hit Microsoft’s cloud services. During that outage, Microsoft blamed a “core authentication configuration” issue that prevented users from accessing the service.

#426. Small retailers brace for 'huge hit' this holiday season as shipping costs spike

When Jenni Shields began her small business eight years ago selling handmade hair accessories, she paid $2.93 to ship an order, or close to $4 in today's dollars when adjusted for inflation.

Last year, Shields said, an average shipping label for her products cost around $4.50. But when she took a typical batch of orders to her local U.S. Postal Service office last month in Kansas City, Kansas, she was shocked to see that it was an average of nearly $6 per package.

Shields is just one of the small- and medium-sized retailers who are struggling to keep up with rising shipping costs as the price of fuel continues to trend upward.

Small business owners such as Shields are facing a difficult holiday season. Customer demand is strong, but some retailers said their profits will be dented — if not eaten up entirely — by higher shipping and other costs.

Shields said she charges her customers $6 in shipping fees, which includes roughly $1.50 to cover her packaging supplies. The dramatic upswing this year means she's no longer breaking even on her orders and instead eating the added costs, she said.

"I am losing money at this point, and I've really taken a huge hit this year," said Shields, the owner of Evie Pearl Handmade, which sells bows, quilted headbands and other accessories. "I feel like it's just been this slow increase up [in shipping costs], and I feel horrible."

The surge in fuel prices since the U.S. and Israel attacked Iran in February has driven up the cost of shipping by sea, air, rail and road.

That means higher costs across the board, including for shipping giant Amazon, which reported an 11% increase in shipping expenses in the third quarter compared with last year, to individual retailers.

Globally, air cargo is going for about $3.37 per kilogram, up 43% from a similar period a year ago, according to transportation marketplace Freightos. Judah Levine, Freightos' head of research, said in addition to higher fuel, demand for fast shipping of hardware used to build data centers is helping to boost air freight rates, even weeks before peak last-minute holiday packages take to the skies.

For businesses, that runup is going to "squeeze their margins in the near-term," said Jason Seidl, managing director and transportation analyst at TD Cowen. "Larger companies tend not to be exposed to the spot market, and they'll have what we call dedicated deals in place."

But that doesn't help smaller companies, which are walking a delicate line of not wanting to alienate customers who have enjoyed free shipping for nearly a generation.

Todd Shelton, founder of the eponymous 23-year-old New Jersey clothing company that makes high-end men's jeans, T-shirts and button-up shirts locally, said he started charging customers for return shipping this week for the first time after absorbing the higher expenses this year. The shipping is deducted from customers' refunds.

Shelton said his costs have been rising for materials, too, such as his imported Japanese denim and shirt fabric from Portugal. The company is still profitable, but the higher costs have eaten into profit margins as they stay elevated, he's considering raising prices or charging for shipping to customers, he said.

Shields said she's considering raising her shipping fees for the first time in six years to keep up with the increases.

The holiday season is one of Shields' busiest. But she said she's seen a slowdown in sales because of shipping fees.

"I can't compete with Amazon on the free shipping. I can't do that. I can't afford that," she said. "I'm seeing so much of my percentage that's supposed to come back to me and my family being put towards the shipping costs."

With no sign of fuel prices declining significantly, Shields, who runs her business out of her spare bedroom at home, said she's not sure that small businesses will be able to emerge on the other side of the holidays.

Shippers including UPS, FedEx and USPS have all instituted holiday surcharges, with prices varying by package type and lasting through the end of the season. Though that's typical during the peak holiday period each year, those charges have continued to rise over the past few years.

But even as costs everywhere climb, consumers are still spending.

According to the National Retail Federation, retail sales through the end of the year are expected to grow 4.2%, translating to total spending of $1.01 trillion. That's compared with last year's holiday sales totaling $972 billion, which was 3.1% growth.

Some of that growth is from inflation, meaning consumers are simply paying more for the same goods.

"Customers all expect to spend more this holiday, but part of why they expect to spend more is they expect things to cost more, not because they'd like to spend more or they were hoping to get more quantity of stuff," said Aaron Cheris, the global retail practice head at Bain & Company. "So what I suspect happens is that non-discretionary categories do fine, because you buy the food you were going to need to buy anyway, but then the amount of cash that's left over for [discretionary] goods is less."

Still, Cheris said it's shaping up to be a record year for shipping costs, which is putting immense pressure on smaller retailers. That means many retailers may find themselves in financial distress in the first quarter of next year as they struggle to make it through the holiday season.

He added that larger retail companies will also have to reckon with rising costs, though they may not feel those effects until later quarters because they have shipping contracts that have already been locked in and have control over their own delivery fleets.

"If anything, it benefits the scale players over the little players, but it's kind of a little bit bad for everybody," Cheris said. "I think that is the challenge."

#423. Uber confirms hackers stole thousands of drivers’ license information in data breach

Uber is notifying thousands of drivers that their personal and driver’s license information was stolen during an October data breach at one of the company’s tech providers.

In notices filed this week with several U.S. attorneys general, including California, the ride-hail giant said it was notified on October 9 by the provider that hackers broke in and stole internal credentials, which were used to access Uber’s data.

The company said it subsequently began notifying affected drivers that their personal information was stored with the provider.

TechCrunch has learned that the provider is Fireworks AI, which lets developers access and use open AI models. Fireworks reported a data breach last month in which hackers stole its customers’ information, including billing data and some private AI datasets.

Online productivity startup Notion confirmed its data was also stolen in the Fireworks hack.

“The notices to drivers are in relation to a security incident impacting one of our third-party vendors, Fireworks.ai, which had access to some Uber data,” an Uber spokesperson told TechCrunch, deferring our questions to Fireworks.

Uber did not say how many customers are affected by the breach, nor how many drivers it was notifying about the incident.

When reached by email, a spokesperson for Fireworks confirmed that the company identified a security issue involving unauthorized access to some of its systems, which affected customer data, but the spokesperson did not address our questions about the incident.

According to data breach notices seen by TechCrunch, the stolen data includes driver’s licenses of 2,567 individuals in Massachusetts, 3,430 people across South Carolina, and 1,873 residents of Rhode Island. A filing with Illinois showed that some state residents had their driver’s license information stolen, but that the number of affected people was pending. Uber also notified drivers who live in California, but did not say how many had their information stolen.

The number is expected to rise as more data breach notifications roll in.

Fireworks attributed its breach to a financially motivated cybercriminal group whose goals are to sell stolen tokens for accessing large language models, or LLMs.

No known cybercriminal group has taken credit for the data theft.

This is Uber’s second known data breach this year. The company reported a security incident at its Uber Freight truck shipping unit, for which an extortion gang took credit for stealing data.

But the 63-year-old industry kingpin recently admitted that he cheated on his wife of 40 years in affairs that could have ramifications beyond Huang’s personal life and U.S. national security.

U.S. officials believe that one of Huang’s likely romantic interests, Megaspeed executive Alice Huang, is a Chinese spy who has been feeding information back to Beijing. (Alice Huang, a Chinese citizen, is of no relation to Jensen Huang.)

Megaspeed is a Singapore-based AI infrastructure company that reportedly purchased $4.8 billion worth of Nvidia chips over the last two years. The U.S. and Singapore have both previously investigated Megaspeed over suspicions that the firm was illegally sharing chips with China. The Justice Department has since taken over the federal investigation into the company, The New York Times has reported.

The story took a sudden turn on Wednesday. Citing five people who worked at Nvidia, the Times reported that Huang also had an extramarital relationship with Kimberly Powell, the company’s vice president of healthcare, who reports directly to him. Nvidia, Huang and Powell have not confirmed the report. Reached by a Times reporter, Huang reportedly said, “I don’t think you should be talking to me directly,” before hanging up.

By early Thursday morning, Huang was no longer CEO of the company he co-founded 33 years ago. Then, Thursday afternoon, Reuters dropped another bombshell, publishing a report alleging that Huang had yet another extramarital affair with another unnamed Nvidia employee.

The U.S. and China are embroiled in a fierce technological arms race to build the most advanced AI infrastructure, which could shape each country’s economic future, military prowess, and capacity for innovation. Nvidia is valuable in this competition as one of the world’s largest makers of AI chips, which are essential for accelerating AI development. But as an American company, Nvidia faces government restrictions on the sale of its most powerful chips to Chinese entities.

As the controversy unfolds, we are organizing the most relevant events into a reverse-chronological timeline to keep you up to date.

More reports continue to roll out that illustrate the shock and severity of Huang’s resignation from Nvidia.

Bloomberg reported that Huang looked “broken and shattered, his legendary energy depleted” by the time he surrendered his role on Wednesday night, uttering phrases like “whatever” and “I don’t care.”

Separately, The Wall Street Journal reported that Huang had been critical of his CFO and current interim CEO, Colette Kress. Huang is said to have described Kress as “a deer in the headlights” during a presentation this summer, and he had described Kress as someone who could not help him steer strategy or decision-making.

In a scathing letter sent to Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, Representative Richard Neal (D-MA) and Senator Ron Wyden (D-OR) said they will investigate how the Trump administration handled Nvidia’s chip sales to China, especially after Trump was briefed about Huang’s relationship with a purported Chinese spy.

“Although [Lutnick] and President Trump were reportedly briefed on this intelligence, the Administration did not appear to acknowledge this evident national security threat. On the contrary: despite reportedly learning of Mr. Huang’s compromising relationship last fall, President Trump granted Nvidia permission to sell its advanced H200 AI chips to China in December 2025 in exchange for a cut of the export sales,” the letter states.

That same evening, Senator Tim Scott (R-SC) requested that Huang and Lutnick both testify before the Senate Banking Committee.

“The American people deserve answers,” Scott wrote on X.

“Jensen and I have been very at odds as some of you already know on export controls, but Nvidia is a very important company to the United States of America,” House Foreign Affairs Committee Chairman Brian Mast said to reporters. “I don’t want to see anything hurt in Nvidia, but he has some very serious questions to answer.”

The testimony is expected to take place behind closed doors, but Mast believes the briefing will be recorded and transcribed.

The Nvidia CEO pulled out of upcoming speaking engagements at the ElevenLabs Summit and Anthropic CEO Forum.

#410. Export-banned Nvidia chips are behind China’s most powerful AI models — uh-oh, Jensen!

The leading open-weight models developed by Chinese companies were trained on Nvidia chips that the U.S. government restricted from shipping to its largest geopolitical rival, a new research effort claims.

The apparently widespread access to Blackwell GPUs among Chinese frontier labs shows the magnitude of the problem facing the leading chip designer. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang left the company this week after admitting to his affair with Alice Huang, executive of Megaspeed, a company implicated in smuggling Nvidia chips into China. Alice Huang has also been alleged to be an agent of China’s intelligence service.

While Jensen Huang’s relationships with a customer and two employees have drawn the most public interest, Nvidia’s potential corporate liability for export control violations likely factored into its decision to part ways with Huang.

U.S. export controls are intended to protect the country’s leadership in AI capabilities against China, but Huang has long sought to open up that market. Blackwell GPUs are banned from export to China or Chinese-owned firms, while the Trump administration allows Nvidia to export the H200, a less powerful chip, provided shipments take a circuitous shipping route that allows the U.S. to claim a 25% tariff.

Will DePue, a former OpenAI researcher who leads the nonprofit research group Unemployment Labs, analyzed the weights — the unique structure — of large language models released openly by DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, MiniMax, and Ant Group. The weights still reflect the configurations of the chips used to train them, and DePue was able to find compelling evidence that the most closely watched models, including Moonshot’s Kimi’s K3 releases and DeepSeek’s V4 models, bear fingerprints from training on Blackwell chips.

White House technology adviser Michael Kratsios said earlier this year that Moonshot had used export-banned Nvidia chips, citing U.S. intelligence sources. Epoch AI, a nonprofit research organization, estimates that about two million advanced chips entered China in 2024 and 2025 through schemes designed to hide their final destination.

That doesn’t mean that these models were trained entirely on Nvidia chips, or that export control laws were necessarily violated; the chips could have been in an offshore data center, which has proven a popular way for Chinese firms to access compute and driven an industrial revolution in Malaysia. Epoch’s data estimates that 275,000 Nvidia AI accelerators were imported to Malaysia in 2024 and 2025.

However, the ostensible goal of the export bans is to prevent exactly that: Chinese AI labs training on the most powerful chips. China hawks have long called for stronger controls on who uses chips, not just who owns them or where they are installed.

“If we care about the U.S. tech edge, it doesn’t make any sense to me that you would not allow Chinese companies to access chips in mainland China or Hong Kong and Macau, but then say, actually, if you just go over to Malaysia, you can do whatever you want,” Sam Bresnick, a researcher at the Georgetown Center for Security and Emerging Technology, told TechCrunch this summer.

Megaspeed, the company led by Alice Huang, has been at the center of that trade for several years through its subsidiary Speedmatrix, and has been the subject of investigations by both the U.S. and Singaporean government. Friday, Bloomberg also reported that a company with links to Alice Huang was involved in smuggling banned Nvidia chips to a data center in Shanghai.

In May, the short-selling research firm Culper published a report arguing that Nvidia had a “China problem” because a significant share of its revenue was driven by Chinese demand, either through remote data centers or smuggling in violation of U.S. law. It detailed Megaspeed’s relationship with Nvidia and alleged that Alice Huang’s companies are backed by Chinese firms that are barred from accessing U.S. chips.

Ironically, short-seller Culper’s bet against Nvidia wasn’t driven by the expectation of a U.S. government crackdown. Instead, it was focused on the Chinese government’s response to the bans, which was to recommit to its domestic chip industry.

If China succeeds, billions of dollars in Chinese demand for Nvidia chips could dry up. Some 600,000 H200 chips are reportedly stockpiled in Hong Kong, awaiting shipment into China. While Chinese companies might want the chips, observers have no consensus on why they haven’t been cleared for import.

Graham Webster, a research scholar at Stanford who focuses on U.S.-China technology policy, said he doesn’t buy the idea that holding the chips back would drive domestic production, since demand for chips is so large. Other theories for the hold-up include preparation for a potential trade deal that would involve importing a large volume of U.S. goods, or concerns about potential surveillance technology in the chips.

For now, U.S. companies have limited obligations to track what their customers do with controlled goods like AI chips. However, Jensen Huang’s personal and professional entanglements with Alice Huang create a much bigger problem for Nvidia if government investigators confirm that chip smuggling took place and that he was aware of it. Nvidia and Huang have both vouched for Megaspeed’s data centers, saying Nvidia officials had visited them to confirm they were legitimate.

The U.S. government’s treatment of Nvidia and Megaspeed is also under scrutiny. Secretary of Commerce Howard Lutnick, who oversees the agencies that license chip exports, could reportedly lose his job after reports that his investigators failed to uncover suspicious chip buyers and were too cozy with Nvidia officials.

#403. Where's the growth? Legacy automakers face a tougher 2027 as costs rise, market tightens

DETROIT — There's a growing concern that the global automotive industry may be stuck in neutral, if not reverse, heading into 2027.

The auto industry — from the companies to their customers — has been surprisingly resilient this decade, but there are fears that rising costs, weaker vehicle pricing and a more challenging market could threaten profits and growth going forward.

Automakers also face pressure outside the U.S. from an onslaught of Chinese carmakers, a thinner new-product pipeline after retreating from some all-electric plans, and company- and region-specific challenges.

"With investor focus increasingly shifting to 2027, the setup appears challenging," Barclays analyst Dan Levy said in an investor note last month. "Rising inflation, continued China pressure, affordability concerns in the U.S., and a flat global production outlook suggest another year of limited earnings growth."

Several Wall Street analysts have said automakers will need continued pricing strength and additional cost savings to offset inflation, higher investment spending and a potentially weaker economy just to maintain their current levels.

"It's a little bit more optimistic in the U.S. and then very pessimistic in Europe," RBC Capital Markets analyst Tom Narayan told CNBC. "As a whole, there's some negativity on '27."

Automakers from the U.S. to Europe to Asia have been facing a growing list of headwinds.

For U.S.-based legacy automakers, Ford Motor said it expects next year's results to be in line with 2026, while General Motors grew more cautious about its ability to continue growing earnings as much as it had previously expected.

"We continue to believe that 2027 will be another strong year and better than 2026, assuming customer demand remains strong, and commodity, logistics and other recent cost pressures moderate," GM CEO Mary Barra told investors Oct. 20.

GM CFO Paul Jacobson said that while it's too early to officially forecast, the company is facing additional inflationary costs that could lead to another $2 billion headwind in 2027, like it expects this year. He specifically cited costs of commodity inflation, logistics and DRAM, or dynamic random-access memory chips as challenges.

Those chips are critical components in modern vehicles and are used for everything from temporary data storage and real-time processing from infotainment systems to safety-critical systems.

GM and others have been able to offset outside pressures through cost cuts, operational efficiencies and resilient consumer demand for higher-margin vehicles. However, there's a limit to what companies can do.

"There's only so much pricing you can push to consumers," Narayan said. "They're already at pretty high pricing."

European automakers including Volkswagen, Mercedes-Benz and BMW in recent weeks reduced their 2026 earnings forecasts in response to U.S. tariffs, the growth of Chinese automakers and rising costs.

Most recently, BMW this week said accelerated restructuring measures would reduce its automotive pretax earnings margin this year by 2.5 percentage points. The company said the restructuring should support "positive effects" into 2027 and a 3% to 5% pretax profit margin for 2028. That compares with a 5.3% automotive margin last year and 3.4% during the third quarter.

Chrysler parent Stellantis, which has large operations in Europe and the U.S., is facing its own challenges, as it's in the midst of a restructuring that aims for a low-single-digit adjusted operating margin this year. It has maintained a 2026 guidance that includes a mid-single-digit percentage increase in net revenue and a low-single-digit adjusted operating margin.

In Asia, South Korean automaker Hyundai Motor, which has experienced notable growth in recent years, also cut key metrics when it reported earnings late last month. The company lowered its 2026 wholesales target to 4 million vehicles, down from 4.16 million, and cut its operating-margin target to between 5.5% and 6.5%, down from a previous goal of 6.3% to 7.3%.

Toyota Motor is an outlier for its most recent quarter and outlook, as the world's largest automaker increased its full-year operating income forecast by 500 billion Japanese yen (about $3.2 billion) on Thursday.

Toyota's results were boosted by weakness in the Japanese yen as well as strong hybrid vehicle demand in the U.S. However, the increase in operating income was its first in six quarters.

Broadly, the changes to earnings and outlooks come as production for the global industry slows.

Consulting and insights firm GlobalData expects global light-vehicle production of about 90.96 million units in 2026, down 2.1% compared with last year. It forecast a production increase of less than 1% next year. That comes on the heels of strong annual growth earlier this decade, led by China.

Cox Automotive reported the average transaction price of a new U.S. vehicle in September was more than $50,500. That was just shy of the record of $50,614 in December 2025.

Lofty vehicle prices have led to record-high and record-long financing deals, as well as concerns that customers are getting priced out of the new-vehicle market.

The typical U.S. new-vehicle payment reached $776 a month in September, the highest since December 2023, according to Cox Automotive.

Cox Automotive chief economist Jeremy Robb, discussing the third quarter for the automotive industry, rated the overall economy, buyer ability and borrowing costs as cautionary "yellow lights."

But he did have some "green lights," such as good credit availability and employment conditions, which could give some relief for automakers and consumers, many of whom need vehicles for their everyday lives.

Robb said there's a "tug-of-war" between headwinds such as rising costs, interest rates and fuel prices and tailwinds such as growth in liquid assets and savings that has helped offset some of the additional costs.

"Consumers remain under financial pressure, but the vehicle demand continues to hold, showing more resiliency in the auto industry than we've seen in other parts of the economy," Robb said during a Sept. 24 call, noting consumer sentiment remains low.

#402. This young startup wants to power floating data centers with the ocean’s heat, and investors are already on board

Anyone who has weathered a hurricane can tell you that the oceans — which fuel the storms — contain a tremendous amount of energy. Thanks to global warming, they’re holding more than ever.

A new startup wants to harness that energy to power data centers.

Drogue, which has kept a low profile until now, is building a system that harvests heat from the ocean to power data centers on board floating vessels. According to founder and CEO Sam Sipe, the first prototype should be bobbing around the ocean, crunching bits, less than two years from now.

The goal, Sipe said, is to help data center developers deploy servers in AI-friendly timelines. “The data center industry is a real estate game, and so they’re playing on 8- to 10-year timelines, including interoperability with utilities and [independent system operators]” that manage regional power grids, he said. “AI demand has increased 10x in just the last three years. It’s completely incompatible.”

Drogue was founded just six months ago in Washington, D.C., and the startup told TechCrunch exclusively that it has already raised $20 million, including from XYZ Venture Capital, Toyota Ventures, Tamarack Global, Dream, FDVC, Garuda Ventures, Flexcap, Boost VC, and Slow Ventures, among other investors.

In those six months, Drogue has designed a multi-megawatt prototype that will be installed on a traditional oceangoing ship and sent out to sea in early 2028. Future versions will be built on floating structures that more closely resemble offshore oil platforms.

To Sipe, the decision to turn to the ocean was obvious.

After years flying E-2D Hawkeyes off U.S. Navy aircraft carriers, he had an appreciation for the ocean’s immense power. “Most people think, oh, ocean — hard waves, storms, corrosion, biofouling. And I just see this majestic place and resource that we just ignore,” he said.

As the planet warms, the oceans have been absorbing an additional 5.8 million terawatt-hours of energy every year. By comparison, all of human civilization uses about 180,000 terawatt-hours each year. If Drogue were to take some, surely the ocean wouldn’t miss it.

The startup’s technology works like a heat pump run in reverse. A heat pump uses electricity to move heat from one place to another; Drogue’s system instead uses a difference in temperature to generate electricity. This approach, known as ocean thermal energy conversion, or OTEC, was first developed in the 1950s.

Surface waters are warmer, heated by the sun, while down-deep temperatures are much colder, about 39°F on average (4°C). “There’s basically no amount of heat we can pull out of that that will collapse that temperature differential,” Sipe said.

To leverage the temperature differential, the startup’s system draws in warm surface water, uses it to cool the data centers, and then sends the now-warmer water to boil ammonia, a chemical that turns to gas at -28°F. That ammonia gas is then sent through a turbine to generate electricity. Once through the turbine, it courses through a condenser, which turns the gas back into a liquid. There, cold water drawn from 1,000 meters deep passes over the condenser’s fins to absorb the heat. The warmed water is then released, and dissipates into the ocean. The ammonia gets sent back to the start of the sealed loop, and the cycle repeats.

The basic idea was tested in the 1980s but tossed aside. “A lot has changed since the ’80s, specifically manufacturing techniques around the pipe and the heat exchangers,” Sipe said.

To find out why, Sipe sought out people who had worked on those early efforts. “I just talked to everyone, basically interviewed them and figured out what went wrong, what went right, what would you do next. And everyone was like, dude, this stuff works. It’s just a matter of being able to raise enough capital to build something big enough, because there’s a sweet spot,” he said.

There also had to be customers who could use that power while still at sea. Sending power to shore would require lengthy, expensive cables, and manufacturing on board would require similarly expensive shipping of materials and finished goods. Data centers are ideal — the servers are compact relative to other equipment, and shipping data off the platform is incredibly cheap. Each vessel would live at sea for about a decade, Sipe said.

Drogue already has “approval in principle,” a preliminary sign-off, from the American Bureau of Shipping, which sets and checks safety standards for ships. Now it’s in the process of buying its first ship to start building a prototype ready for the open ocean.

In many ways, Drogue’s concept is similar to Elon Musk’s plan for orbital data centers: find a cheap source of power, and get the data centers as close to it as possible. Except unlike orbital data centers, Drogue’s don’t need to launch expensive rockets to get there. They can just sail a ship on the ocean, something humanity has been doing for millennia.

It was the resignation heard across the world. Jensen Huang, Nvidia’s embattled CEO who co-founded and led the company for 33 years, had officially stepped down. “It’s like a bomb went off,” one neocloud founder told TechCrunch when he first heard the news.

Most of the significant decisions to invest in startups often came from Jensen himself, according to Umesh Padval, managing partner at Seligman Ventures. Padval has known Huang since serving on the board of Mellanox Technologies, a networking chipmaker Nvidia acquired for approximately $7 billion in 2020. While there is hope that Huang’s eventual successor will resume his dealmaking spree, investors aren’t hopeful that it will happen in the short term.

Nvidia’s interim CEO, Colette Kress, is expected to institute some changes, especially when it comes to Nvidia’s investments into AI startups at the crux of the billion-dollar data center build-out. Huang’s rampant dealmaking in this sphere has been a source of concern for Wall Street.

“She’s much more conservative,” one investor said of Kress. “I don’t think she’s going to commit the same amount of money to keep the early-stage ecosystem going.”

For instance, Nvidia backed CoreWeave when it was still private and participated in two of Crusoe’s rounds, including its $1.4 billion Series E. It bought Hugging Face this year for $13 billion (expected to close in 2027) and holds investments in Mistral AI, Lambda, and Black Forest Labs. Nvidia was often the largest investor in the deals. Last year, it wrote an $800 million check into a $2 billion Series B round for Reflection AI, before the open source startup began to earn meaningful revenue, according to the Wall Street Journal.

“Jensen believed in open source and frankly speaking, Europe,” Bankiya said of how Huang invested so much in AI across the world. The cease of that support in other regions outside of America, like Europe “would be a massive funding and honestly ecosystem gap that no one can fill.”

It’s also true that Nvidia was such a dominant player in the ecosystem that at times, it felt like a monopoly. By backing so many companies with such generous checks, Nvidia effectively locked those startups into using its chips, which helped reinforce its dominant market position.

If the company does grow more conservative with its startup investments, that could leave more room for rivals and smaller competitors. (Coincidentally, Google announced two new chips this week amid Huang’s Nvidia fallout.)

Some investors with whom TechCrunch spoke hope that large cloud companies and other chipmakers will step in to back startups and fill some of the void that will likely be left by Huang.

Dharmesh Thakker, a general partner at Battery Ventures, for example, expects Big Tech rivals to vie for Nvidia’s investment crown, “depending upon who brings the best combination of a great product, an ecosystem and community to compete with CUDA, and the financial power to bankroll the ecosystem,” he said. CUDA is Nvidia’s software platform that enables developers to run AI models on its GPUs.

While companies like Google, Amazon, Microsoft, and AMD may back mature startups to drive adoption of their chips and clouds, Thakker doubts they will pour hundreds of millions into pre-revenue infrastructure startups the way Nvidia did.

Under Huang, infrastructure companies that needed $100 million to $500 million in GPUs would often raise half of that amount at valuations of $1 billion or more from Nvidia just to get started, one VC said. The chipmaker’s participation gave other investors the comfort in backing these companies at the lofty valuations.

Now Nvidia is expected to be more selective in the new companies it backs, while writing smaller-sized checks into its existing portfolio, contributing to a drop in valuations.

“When Nvidia was on the cap table, everyone felt safe,” Padval said. Without that backing, investors say traditional VCs are likely to think twice before writing checks into these startups and may even pause certain dealmaking.

Even so, few believe that Nvidia’s investment slowdown will by itself halt the broader AI boom.

Though everyone agrees that Huang is a massive loss for Nvidia, one of his ex-employees, Amulya Vishwanath, who now runs her own VC fund, said that “founders and leaders must be held accountable,” and that the ecosystem is bigger than one person. “That talent will, and must, continue to thrive.”

The Signature Edition supports a magnetic cover with built-in page-turn buttons that connects to the pogo pins on the back of the device, but I can’t speak to this experience because I didn’t have access to the case.

The biggest drawback of the Colorsoft is its price. It’s a steep investment for a device that’s just used for reading. That said, if you really want a color display, the Colorsoft is definitely worth considering. For everyone else, it’s a nice upgrade, but far from a must-have. Many readers would be better off with the basic Kindle or Paperwhite, which start at $169.99 and $199.99, respectively.

#386. Cosm rides premium moviegoing wave with more immersive screenings and a wider library

The next big thing in premium large format theatrical viewing takes inspiration from the stars.

Cosm, an immersive entertainment technology company, got its start programming planetariums — its name a combination of "cosmos" and "colosseum." The company's massive, domed screens first offered guests a view of the universe, now they mimic courtside seats at sports venues around the world and drop film fans into some of Hollywood's most iconic sets.

At a time when the theatrical industry is seeing its biggest growth from the sale of premium large format, or PLF, tickets, Cosm seeks to offer a fresh take on an elevated viewing experience. And it's quickly expanding both its footprint and its content library.

Since 2024, Cosm has opened four theaters — near Los Angeles, Dallas, Atlanta and Detroit — with a Cleveland hub set to open in 2027. These experimental venues have garnered the attention of sports fans, screening live matchups in an arena-like environment. Showings often sell out, according to Cosm.

More recently, Cosm has partnered with Hollywood studios like Warner Bros., now a part of Skydance, to bring the likes of "The Matrix," "Willy Wonka & the Chocolate Factory," "Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone" and "It" to its locations. Cosm recently signed a deal with Universal, which will bring "Shrek" to its domes in December.

The agreements mark a lucrative new chapter for the privately held Cosm, expanding its audience reach and viewing opportunities beyond timed, live events.

As a privately held company, Cosm doesn't disclose its financials. Tickets cost anywhere from $18 for a matinee to around $70 for prime seats or in-demand showings. That doesn't include added fees for concessions or items from special, themed menus tied to screenings.

Sony invested $100 million in the company in June, taking a minority stake and placing Sony Pictures Entertainment Chairman and CEO Ravi Ahuja onto its board of directors.

Cosm also counts among its investors Cosm chairman Steve Winn's firm, Mirasol Capital; former Milwaukee Bucks co-owner Marc Lasry's Avenue Sports Fund; Rocket Mortgage co-founder Dan Gilbert's Rock; and David Blitzer, managing partner of the New Jersey Devils and Philadelphia 76ers, who invested through his firm Bolt Ventures.

"Cosm is really early in its growth trajectory at the moment, and we saw an opportunity to partner with the company at, what I would say is at, a very important stage in its development," Jay Levine, chief strategy and business operations officer at Sony, told CNBC. "They have about five locations with the goal of a pretty significant rollout. And the real reason why we did it was it gave us a lot of exposure to a growing distribution and what we deem an experiential platform that we think has long-term potential."

Sony has made several plays in the theatrical space in recent years. It bought Alamo Drafthouse in 2024 and entered a long-term lease to reopen and restore the iconic Cinerama Dome in Los Angeles in July of this year.

"Our experiences strategy is around tech, it's around fandom, and it's around new and innovative approaches to content distribution," Levine said. "For us, we're all about these differentiated experiences."

Cosm's 87-foot-diameter, concave screens drop viewers into the middle of the action, whether it's an NFL game or inside the world of "The Matrix." The company's forthcoming Cleveland location will have an even bigger dome, at 100 feet in diameter.

The experience is akin to that of the Sphere in Las Vegas, which has garnered headlines since opening in 2023 for hosting high-profile concerts and events.

"People absolutely love to live out what they love in fandom, just like wearing a sports jersey," Alexis Scalice, head of Cosm Studios, told CNBC. "And I love that I'm seeing that same behavior happen with cinema."

Stadium-style seating is nestled within each Cosm dome, with booths and tables set on each level. The dome's width is such that it fills much of the viewer's periphery, giving the sensation that they are part of the on-screen environment.

The Los Angeles and Dallas locations have capacity for around 350 seats each, while the newer domes in Atlanta and Detroit have up to 500 available seats. Cosm has not shared how many seats the Cleveland location will have, but with its larger screen it is expected to have a larger number of seats.

For live sports events, Cosm has specialty cameras mounted in arenas that broadcast back to the dome, creating the illusion of being on the field of play.

For theatrical features, the film is played in a large rectangle in the center of the dome and the background is transformed to extend the scene.

In Cosm's presentation of "Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone," for example, during the iconic scene where thousands of Hogwarts acceptance letters flood through the fireplace and into a living room, the background is swarmed with animated envelopes.

Cosm has partnered with experiential production company Little Cinema and creative studio MakeMake to create the visuals and environments for each scene of a film released in the dome. From storyboarding to post-production, this process can take up to a year as Cosm, the participating studio and the visual effects artists craft and render each moment.

"We really work hard not to take away from the story, so the film remains the hero in the narrative," Scalice said. "Working very closely with Warner Bros. and our creative partners to make sure that we're not detracting too much from the story, but we're enhancing those moments for more elevated emotional response."

With the exception of "It," which was released in 2017, Cosm's theatrical offerings thus far have been films that were released decades ago and that typically have long-established fan bases.

That helps Cosm leverage what Scalice called "that nostalgia factor" — "parents coming in that ... grew up on this, and they want to share it with their children."

"We can reinvigorate these legendary films," she added.

Cosm has dabbled across genres — horror, fantasy, science fiction — and also demographics — family-friendly and R-rated. "Shrek," set to debut Dec. 16, will be its first foray into animation.

"The thing that's so incredible about it is everybody thought initially it's just going to be working for sports, and when you look at what they've done, a lot of the titles with 'Harry Potter,' with 'Shrek,' and some of the other ones, I mean, the fill rates are actually incredible," Sony's Levine said.

Studios and theatrical exhibitors alike are looking for innovative ways to showcase Hollywood's product to moviegoers, especially as major shifts in viewing habits have disrupted the business.

In the post-pandemic era, the cinema space has been defined by fewer screens and fewer moviegoers, but those declines have been masked by higher movie ticket prices, particularly for PLF experiences.

These elevated offerings — think Imax, Dolby Cinema, ScreenX and 4DX — have bigger screens and better sound or introduce a unique experience, such as moving seats and fog machines. They're particularly popular with Gen Z and millennials, who've yet to be deterred by rising average ticket prices.

While an average standard movie ticket costs around $13, premium tickets average more than $18 each, according to data from EntTelligence.

Prices for Cosm's events fluctuate based on showtime and venue. Sony's Levine said the company's ticket prices and attendance rates are "higher than I think anybody really would have thought."

Unlike Cosm's sports offerings, which can also be viewed from outside the dome from its hall and deck viewing areas at a lower price, theatrical presentations are only available in the dome.

"They have no problem selling these tickets because people want to experience these things, like an immersive format," said Mike Polydoros, CEO at cinema marketing firm PaperAirplane Media. "That is something they can't get at home."

#378. The AI graveyard: a running list of projects and startups that didn’t make it

Aside from Character.ai and Ollie, plenty of AI bets never even survive inside the companies that build them. According to S&P Global Market Intelligence, about 42% of AI initiatives are ultimately abandoned by their corporate parents. The reasons vary: insufficient funding, technical challenges, competition, difficulty scaling, or just weak user demand.

The result is a growing AI graveyard. Below is a look at some of the most notable AI products, startups, launches, and bets that have shut down, pivoted, or significantly missed expectations. Think of it less as a scorecard of flops than as a record of what these attempts can (hopefully) teach the rest of the industry.

Even the biggest players aren’t immune. OpenAI, one of the most valuable private companies in the world, has had its own share of casualties and missteps. One of the more recent examples was its attempt to turn ChatGPT into a broader “super app.” On July 9, OpenAI updated the app to combine several experiences, including separate “Chat,” “Codex,” and “Work” modes, while renaming the traditional, familiar version “ChatGPT Classic.”

The redesign quickly drew criticism from users who found the new interface confusing and cluttered, and OpenAI rolled back the change soon after, bringing the familiar ChatGPT interface back.

That wasn’t OpenAI’s only attempt to pull everything under one roof. The company has also shut down several stand-alone apps and folded their features directly into ChatGPT — a strategy that’s worked out better than the redesign did. ChatGPT Atlas, a stand-alone AI-powered web browser, lasted less than a year before being discontinued on August 9, with its most useful features absorbed into ChatGPT.

The company did the same thing with Operator, an AI agent that could browse the web and complete tasks on a user’s behalf, and OpenAI has increasingly built image generation directly into ChatGPT, shrinking the role of DALL-E as its own separate destination for creating images.

We also can’t forget its video-sharing platform, Sora, which struggled with high operating costs and user-retention challenges and shut down in March 2026.

When Apple unveiled Apple Intelligence in 2024, an improved Siri was one of the highlighted features. The pitch promised a Siri that could understand context, know what was happening across apps, and get tasks done effectively.

However, Apple repeatedly pushed back the release of the new Siri, citing various reasons like engineering issues and bugs. This delay eventually contributed to a $250 million settlement over claims regarding how Apple marketed the AI capabilities of the iPhone 16.

Siri AI finally appeared in the iOS 27 beta this past July, with the new AI-powered version rolling out to English-language users first this month; support for other languages is expected to follow.

Founded in 2021 by former Google researchers Noam Shazeer and Daniel De Freitas, Character.ai was a consumer AI app that let people chat with customizable bots, many of them modeled on celebrities and fictional characters. It raised $150 million at a $1 billion valuation in 2023. Then, the next year, Google paid a reported $2.7 billion to license its technology and bring its founders back into its orbit, leaving the startup to carry on without them.

Character.ai has run into a lot of trouble over the years. Families have sued, alleging the chatbots contributed to teen suicides and self-harm, and Disney sent a cease-and-desist letter over unauthorized use of its characters. Character.ai barred under-18 users from open-ended chats late last year and agreed to settle several of the suits, along with Google, in early 2026.

But it wasn’t enough. The company filed for bankruptcy in early October, citing legal, regulatory, and operational headwinds. In an ironic twist, Disney is now the opening bidder, offering $20 million for a license to some of its intellectual property. The app is still running during the process, and the sale is expected to wrap up by late November or early December.

Relay, an AI-powered workflow automation tool built as an alternative to Zapier, shut down entirely in August 2026.

The product let users automate email and task workflows using AI agents, but as OpenAI, Google, and other larger platforms built similar automation directly into their own tools, five-year-old Relay struggled to hold on to a reason to exist as a stand-alone product.

Similar to the other family-focused agents, the idea behind Ollie was that families already have the information they need, but it lives across school messages, calendars, task lists, and group chats. Ollie aimed to bring those pieces together and keep them in sync. It kept track of calendars, emails, reminders, tasks, and other household details, then delivered relevant information to users through text.

However, the app faced a very common challenge: keeping up with heavyweights like Muse and Instinct. Co-founder and CEO Bill Lennon revealed the app would shut down on November 6, citing difficulties like being unable to stand out, failing to grow, and not raising enough capital.

Announced at its 2024 Build developer conference, Microsoft’s Recall feature was pitched as an AI-powered “photographic memory” for Windows PCs, periodically taking screenshots of what a user was doing so they could later search back through their own digital history.

Even after a redesign, the controversy hasn’t gone away. A cybersecurity researcher recently built a tool that could extract and display data that Recall had captured, reviving questions about whether Microsoft has actually fixed the underlying problem.

Notion Mail launched in April 2025 as an AI-focused email product designed to help users organize and automate their inboxes. However, the company noticed its users were using separate AI agents to handle their email inboxes instead. As a result, Notion Mail is scheduled to shut down on September 22.

The Humane AI Pin is probably one of the most well-known failures in AI hardware. Designed to provide AI features through a wearable device rather than a traditional smartphone, Humane raised $230 million from investors and garnered significant attention.

But the product struggled mightily with performance, and the situation worsened when Humane warned customers to stop using its charging case because of a potential battery fire risk. Humane then shut down its AI Pin business in February 2025, with most of the company’s assets acquired by HP for $116 million.

The Rabbit R1 was another heavily hyped AI device. Unveiled at CES in January 2024, the AI companion was designed to perform tasks on behalf of its owner. Rabbit said it had sold 100,000 units shortly after launch.

However, strong initial sales didn’t mean it was a strong product. Early reviews described the device as unfinished, with unreliable performance and a limited number of useful integrations.

The company hasn’t given up, though. It has continued updating the R1, positioning it as a computer controller capable of performing agentic tasks. It also recently announced a new hardware project, dubbed “Project Cyberdeck,” aimed at creating a portable device built for vibe-coding.

Huxe was an AI audio app, built by former developers of Google’s NotebookLM, that turned written information into conversational, podcast-style audio.

The company shut down in May 2026, partly as larger platforms began offering similar experiences to large existing audiences. For instance, Spotify has been expanding its own AI-powered audio tools.

Yupp was an interesting AI product: a free playground that allowed users to compare responses from hundreds of AI models side by side. Users could also vote on responses and earn cryptocurrency through the platform.

At its peak, the platform let users test more than 800 models, including from companies such as OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic. But according to its founders, Yupp never achieved strong enough product-market fit to survive. The platform shut down in March 2026.

Figgs AI operated from 2023 to 2024, letting users create and interact with customizable AI characters for role-play and storytelling. The platform reportedly drew more than 1 million users, but its developers said keeping the service free became too expensive to sustain, and they shut it down.

#353. Where was Nvidia’s security team?

Outsiders had little reason to notice when Nvidia’s longtime chief security officer and head of product security stepped down in August. Just two and a half months later, CEO Jensen Huang himself left the company over growing questions about his extramarital relationships — starting with an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore data center company that U.S. investigators suspect of funneling Nvidia chips to China, then widening to reports about two separate affairs with Nvidia employees.

At the time, David Reber, who spent four years as CSO and seven at Nvidia overall, said he was leaving for health reasons. “This wasn’t a pivot I had planned. But I know what needs to come first: family and health,” he wrote on LinkedIn in late August, disclosing that he’d been diagnosed with young-onset Parkinson’s more than two years ago.

Reber’s replacement, Saša Zdjelar, previously spent 17 years at ExxonMobil in Houston, leaving as a senior IT manager before spending one year at Salesforce and several years at the cybersecurity company ReversingLabs. Zdjelar didn’t respond to questions Thursday about the transition or what the security team knew.

Nothing public connects Reber’s or Zdjelar’s role changes to the Megaspeed matter, but it seems fair to wonder what Nvidia’s security team knew about the government’s scrutiny of Megaspeed and about Huang’s relationships, and whether anyone raised concerns. Reber didn’t respond to questions Thursday. Nvidia also has not answered our related questions.

Relationships between a CEO and his employees typically fall to HR, legal and the board, not security. But security specialists say any affair — even one inside the office — can give a foreign intelligence service leverage over an exec who wants it kept quiet. And Huang’s relationship with an executive at Megaspeed, a company under federal investigation, wasn’t an office matter at all. (Megaspeed has denied any wrongdoing.)

Still, spotting the clues isn’t easy. The way intelligence services collect information, much of it through everyday conversation rather than hacking, makes it especially difficult for security teams to identify risks, according to retired senior intelligence officer Nicholas Eftimiades, who says he has trained more than 1,000 corporate security professionals.

Some people inside Nvidia clearly knew parts of the picture. Nvidia’s top lawyer was engaged with the government’s Megaspeed inquiry more than a year ago, The New York Times reported earlier this week. (Nvidia has said it regularly contacts officials.) Reports about Huang’s relationships with employees suggest colleagues also knew of them, even if the behavior is not expressly forbidden by Nvidia’s code of conduct. The Times cited five Nvidia employees who said he was involved with a vice president who reported directly to him, and Reuters, citing two people, later reported a second relationship. What’s unclear is whether any of that reached security.

Even if it had reached Nvidia’s security team, Eftimiades, who wrote Chinese Espionage Operations and Tactics in 2020, said a security team’s resources are often limited. For one thing, they’re often underfunded because, unlike some other cost centers, they don’t drive revenue. It’s astonishingly common for CEOs to answer a security team’s warnings with “not a nickel more than compliance,” he said.

Many companies also define security overly narrowly, specialists say. In an opinion piece in The Hill, Don Aviv, CEO of the corporate investigations firm Interfor International, and author Jeremy Hurewitz, wrote, “Too many companies still approach security primarily as a matter of protecting facilities, preventing cyberattacks, and responding to physical threats.” The two argued that “[s]ecurity teams need to pay greater attention to insider threats, including the cultivation of executives and employees by foreign intelligence services, suspicious business relationships, conflicts of interest and attempts to obtain sensitive information through trusted personal contacts.”

It would be surprising if Nvidia’s security team didn’t have a more expansive remit than most companies given its scale and business partners, but it’s possible.

In fairness, some of it is simply beyond what any team can do. Speaking generally, not about Huang, Eftimiades offered a hypothetical in which a CEO complains that he can’t do something because “I’m fighting a board who doesn’t want to do this.” That tells an intelligence asset who the obstacle is. The service then asks, “Who’s the sticking point here?” and looks for ways into that person’s email and phone.

What a rival intelligence service wants is often less technology than policy insight, including who’s pushing to change export rules and who the biggest blocker is. “Intel world tends to be like cancer,” he said. “Once it gets into the body, it looks to spread and looks to reach out.”

The playbook isn’t new, Eftimiades added, “particularly for China.” Any executive dealing with the country “will tell you that they’ve been approached.” He said there are also “tons of diplomat stories” of U.S. diplomats walking into a hotel room to find a woman on the bed. He said what’s newer compared with Soviet era spying is the relationships these services cultivate now often last years, not weeks.

Technology can augment that human intelligence, Eftimiades said, pointing to IMSI catchers as one example. The devices mimic a cell tower so that nearby phones connect to them. A basic version can cost about $1,000 and fit in a suitcase, he said, though government-grade models cost far more, and there were a couple of cases involving Chinese espionage where these factored in last year.

Eftimiades says no one knows yet if the devices were used against Huang, whose phone was presumably hardened such that compromises could be detected, if not stopped altogether. But what Nvidia’s security team knew, and whether it could have acted on it, will only become clearer as the board’s independent investigation and federal inquiries proceed.

In the meantime, Eftimiades said he hopes the whole episode is “a wake-up call” for U.S. industry, which he said is mostly “clueless” about China’s tactics. “We have lost trillions [of dollars] in intellectual property,” is his guess. “Whatever we think we’re doing isn’t working.”

#335. Poppi founders are going after PepsiCo's Gatorade with new beverage startup Jetty

After disrupting the soda category with Poppi, founders Allison and Stephen Ellsworth are now taking aim at hydration with their new brand, Jetty.

Starting Monday, Jetty drinks and ready-to-mix electrolyte packets will be on shelves at Whole Foods grocery stores and available online at Target and Amazon.

The new, self-funded venture directly pits the Ellsworths against PepsiCo, which bought Poppi for $1.95 billion in 2025. Pepsi owns Gatorade, which dominates the U.S. sports drink category with about 60% market share, according to Numerator data.

Poppi shook up the soda category with prebiotic fiber, low calorie and sugar counts and its fun marketing aimed at Generation Z and millennials. Jetty looks to upend the hydration category through a similar strategy. To do so, it will face off not only against Gatorade but also a swarm of other upstarts looking to get in on the electrolyte-fueled trend.

For decades, Gatorade and other sports drinks have pitched their products to athletes who need electrolytes and fluids after an intense workout, although many consumers drink them every day without even sweating. Jetty is aimed at people who need a hydration boost, but not the same level of electrolytes or sugar as a traditional sports drink.

"No one has really created a product for just everyday hydration — literally everyday hydration for normal people," Allison Ellsworth, Jetty's chief brand officer, told CNBC. "Like I woke up this morning, I didn't run a marathon."

Following Poppi's sale to PepsiCo, Ellsworth stayed on as an advisor to the brand, although she said she is no longer involved in the day-to-day operations.

After 10 years building Poppi, Ellsworth said, she finally had free time. As Ellsworth tells it, she went to the doctor for the first time in a decade. She started using a GLP-1 medication and lost 60 pounds.

Ellsworth's husband, Stephen, now CEO of the new brand, noticed she wasn't hydrating, even when she came back from the gym.

GLP-1 medications such as Ozempic and Mounjaro can make people less thirsty. But because staying hydrated can also help mitigate some of the drugs' side effects, such as nausea and headaches, it can become more important.

"I physically struggle getting liquid into my body," Ellsworth said.

Plain old water is often less appealing to consumers, even dehydrated GLP-1 users, but more flavorful options with added benefits can be tempting. About 44% of GLP-1 users report consuming more functional beverages, according to EY-Parthenon's third annual survey of more than 3,600 current or former GLP-1 users.

Ellsworth sees a broader appeal for Jetty, too.

"Our core audience is going to be millennial moms and Gen Z," she said. "Gen Z just isn't relating to these old school brands, the legacy of the Gatorades and the Powerades. They're just not cool."

Consumers broadly have become more interested in staying hydrated.

Dollar sales of hydration and electrolyte products climbed 20% to $2.6 billion over the 52 weeks ended Oct. 4, based on data from SPINS, which tracks new universal product codes, or UPCs. The category also has a strong repeat buyer rate of 62%.

However, the Ellsworths aren't the only ones to notice the potential in hydration. Big food and beverage companies have been looking to hydration, too.

Kraft Heinz debuted Capri Sun juice pouches and Kool-Aid packets with electrolytes this spring. In April, Pepsi unveiled an overhaul of the Gatorade brand, focused on making it more appealing to a wider swath of consumers, with more potential occasions like a "fresh morning start" or an "afternoon walk." And Electrolit is a top performer for its distributor Keurig Dr Pepper, with 40% retail sales growth in the company's third quarter.

All of that means more competition for shelf space and consumers' "share of throat," an industry term for beverage market share.

To increase Jetty's visibility across the grocery store and to maximize convenience for shoppers, Jetty will be available in both powders and bottles.

Single-use electrolyte mixes are growing at a much faster clip than the ready-to-drink category, Ellsworth said, thanks to Unilever's Liquid I.V. and smaller upstarts like LMNT. On Amazon alone, powders are a $1 billion category, according to Ellsworth.

The ready-to-drink, or RTD, sports beverages are a much larger market, but the category's sales are shrinking as consumers reach for other hydration options instead. Unit sales of sports drinks have fallen 9.1% from 2022 to 2026, according to Numerator data. And Coca-Cola has written down the value of BodyArmor by more than $1.7 billion after buying control of the brand for $5.6 billion in late 2021.

"Me and my husband know how to do RTD," Ellsworth said.

Jetty will launch with five flavors, including watermelon, orange mango, fruit punch, black raspberry and white cherry. All of them contain 100 milligrams of sodium, 310 milligrams of potassium and 100 milligrams of magnesium in a 15.2-ounce bottle or 15-gram packet.

In addition to electrolytes, Jetty products also contain B vitamins, 3 grams of fiber and 6 grams of sugar. It doesn't use artificial sweeteners or dyes, its founders say.

For comparison, a 20-ounce bottle of traditional Gatorade Thirst Quencher has about 270 milligrams of sodium and 80 milligrams of potassium, plus 34 grams of sugar. The sports drink brand is also phasing out artificial colors and promoting lower-sugar versions.

"What's really interesting about this product and this opportunity is we now can capture all those lapsed drinkers — or that generation that didn't even ever drink it in the first place," Ellsworth said.

Poppi's new owner knows about Jetty and the market it is trying to capture, too.

"I don't think [Pepsi] gets it, but what they do get is that the movement is changing, and they think that just by taking dyes out or doing 90% less sugar, they're going to capture this audience," Ellsworth said. "But that is very big corporate thinking."

She added that she believes Pepsi has missed the mark on advertising to Gen Z consumers across its portfolio, such as in its campaigns for Sprite competitor Starry.

Perhaps as an extension of that, Poppi's performance has been underwhelming under PepsiCo's ownership.

The food and beverage giant now estimates the fair value of potential performance-based payments to Poppi's prior owners is $12 million, Pepsi said in a regulatory filing in October. At the time of the deal, Pepsi promised an additional payment of $300 million if Poppi hit certain targets by the third quarter of 2027. The Ellsworths declined to comment on the filing to CNBC.

This time around, with one successful brand under their belts, Jetty's founders say they have learned from past mistakes. Those lessons should help Jetty as it launches at a much larger scale than Poppi did.

Three different claims lawyers have signed off on Jetty's packaging, according to Ellsworth. Poppi paid $8.9 million to settle class-action litigation alleging false advertising over its "gut healthy" claims.

For now, Jetty is entirely self-funded with $2.5 million, although Ellsworth said that the brand will likely raise money in 2027. This time, though, she said investors will probably look less like traditional venture capitalists.

"I want to bring in investors that bring value," Ellsworth said. Instead of a venture capitalist who is willing to invest $15 million, Jetty would rather consider investors who can only write a check for $1 million but have access to celebrities. "So then you're getting access to talent on a cheaper level and people that are invested in the brand."

And Jetty has also learned a few lessons from Gatorade. For example, the brand is already at work on a lemon-lime version — the original flavor of Gatorade.

"We'll eventually do it," Ellsworth said. "But it was really important at the beginning just to stick to flavors that we love, for it to taste good."

#326. Restaurant chains refocus on food quality to bring back diners

Juicier chicken tenders. Revamped sauces. Richer and creamier Frosties.

Across the restaurant industry, chains are turning their focus back to their food in the hopes that better quality will win back diners.

Over the last two years, consumers have dined out less frequently and grown pickier about where they choose to eat, based on data from Black Box Intelligence. In that time, "value" has been the buzzword du jour for restaurant executives, who have explained to analysts and investors that the word encompasses more than menu pricing alone.

Now, industry CEOs are turning to their corporate chefs to try to beat rivals on quality and taste, as they look to expand their definition of value. Discounts and one-off promotions can lift traffic for a time, but the actual product still matters for restaurants' long-term growth, as well as franchisee profitability.

From Wendy's to Chipotle Mexican Grill, chief executives touted new or upcoming improvements to menu items on their quarterly earnings conference calls in recent weeks.

"We think the product's improved and even better," Chipotle CEO Scott Boatwright told analysts on the company's call in late October, referring to the burrito chain's brisket, which returned to menus for a limited time Thursday.

For some chains, better food is a key step in ambitious turnaround plans.

For example, "quality food at real value" is one pillar of Wendy's five-part turnaround strategy that the chain unveiled on Thursday.

"We're also rebuilding our food offerings and have begun testing improvements to quality and craveability of our core items, including thicker, crispier bacon, a richer and creamier Frosty, whole leaf lettuce on sandwiches and improved salads," Wendy's CEO Bob Wright told analysts on the call Thursday.

That same day, the company reported its seventh straight quarter of same-store sales declines and publicly rejected a $1.4 billion takeover bid led by Nelson Peltz's Trian Partners. Wendy's said the proposal did not take into account the potential value that its turnaround plan could unlock.

There is precedent to suggest that better-tasting food can contribute to higher sales.

In 2009, Domino's Pizza changed its recipe, swapping out its sauce, adding garlic to its crust and switching to shredded mozzarella with a hint of provolone. Along with blunt commercials that compared its previous crust to cardboard and old sauce to ketchup, the changes helped to spur U.S. same-store sales growth of 9.9% the following year.

Now, some of the same team that led the Domino's turnaround is copying the playbook at Burger King's U.S. restaurants.

Part of the burger chain's multiyear comeback plan has focused on revamping its core menu items. It started earlier this year with its flagship offering, the Whopper, which received a better bun and a new mayonnaise. And in September, it rolled out an overhaul of its nuggets, switching to 100% white meat, crispier breading and a more flavorful marinade. It also reformulated its sauces.

"We've tried to look at our biggest categories first," Josh Kobza, CEO of Burger King parent Restaurant Brands International, told CNBC. "We've also thought about the ones where the fixes are more straightforward and less operationally involved."

Following the changes, Whopper sales were up by a double-digit percentage in the third quarter, Kobza said on the company's earnings call in late October. And nugget sales climbed more than 35%. Burger King said its U.S. same-store sales jumped 8.4% in the quarter.

Like Domino's, Burger King is highlighting diners' past gripes with its Whoppers and nuggets in its ads, which star Burger King U.S. president and former Domino's executive Tom Curtis.

Better-tasting food isn't just for chains that are in trouble.

Even Chili's, which has seen its same-store sales soar 52% over the past three years, is improving more of its existing menu options. After tackling its baby back ribs, burgers and nachos, it will turn next to salads and steaks, Brinker International CEO Kevin Hochman told CNBC.

"We're going to be doing more dry aging of our beef to make it more tender, versus doing it in a plant," he said. "It's a little bit more expensive, but we think the taste is going to be a whole lot better."

Chili's rival Applebee's launched a juicier chicken tender during its latest quarter, according to John Peyton, CEO of the chain's parent company Dine Brands. In 2027, Applebee's plans to overhaul more of its protein options.

"We think proteins are, number one, our biggest opportunity," Peyton told CNBC. "Number two, they're a big focus of the way people are thinking about eating right now, leaning into proteins and a little bit less with the carbs."

Chicken is also a focus for McDonald's, which is testing hand-breaded wings, strips and a sandwich in a handful of Chicago-area restaurants. It plans to expand the trial to more locations in the U.S. and Ireland in early 2027.

Moreover, McDonald's is trying to improve its coffee, with fresher beans and upgraded equipment, executives said at the company's investor day in September. Both chicken and coffee are fast-growing categories, offering more opportunities to lift sales than McDonald's signature beef burgers.

While the burger giant isn't in full turnaround mode like rivals Wendy's and Burger King, its U.S. business has struggled in recent quarters after its value strategy didn't gain the traction the company had hoped. In the third quarter, its U.S. same-store sales fell 0.2%.

In addition to menu tests, McDonald's is also leaning into training to improve the quality and consistency of its core menu items. Starting in October, the company launched "Make It Golden," a multiyear training program for corporate employees, restaurant workers and franchisees.

Ultimately, improved menu items mean little if the recipe changes aren't well executed.

#303. Eli Lilly’s Foundayo obesity pill eases sleep apnea in late-stage trial, paving way for approval

Eli Lilly on Monday said its obesity pill Foundayo significantly reduced the severity of obstructive sleep apnea, the most common sleep-related breathing disorder, in patients with obesity, in a late-stage trial.

The drugmaker plans to file by the end of the year for U.S. approval of the daily pill for moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea, or OSA, in adults with obesity or who are overweight. The results make the pill — which rolled out in the U.S. in April — one of several GLP-1 treatments to show promise in treating the condition.

OSA refers to interrupted breathing during sleep due to narrowed or blocked airways, which can cause drops in oxygen and increases in blood pressure that are associated with serious complications, such as hypertension, heart failure and stroke. The condition affects nearly 1 billion adults globally, including 80 million people ages 20 and over in the U.S., according to the National Council on Aging. Most have never been diagnosed, Lilly said in a release.

Lilly's blockbuster weight loss injection Zepbound became the first medication to win U.S. approval for OSA in December 2024. Patients have limited treatment options outside of that drug and many use cumbersome machines that provide positive airway pressure, or PAP, to allow for normal breathing.

"Foundayo is already making a difference for people living with obesity, and with plans to submit these results to the FDA by the end of the year, we could have the opportunity to help people living with OSA," Dr. Thomas Seck, senior vice president of product development of Lilly Cardiometabolic Health, said in a release.

The trial tested Foundayo in two independent studies, the first of which included 363 patients who were unable or unwilling to use PAP therapy. The second study included 349 people who were on PAP therapy for at least three months at the time of screening and continued that treatment during the trial.

Patients in both studies who took Foundayo started at a low dose and increased it gradually at four-week intervals to different maximum dosages.

Researchers specifically examined how much the pill reduced the so-called apnea-hypopnea index, or AHI, which records the number of times per hour a person's breathing shows a restricted or completely blocked airway. The index is used to evaluate the severity of obstructive sleep apnea and the effectiveness of treatments for the condition.

People who took Foundayo and were not using PAP therapy had an average of 21.1 fewer AHI events per hour at 52 weeks, compared with an average reduction of 4.8 per hour for the placebo, according to the results.

Foundayo also met the trial's second goal in that group, leading to an overall AHI reduction of 43.8% compared with a decrease of 6.3% for the placebo. Lilly said 31.5% of the participants in the Foundayo group met the criteria for remission or improvement to mild OSA.

Meanwhile, patients who were on both Foundayo and PAP therapy had an average of 18.3 fewer AHI events per hour at 52 weeks, compared with an average reduction of 5.5 per hour for the placebo.

#289. Why the UK is dithering over what to do about e-scooters

Why the UK is dithering over what to do about e-scooters

When I step out of Southampton Central railway station, it takes me just seconds to spot an e-scooter. In this city, these devices have become a normal part of the transport ecosystem, along with e-bikes.

"It's just the most convenient way to commute from home to the station and back," says Keith Reed, who I speak to as he arrives at the station to catch a train to London. Keith says uses rental e-scooters twice a week. His alternative option would be his bike, but he'd then have to worry about where to leave it.

Around the city, there are parking areas for bright orange rental devices everywhere - but they're not necessarily a permanent feature. Southampton hosts one of the numerous government-backed trials around England. This one has been going on since 2021.

A study suggests around half of users of e-scooter do so in conjunction with another mode of transport - like Keith, who rides to the railway station

It also doesn't take long to find e-scooters which aren't part of the trial, but instead are owned by individuals. I see a man riding one along the pavement outside a parade of shops. He doesn't want to stop and talk.

These privately owned e-scooters are illegal to ride on public roads across the UK, but they've proliferated regardless. One expert told me the situation was a "Wild West".

Among people I spoke to in Southampton, the same concerns came up time and time again: "They're a blinking nuisance," says elderly pedestrian Fred. Another shopper, Martin, says he's "very concerned" whenever he sees them racing through precincts: "An accident's almost inevitable."

The endgame of the trials is to reach a decision on whether to make permanent hired e-scooter schemes such as the one Keith uses - and whether to bring in legislation that would legalise and regulate privately owned e-scooters too.

But six years since the trials started around England, there's still no conclusion - and as the UK government has responsibility for road traffic legislation across Great Britain, officials in Scotland and Wales say they are waiting on Westminster to act.

So why is it taking so long for the government to decide what to do, and what are the consequences of delay? What does the evidence tell us about how safe these things are?

And why are people using them in the first place - are they here to stay as a useful part of the transport offer, or will they end up being viewed as a pest on our streets?

You'd recognise an e-scooter if you saw one. The Department for Transport's (DfT's) definition of an e-scooter includes no pedals, two wheels and a maximum continuous power rating of 500W.

And so far, the trials around England appear to have proved popular. According to DfT research, fom January 2022 to May 2024, 36.9 million trips were made across 30 trial areas outside of London. On average, nearly 19,500 e-scooters were available to hire every day.

Dr Yasir Ali, a reader in Transport Modelling at Loughborough University, says e-scooters are "complementary" to other modes of transport, rather than replacing them. Due to factors such as range and being exposed to the elements, they are less suitable for longer trips, rural areas with less infrastructure or in bad weather. They work best as "a first- and last-mile solution" in urban areas, to reduce reliance on cars for short trips.

Shared mobility charity CoMoUK's 2025 report suggests that for more than half of the people using rental e-scooters, the most common use was travelling to work or school. Its analysis of DfT data suggests the majority of people using hired e-scooters are male and under 35.

"Sometimes it's a bit difficult to take the bus from dot to dot", says Yan Zhang, who works at Southampton university and doesn't drive, "so for shorter distances, I'll take the scooter."

Like Yan Zhang, e-scooter users are less likely to have a car, CoMo's report says - this is partly explained by official trials' concentration in urban areas

Around half of respondents to CoMoUK's online survey used it in conjunction with another mode of transport - such as riding to a railway station, like Keith.

Earlier evidence from a national evaluation of the trials published in 2022 found that although some users would have driven instead had they not had access to an e-scooter, the biggest "modal shift" - meaning switch in ways of getting around - to e-scooters was from walking. Given walking is an active mode of travel, this has raised questions over how significant the public health impact of e-scooter use really is.

And the usage of privately owned e-scooters isn't reflected in this research. The DfT has said between 760,000 and 1.2 million people in England aged 16 and over own one - despite the fact that it remains illegal to ride them in public.

In the eyes of the law, e-scooters are classed as motor vehicles. Outside of the trials, they can't be insured, and thus can only legally be ridden on private land, with the owner's permission.

Parking bays for e-scooters and e-bikes are available across Southampton

The trials themselves are bound by strict rules. The devices' top speed is limited. Businesses who operate the pilots must insure them. Users must have a full or provisional driving licence, and they must not ride on the pavement. Helmets are recommended but not mandatory.

Richard Dilks, CEO of CoMoUK says "mass-scale use of something that is inherently low-carbon, low-impact, low-cost" is a "good thing" but "we also don't have rules of engagement about how they should be ridden".

And as a result of the delays in introducing regulation, Loughborogh University's Yasir Ali says "we are increasingly in a situation where the law and reality have diverged", with "the disadvantages of e-scooter use without securing the full benefits of regulation".

Back in Southampton, I meet a lady pushing a black e-scooter, who doesn't want to give her name. She says nobody told her when she bought it that it was illegal to ride on the streets.

The Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency has a unit that carries out surveillance of retailers to check they're complying with the law. But earlier this month, an investigation by the Press Association news agency discovered some retailers were advertising them for commuting purposes.

Police tell me they regularly see children illegally riding private e-scooters. Margaret Winchcomb, deputy executive director at the Parliamentary Advisory Council for Transport Safety (PACTS), says some are being specifically marketed at kids.

Police say they will seize privately owned e-scooters that are ridden illegally

So what can police do? Forces regularly launch crackdowns where they seize illegally used devices, and often crush them. Each year, awareness campaigns run urging parents not to buy their kids an e-scooter for Christmas. But enforcement is a challenge.

Insp Matt Boiles from Avon and Somerset's road policing team says problems arise even with the legal trial devices - for example, people riding them after drinking alcohol, which counts as drink-driving. Riding with another person on the back is another common issue.

But officers see private e-scooters being illegally ridden in public places "all the time, every single day" and they can be really hard to catch - it isn't always safe for police in a car to pursue someone on an e-scooter, he says.

Insp Boiles despairs over parents buying them for children: "We do have parents attend a police station quite a lot, getting very, very upset that their child's Â£500 e-scooter has been seized." But "the legislation is really, really clear that you can't have it back".

There's a potential risk of fire if unregulated devices with lithium batteries are left to charge unattended. Most train operators have banned e-scooters on board for this reason.

But there's also the fact that riders are unprotected in the case of falling off or being hit by motorised vehicles. And - along with e-bikes - they can end up involved in collisions where pedestrians are hurt. Many trial e-scooters in the UK are restricted to 12.5mph, but privately owned scooters' speeds aren't.

Some coroners have even raised concerns. In 2022, a coroner in London called for action after the death of 14-year-old Fatima Abukah on a privately owned scooter.

According to the DfT, in 2024 - the most recent year for which figures are available - 1,390 casualties were recorded in collisions that involved e-scooter in England, Scotland and Wales, three more than in the previous 12 months. The vast majority, 1,096, were e-scooter users themselves.

Young boys and men appear to be over-represented in the stats. Of the casualties, 302 - the most of any age and sex category - were males aged 10-19.

Six deaths were recorded in 2024, unchanged from the previous year. Five of these were riders and one a pedestrian.

Although the numbers aren't conclusive, it is thought more accidents are happening on privately owned e-scooters. Winchcomb says police statistics "aren't reflective of the number of injuries".

Nonetheless, campaigners believe there is enough evidence to show that regulation is urgently needed.

Carly Calland's 14-year-old son Jacob died in March 2025 of a catastrophic head injury. He was a passenger on an e-scooter that was involved with a collision with a car.

If privately owned e-scooters are legalised for public use, Carly believes, there should be mandatory helmets, a ban on carrying passengers and penalties for parents that allow children to ride illegally.

"If Jacob was wearing a helmet that day, he would still be here," Carly, from Wythenshawe in Greater Manchester, says.

Carly is not against e-scooters. They "are really good for people to get to work, and they are eco-friendly", she tells me, but "they just need to be used in the correct way."

In recent years, successive British transport secretaries have supported the idea of a new law to legalise and regulate the e-scooters available for sale. E-scooters were mentioned in the 2022 Queen's speech, but nothing materialised.

It was a Saturday, and with the holiday season getting under way, thousands of passengers were heading to London's Heathrow and Gatwick airports to catch flights abroad. It was always going to be a very busy day.

What followed, however, was chaos for travellers. As air traffic controllers struggled to route aircraft around the storms, airspace became heavily congested and many planes due to fly were simply forced to wait on the ground. In the end, some 900 flights to and from both airports were delayed, some by up to 11 hours. Dozens more were cancelled. At airports across Europe, passengers expecting to fly into London also faced long waits and uncertainty.

The weather in the UK may now have cooled, and passengers may soon forget their experiences. But some within the industry believe what happened in June should be seen as a warning.

The timing in this case was clearly unlucky. But there are concerns that such incidents are only going to become more common as the effects of climate change mount up around the world and weather systems are likely to become increasingly unstable, making disruption more likely and adding to costs for airlines, airports and travellers.

Tim Atkinson, an airline risk consultant and former pilot, says the very crowded skies over the UK and northern Europe mean the region is particularly exposed.

He thinks there is a real risk, for example, that all of London's airports could be affected by storms at the same time, potentially with major consequences in the air and on the ground.

"What we're seeing now is very rapid climate change," he says.

This is where the scenario set out by Atkinson comes in. He warns of a situation where traffic is diverted from Heathrow, Gatwick, Luton and Stansted at the same time, leaving aircraft coming in across the Atlantic and a large number of European flights all looking for somewhere to land at the same time, and some with dwindling fuel reserves.

This would at the very least create serious disruption for passengers, who might end up hundreds of miles from their destinations. But it could also, he thinks, create "a very serious risk event, such as an aircraft running out of fuel in the sky".

Thunderstorms are caused by what is known as convective activity, where moist air close to the ground warms rapidly and rises quickly. As it rises, it expands and cools, allowing the moisture it carries to condense, forming large clouds. More heat means more convective energy, potentially creating instability. In addition, for every degree the Earth warms, the atmosphere can hold about 7% more moisture, increasing the probability of heavy rainfall.

It is not clear whether this means we will actually get more thunderstorms in the UK and across Europe, as so many variables are involved beyond the simple matter of energy and moisture. Some parts of the world may even get fewer. However, studies have shown that higher latitudes, particularly in the northern hemisphere, will see the biggest increases in favourable conditions for storms to form, and those that do may be more violent. According to the UK's Met Office, "while we may not see more thunderstorms overall, the ones we do experience could be more impactful".

Even though aircraft are designed to cope with severe conditions, storms can produce intense turbulence, capable of damaging the structural frame of the plane, as well as throwing people around the cabin if they are not strapped in. Hail can also be a big problem, potentially cracking windscreens or causing engine problems. So, however much inconvenience it may cause passengers, crews will invariably put safety first.

Onboard weather radars can help aircrew to see up to 320 nautical miles ahead. They also have access to live weather forecasting resources online. The cost of running a High Street business has also risen; employment costs are higher, and there's the burden of business rates.

Firms need customers. Footfall on many High Streets has been in long-term decline as shopping and services move online, and this enormous structural shift has yet to play out fully.

Two-thirds of town centre experts think there is still 20% to 40% too much retail space, according to a survey by retail body Revo and real estate consultancy Lambert Smith Hampton.

In Aberdeen's boom years of North Sea oil, from the late 1970s through to the early 2010s, it didn't have to work hard to fill its hotels and restaurants and get the cash tills ringing because of the wealth the energy industry brought to the city.

I remember weekends with friends as a teenager, spending whatever cash we had in the fashion chains and shoe shops. Later came the pubs and bar-hopping, and trips to the art deco Capitol Theatre (now office space), where I saw Duran Duran.

But by the end of 2022, Union Street had hit rock bottom. Aberdonians had long deserted the High Street for a shopping centre, which opened in 2009 next to the railway station.

"They'd fallen out of love with their city," says Bob Keiller, head of Our Union Street coalition, a community-led group formed after an emergency summit to reverse the decline.

The first thing it did was listen to what residents wanted, receiving thousands of submissions. Restoring a bit of civic pride and filling empty units became the focus.

It's been a co-ordinated effort, involving letting agents, the council, landlords, businesses and a big team of volunteers, led by Keiller, a former FTSE 100 CEO, who isn't getting paid. He has a map on his office wall, detailing the status of every property, unit by unit, with colour-coded Post-it notes.

They audited the street, tracking down absent landlords, and logging what was empty and why. Volunteers offered to give the empty properties a clean and a lick of paint, to make them more attractive to let. They also spruced up the streets, picking litter, weeding, and painting bins. A small team still goes out once a fortnight.

There's a website where all the available properties are collated; crucially, all of the financial incentives are also listed. These can include council fit-out grants (where a new business gets money to decorate premises), and a two-year discount from business rates.

In 2023, 57 of the 198 units on Union Street were empty. As of today, 33 have been filled, with a further 14 in the process of being let.

But it can feel like a game of whack-a-mole because since the initiative started, another 20 units have become vacant. "The key thing is, we're filling quicker than emptying," Keiller insists.

Keiller's operations director, David McKeith, describes it as putting an arm round the shoulder of those they can help and being a "wasp in the ear" of people who just say no.

At one end of the office, a small, young team produces social media content, linking visitors and residents to a new Our Aberdeen app, showing what the city can offer.

For Keiller, the "small wins" - like tackling graffiti - are needed to help change public perception.

"We've realised the psychology is not to do it once but be prepared to do it two or three times before people that do these things eventually say, 'there's no point in putting it there because somebody's going to paint over it.'"

Attention is also turning to the often-forgotten upper floors of buildings along the once-iconic Union Street. In a separate initiative run by the business group Aberdeen Inspired, experts are getting inside those vacant high-up spaces to identify the barriers to bringing them back into use.

Aberdeen sought the help of a High Street troubleshooter, Iain Nicholson, who's worked with dozens of towns and cities through his Vacant Shops Academy.

Out of more than 1,000 empty units he helped audit nationally, on average more than half didn't have a letting agent actively marketing the property. He says some councils don't even know who the landlords are.

Empty shops, he says, won't all be filled by the market alone. Towns and cities need to encourage a broader range of occupiers, from arts and culture to leisure, health and well-being.

He says what's striking about Aberdeen is its joined-up approach, and the fact people "rolled up their sleeves" and overcame differences. "That's not something you can yet say about everywhere."

Every town and city faces its own mix of challenges.

Nicholson also helped Crewe in Cheshire. As a smaller place than Aberdeen, it is focusing on arts, culture, and community, with a new women's centre opened this month.

And English councils are being encouraged to take a more interventionist role.

They can now force an empty commercial unit that's been vacant for at least a year to be offered to tenants through a High Street Rental Auction, even if the landlord doesn't want to let it.

It's early days but the mere prospect of losing control of a property is delivering some results. Bassetlaw Council in Nottinghamshire, an early adopter of the scheme, says more than 30 shops across the district have been let or are currently under offer as a result of the policy.

But filling empty units is only part of the challenge. The bigger question is what town and city centres are actually for.

Changing habits mean we won't need so many shops. Instead, it's argued, town centres will need more people living and working in them, with new homes and office space alongside services that can't be done online. With an ageing population, for example, bringing NHS health centres into the heart of a town could be a big opportunity.

In Aberdeen, the building that used to house BHS - a department store shuttered in 2016 - is being converted into an entrance plaza for the new food and drink market.

The physical appearance of Union Street is changing too. Like many Victorian shopping thoroughfares, it was built with a wide road (probably to give space to horses and carriages). Now parts of the pavements are being widened to make it a more pleasant place to spend time, though many Aberdonians are weary of the disruption, and of new traffic restrictions.

Money is a big barrier. Aberdeen's new indoor food and drink market is costing Â£40m, funded by the council and the UK Levelling Up Fund. In other places, regeneration schemes can struggle because the cost of redevelopment outweighs the likely financial return, making support from the public sector essential. And the economics are becoming more challenging.

"Higher interest rates, rising costs of construction and other issues are impacting the viability and delivery of new housing and big redevelopment projects," says Steve Norris, a director at Lambert Smith Hampton. He says that big projects usually require cash from both the public and private sectors.

He wants to see "more creative ways of funding".

Part of Burnham's plan is to give local leaders more freedom to invest in town centres and drive regeneration, building on the approach he has championed in Greater Manchester. But bigger projects take years.

Making it easier and cheaper to open and run a High Street business is one of the more immediate solutions being called for, with business rates a particular concern.

Burnham has promised further rates reform. All eyes are on this month's Budget to see how far he will go. All eyes are on this month's Budget to see how far he will go in England. Business rates are devolved in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The chief executive of Next, Simon Wolfson, argues that the bigger challenge is not business rates, but planning reform.

Back in Aberdeen, there are some signs of traction.

Lauren Reid and her mother Rose moved their fashion boutique to Union Street in 2024. A council grant helped with the refurbishment after they decided to make the leap.

Can they make the sums add up? "We're getting there," she says.

She feels the same about the future of her street. "I always say Rome wasn't built in a day."

Right now the area feels like a construction site - but another gaping hole will soon be filled. Robert Keane is a local property developer who in 2023 bought the Trinity shopping centre, once home to Debenhams. The space is now set to be re-occupied by a national leisure brand. A notoriously tiring stairway leading to the Trinity from the train station is also being replaced by an escalator.

"We saw a real good opportunity, because it was dying," he says.

He reckons he can make a go of it, cutting the cost of parking and overheads, resetting rents and being more nimble than previous far-flung owners.

It was a bold move given Lambert Smith Hampton suggests that more than one in 10 UK shopping centres is ripe for demolition.

But he's betting the dynamics of Aberdeen are finally changing.

"I think by 2027 it should look and feel like a different city."

That is certainly the hope. Yet walking along Union Street today, past the boarded-up shopfronts and roadworks, it can still feel like a city caught between its past and whatever comes next.

Huang, who co-founded Nvidia and led it for 33 years, resigned on Thursday after two separate extramarital affairs — one with an alleged Chinese spy and the other with an employee — were made public. The board named finance chief Colette Kress as interim CEO while it conducts a search for a full-time successor.

Nvidia shares fell 13% this week, the most since April 2025. News of the first reported affair hit the front pages over the weekend, when Huang sent a letter to employees apologizing for being "unfaithful to my wife." The board convened late Wednesday after The New York Times reported on the other affair, this one with the company's vice president of health care.

By early Thursday morning, Huang was out, capping a stunning fall for the Nvidia co-founder, who had emerged as one of President Donald Trump's top allies in the corporate world.

"With the unfortunate departure of CEO Jensen Huang we expect a challenging transition for NVIDIA over the next few months," Gil Luria, an analyst at D.A. Davidson, wrote in a report on Thursday. Luria lowered his price target to $200 from $300, but maintained his buy rating. "We believe the market has appropriately adjusted for this transition," he wrote.

Last week, prior to Huang's departure, Nvidia shares dropped about 9%. Much of the decline came after the midterm elections, which resulted in sweeping victories for Democrats and the passage of dozens of ballot measures that could slow the artificial intelligence buildout.

In the weeks before the election, Nvidia's stock was sliding alongside the rest of the AI trade on concerns that rising interest rates would punish debt-heavy companies. While Nvidia is wildly profitable, big customers like neoclouds are reliant on the debt markets.

Heading into next week, the pressure is on Kress. Nvidia is scheduled to report quarterly results on Tuesday. Analysts are projecting 91% year-over-year revenue growth to $108.9 billion, according to LSEG. That's up 18-fold from the same quarter in 2022, just before OpenAI launched ChatGPT and kicked off the generative AI era.

Analysts at Oppenheimer wrote in a report on Thursday that, even though they see the company reporting solid earnings, "we expect fundamentals to take a backseat near term."

"The departure of Mr. Huang could, in our view, translate to a lingering share overhang," they wrote.

#260. Goldman CEO Solomon says AI financing remains strong despite market jitters

Goldman Sachs CEO David Solomon told CNBC on Monday that financing demand for artificial intelligence infrastructure remains strong despite recent market jitters as investors reassess the risks involving the massive build-out.

Solomon said that his firm has been "very active" in AI capital markets deals over recent weeks, a setup that he expects to continue.

"There's still very strong demand for investments in the AI infrastructure build and the technology around the growth of AI," Solomon told CNBC's Leslie Picker.

Changes in the political environment are among factors that will "create recalibrations and ups and downs, and we're seeing some of that," Solomon said. "But I still deeply believe in the long-term trajectory of the power of AI in our economy to drive productivity."

AI-related stocks, including chipmakers and neocloud providers, have tumbled in recent weeks as investors reassess whether the hundreds of billions of dollars being poured into AI infrastructure will generate good returns.

The selloff, which began before the U.S. midterm elections, intensified after Democrats scored sweeping victories on Tuesday, raising concerns that political opposition could slow the industry's expansion.

The shifting sentiment comes at a key moment for AI model lab Anthropic, which is preparing for a potential IPO that could become one of the biggest tests yet of public-market appetite for AI companies. Goldman is among the banks leading that IPO process.

"We'll see, obviously, if this IPO moves forward, how it all plays out," Solomon said, declining to comment in detail about the IPO. "I would say that the market environment is still pretty constructive."

Solomon also pushed back against political resistance to data center construction, arguing that the infrastructure is essential to the U.S. economy and that it can be built responsibly.

"We certainly don't want this infrastructure going to places outside the U.S., which is what will naturally happen because the demand for compute is there," Solomon said.
