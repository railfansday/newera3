#444. The AI graveyard: a running list of projects and startups that didn’t make it

Aside from Character.ai and Ollie, plenty of AI bets never even survive inside the companies that build them. According to S&P Global Market Intelligence, about 42% of AI initiatives are ultimately abandoned by their corporate parents. The reasons vary: insufficient funding, technical challenges, competition, difficulty scaling, or just weak user demand.

The result is a growing AI graveyard. Below is a look at some of the most notable AI products, startups, launches, and bets that have shut down, pivoted, or significantly missed expectations. Think of it less as a scorecard of flops than as a record of what these attempts can (hopefully) teach the rest of the industry.

Even the biggest players aren’t immune. OpenAI, one of the most valuable private companies in the world, has had its own share of casualties and missteps. One of the more recent examples was its attempt to turn ChatGPT into a broader “super app.” On July 9, OpenAI updated the app to combine several experiences, including separate “Chat,” “Codex,” and “Work” modes, while renaming the traditional, familiar version “ChatGPT Classic.”

The redesign quickly drew criticism from users who found the new interface confusing and cluttered, and OpenAI rolled back the change soon after, bringing the familiar ChatGPT interface back.

That wasn’t OpenAI’s only attempt to pull everything under one roof. The company has also shut down several stand-alone apps and folded their features directly into ChatGPT — a strategy that’s worked out better than the redesign did. ChatGPT Atlas, a stand-alone AI-powered web browser, lasted less than a year before being discontinued on August 9, with its most useful features absorbed into ChatGPT.

The company did the same thing with Operator, an AI agent that could browse the web and complete tasks on a user’s behalf, and OpenAI has increasingly built image generation directly into ChatGPT, shrinking the role of DALL-E as its own separate destination for creating images.

We also can’t forget its video-sharing platform, Sora, which struggled with high operating costs and user-retention challenges and shut down in March 2026.

When Apple unveiled Apple Intelligence in 2024, an improved Siri was one of the highlighted features. The pitch promised a Siri that could understand context, know what was happening across apps, and get tasks done effectively.

However, Apple repeatedly pushed back the release of the new Siri, citing various reasons like engineering issues and bugs. This delay eventually contributed to a $250 million settlement over claims regarding how Apple marketed the AI capabilities of the iPhone 16.

Siri AI finally appeared in the iOS 27 beta this past July, with the new AI-powered version rolling out to English-language users first this month; support for other languages is expected to follow.

Founded in 2021 by former Google researchers Noam Shazeer and Daniel De Freitas, Character.ai was a consumer AI app that let people chat with customizable bots, many of them modeled on celebrities and fictional characters. It raised $150 million at a $1 billion valuation in 2023. Then, the next year, Google paid a reported $2.7 billion to license its technology and bring its founders back into its orbit, leaving the startup to carry on without them.

Character.ai has run into a lot of trouble over the years. Families have sued, alleging the chatbots contributed to teen suicides and self-harm, and Disney sent a cease-and-desist letter over unauthorized use of its characters. Character.ai barred under-18 users from open-ended chats late last year and agreed to settle several of the suits, along with Google, in early 2026.

But it wasn’t enough. The company filed for bankruptcy in early October, citing legal, regulatory, and operational headwinds. In an ironic twist, Disney is now the opening bidder, offering $20 million for a license to some of its intellectual property. The app is still running during the process, and the sale is expected to wrap up by late November or early December.

Relay, an AI-powered workflow automation tool built as an alternative to Zapier, shut down entirely in August 2026.

The product let users automate email and task workflows using AI agents, but as OpenAI, Google, and other larger platforms built similar automation directly into their own tools, five-year-old Relay struggled to hold on to a reason to exist as a stand-alone product.

Similar to the other family-focused agents, the idea behind Ollie was that families already have the information they need, but it lives across school messages, calendars, task lists, and group chats. Ollie aimed to bring those pieces together and keep them in sync. It kept track of calendars, emails, reminders, tasks, and other household details, then delivered relevant information to users through text.

However, the app faced a very common challenge: keeping up with heavyweights like Muse and Instinct. Co-founder and CEO Bill Lennon revealed the app would shut down on November 6, citing difficulties like being unable to stand out, failing to grow, and not raising enough capital.

Announced at its 2024 Build developer conference, Microsoft’s Recall feature was pitched as an AI-powered “photographic memory” for Windows PCs, periodically taking screenshots of what a user was doing so they could later search back through their own digital history.

Even after a redesign, the controversy hasn’t gone away. A cybersecurity researcher recently built a tool that could extract and display data that Recall had captured, reviving questions about whether Microsoft has actually fixed the underlying problem.

Notion Mail launched in April 2025 as an AI-focused email product designed to help users organize and automate their inboxes. However, the company noticed its users were using separate AI agents to handle their email inboxes instead. As a result, Notion Mail is scheduled to shut down on September 22.

The Humane AI Pin is probably one of the most well-known failures in AI hardware. Designed to provide AI features through a wearable device rather than a traditional smartphone, Humane raised $230 million from investors and garnered significant attention.

But the product struggled mightily with performance, and the situation worsened when Humane warned customers to stop using its charging case because of a potential battery fire risk. Humane then shut down its AI Pin business in February 2025, with most of the company’s assets acquired by HP for $116 million.

The Rabbit R1 was another heavily hyped AI device. Unveiled at CES in January 2024, the AI companion was designed to perform tasks on behalf of its owner. Rabbit said it had sold 100,000 units shortly after launch.

However, strong initial sales didn’t mean it was a strong product. Early reviews described the device as unfinished, with unreliable performance and a limited number of useful integrations.

The company hasn’t given up, though. It has continued updating the R1, positioning it as a computer controller capable of performing agentic tasks. It also recently announced a new hardware project, dubbed “Project Cyberdeck,” aimed at creating a portable device built for vibe-coding.

Huxe was an AI audio app, built by former developers of Google’s NotebookLM, that turned written information into conversational, podcast-style audio.

The company shut down in May 2026, partly as larger platforms began offering similar experiences to large existing audiences. For instance, Spotify has been expanding its own AI-powered audio tools.

Yupp was an interesting AI product: a free playground that allowed users to compare responses from hundreds of AI models side by side. Users could also vote on responses and earn cryptocurrency through the platform.

At its peak, the platform let users test more than 800 models, including from companies such as OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic. But according to its founders, Yupp never achieved strong enough product-market fit to survive. The platform shut down in March 2026.

Figgs AI operated from 2023 to 2024, letting users create and interact with customizable AI characters for role-play and storytelling. The platform reportedly drew more than 1 million users, but its developers said keeping the service free became too expensive to sustain, and they shut it down.

#442. Transportation secretary blames ‘routine’ equipment upgrade for grounding flights nationwide

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said Saturday that a nationwide ground stop halting flights across the United States was caused by seemingly routine maintenance work.

Duffy said that during “routine maintenance,” copper wire at a remote location was upgraded to fiber optic cable by L3Harris, a longtime contractor for the Federal Aviation Administration. After the upgrade, a router overloaded a critical communication system, leading to the ground stop.

“It’s pretty straightforward work that we were doing yesterday at, again, a very remote location,” Duffy said during a Saturday press conference that appeared to have technical issues of its own. “What we’re working to determine was whether we had incompetence — meaning, was it user error or [installer] error from L3Harris? Or did they install a faulty router?”

The FAA’s acting CTO Rebecca Guy added, “When we started to see the occurrence where our network was having instability, we started to look at what was the cause. And as such, we saw two places that were actually pinging our network at a very high rate — a thousand connections over every four to five seconds.”

Guy said the FAA disabled “both of those, right away and were able to stabilize our network,” and is now performing a root cause analysis.

Duffy has repeatedly insisted, at multiple press conferences, that Congress needs to provide more funding for aviation equipment upgrades. Asked why the upgrades were made at 3pm on a Friday afternoon — rather than, say, at midnight on a Saturday — he responded that while “switchovers” at bigger airports will take place late at night, remote switchovers happen during the day.

“There will be reevaluation of when we do this work,” he said.

This was only the third nationwide ground stop of the 21st century. More than 8,200 flights were canceled on Friday evening, according to aviation data firm Cirium. And the effects continued after the ground stop was lifted, with at least another 3,000 flights canceled on Saturday.

The ground stop came just a day after the FAA announced the rollout of an AI tool for air traffic controllers; the air traffic controllers union said it was not consulted in designing the system. However, FAA administrator Bryan Bedford said the agency sees “zero evidence of anything nefarious and zero evidence of anything AI-related.”

“We want to be clear that we have an equipment issue, and we’re working through what this defective piece of equipment was,” Bedford said.

When Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin was asked earlier if the ground stop was caused by AI, he replied, “We call it SI [super intelligence] around here, remember? SI.”

#428. Where was Nvidia’s security team?

Outsiders had little reason to notice when Nvidia’s longtime chief security officer and head of product security stepped down in August. Just two and a half months later, CEO Jensen Huang himself left the company over growing questions about his extramarital relationships — starting with an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore data center company that U.S. investigators suspect of funneling Nvidia chips to China, then widening to reports about two separate affairs with Nvidia employees.

At the time, David Reber, who spent four years as CSO and seven at Nvidia overall, said he was leaving for health reasons. “This wasn’t a pivot I had planned. But I know what needs to come first: family and health,” he wrote on LinkedIn in late August, disclosing that he’d been diagnosed with young-onset Parkinson’s more than two years ago.

Reber’s replacement, Saša Zdjelar, previously spent 17 years at ExxonMobil in Houston, leaving as a senior IT manager before spending one year at Salesforce and several years at the cybersecurity company ReversingLabs. Zdjelar didn’t respond to questions Thursday about the transition or what the security team knew.

Nothing public connects Reber’s or Zdjelar’s role changes to the Megaspeed matter, but it seems fair to wonder what Nvidia’s security team knew about the government’s scrutiny of Megaspeed and about Huang’s relationships, and whether anyone raised concerns. Reber didn’t respond to questions Thursday. Nvidia also has not answered our related questions.

Relationships between a CEO and his employees typically fall to HR, legal and the board, not security. But security specialists say any affair — even one inside the office — can give a foreign intelligence service leverage over an exec who wants it kept quiet.

Still, spotting the clues isn’t easy. The way intelligence services collect information, much of it through everyday conversation rather than hacking, makes it especially difficult for security teams to identify risks, according to retired senior intelligence officer Nicholas Eftimiades, who says he has trained more than 1,000 corporate security professionals.

Some people inside Nvidia clearly knew parts of the picture. Nvidia’s top lawyer was engaged with the government’s Megaspeed inquiry more than a year ago, The New York Times reported earlier this week. (Nvidia has said it regularly contacts officials.) Reports about Huang’s relationships with employees suggest colleagues also knew of them, even if the behavior is not expressly forbidden by Nvidia’s code of conduct. The Times cited five Nvidia employees who said he was involved with a vice president who reported directly to him; Reuters, citing two people, later reported a second relationship. What’s unclear is whether any of that reached security.

Even if it had, Eftimiades, who wrote Chinese Espionage Operations and Tactics in 2020, told TechCrunch that a security team’s resources are often limited. For one thing, they’re often underfunded because, unlike some other cost centers, they don’t drive revenue. It’s astonishingly common for CEOs to answer a security team’s warnings with “not a nickel more than compliance,” he said.

Many companies also define security overly narrowly, specialists say. In an opinion piece in The Hill, Don Aviv, CEO of the corporate investigations firm Interfor International, and author Jeremy Hurewitz, wrote, “Too many companies still approach security primarily as a matter of protecting facilities, preventing cyberattacks, and responding to physical threats.” The two argued that “[s]ecurity teams need to pay greater attention to insider threats, including the cultivation of executives and employees by foreign intelligence services, suspicious business relationships, conflicts of interest and attempts to obtain sensitive information through trusted personal contacts.”

It would be surprising if Nvidia’s security team didn’t have a more expansive remit than most companies given its scale and business partners, but it’s possible.

In fairness, some of it is simply beyond what any team can do. Speaking generally, not about Huang, Eftimiades offered a hypothetical in which a CEO complains that he can’t do something because “I’m fighting a board who doesn’t want to do this.” That tells an intelligence asset who the obstacle is. The service then asks, “Who’s the sticking point here?” and looks for ways into that person’s email and phone.

What a rival intelligence service wants is often less technology than policy insight, including who’s pushing to change export rules and who the biggest blocker is. “Intel world tends to be like cancer,” he said. “Once it gets into the body, it looks to spread and looks to reach out.”

The playbook isn’t new, Eftimiades added, “particularly for China.” Any executive dealing with the country “will tell you that they’ve been approached.” He said there are also “tons of diplomat stories” of U.S. diplomats walking into a hotel room to find a woman on the bed. He said what’s newer compared with Soviet era spying is the relationships these services cultivate now often last years, not weeks.

Technology can augment that human intelligence, Eftimiades said, pointing to IMSI catchers as one example. The devices mimic a cell tower so that nearby phones connect to them. A basic version can cost about $1,000 and fit in a suitcase, he said, though government-grade models cost far more, and there were a couple of cases involving Chinese espionage where these factored in last year.

Eftimiades says no one knows yet if the devices were used against Huang, whose phone was presumably hardened such that compromises could be detected, if not stopped altogether. But what Nvidia’s security team knew, and whether it could have acted on it, will only become clearer as the board’s independent investigation and federal inquiries proceed.

In the meantime, Eftimiades said he hopes the whole episode is “a wake-up call” for U.S. industry, which he said is mostly “clueless” about China’s tactics. “We have lost trillions [of dollars] in intellectual property,” is his guess. “Whatever we think we’re doing isn’t working.”

#417. Flaming Hydra’s new Kickstarter campaign rethinks e-book ownership and digital lending

Here’s a big promise: A new Kickstarter campaign aims to usher in the “return of the human internet.”

That’s the tagline of a crowdfunding campaign for The Phoenix Project, an initiative from the online culture and commentary magazine Flaming Hydra. If you back The Phoenix Project, you’re supporting three things — a one-off print magazine designed to celebrate “the values and fun of the early internet,” a new e-book service for libraries, and a newsletter subscription/archiving program that’s also designed for libraries.

To Flaming Hydra’s lead editor Maria Bustillos, these disparate projects are “basically all one thing,” tied together by the goal of “protecting freedom of information” and fulfilling the “promise of the early internet, where you’re making information available to as many people as possible, while still protecting the right of artists and authors to make a living.”

Bustillos told me that these issues have been highlighted on Flaming Hydra itself, for example when Tal Levin wrote about finding countless AI-generated clones of his book on Amazon. By “creating an ecosystem that is based in people working together, who are offering their stuff in a context that is controlled and doesn’t allow” others to create and sell AI-generated clones, Bustillos said Flaming Hydra is “part of the human project.”

“AI is not this unstoppable force,” she added. “If people just set up publishing pipelines and networks and organizations that are dedicated to the human internet, people who want to read stuff can go to them, and it’s actually completely isolated from mechanistic interference.”

The Phoenix Project fits into this vision by making the work of human writers more accessible to libraries.

Back in 2020, Bustillos wrote about the e-book systems dominating libraries and schools. In one example, instead of just buying a book, a school had to pay $27 per student per year for continued access to copies of “The Diary of Anne Frank.”

This is symptomatic of a broader system that treats true ownership of e-books and other digital media as a thing of the past. Instead, we “license” these e-books from their publishers, and they can be altered or removed at any time.

“E-books are books,” Bustillos said. “They’re not some other thing. They are books, and people should be able to own them, and libraries should be able to own them, and media should be able to be owned. All digital media should be able to be owned.”

In her view, ownership boils down to a simple question: “If you are at the mercy of somebody who can shut the book off, you do not own it.”

So even beyond the question of ongoing payments, there have also been examples of e-books being altered or removed from readers’ devices long after their purchase. Bustillos acknowledged that for individual pieces of media, this distinction may not seem like a big deal, “but if it’s all the textbooks for everybody, if it’s all the books,” then it’s a real problem.

Flaming Hydra and The Phoenix Project seek to solve this by letting libraries truly purchase and own e-books. They can then lend those e-books to their patrons through a new, open source “library in a box” system called Lenny for Libraries.

The Phoenix Project is starting by offering five new e-books from Flaming Hydra writers, including collections of writing from The Awl, and of essays by Tom Scocca and Bustillos herself. That might sound like a relatively small start, but Bustillos said she’s talking to library consortiums and author groups, and the goal here is to “change the conversation” around e-books and digital archiving and lending. So The Phoenix Project could be paving the way for other initiatives that follow a similar model.

Similarly, The Phoenix Foundry Archive is designed to make paywalled newsletter content available to libraries. Bustillos noted that “so much of the best journalism, criticism, essays” is now being published in paywalled newsletters that are inaccessible to libraries (as well as the Internet Archive).

Flaming Hydra has been working on all this even before launching the Kickstarter, and Bustillos said she was grateful for the financial support from backers like Anand Raganathan, Brewster Kahle, and Paul Ford. But by turning to crowdfunding, Bustillos said Flaming Hydra can better protect the work, ensuring that it remains sustainable and independent.

“We don’t have to be at the mercy of anybody, and it also protects editorial independence,” she said. “Those things go hand in hand.”

Flaming Hydra seeks to raise $30,000 for The Phoenix Project, with the campaign ending on November 27.

The project is separate from Bharat Semi’s image-sensor business, Kapoor added. The startup plans to remain fabless, outsourcing chip manufacturing while focusing on semiconductor design and software. The new funding will not be used to finance the proposed fabrication facility.

#392. Freshworks alums raise $53M to take on Salesforce, HubSpot with AI-native CRM

Dreamteam, a startup founded by former Freshworks executives, has raised $53 million to challenge Salesforce and HubSpot with its AI-native CRM, betting that AI can finally shake up a market where established players have so far proved difficult to dislodge.

The funding comprises seed and Series A rounds, led by Together Fund and Accel respectively, with participation from Premji Invest. The San Francisco-based startup declined to disclose the size of each round or its valuation.

Traditional CRMs depend on salespeople to manually record customer interactions and keep deal information up to date. That often leaves businesses with incomplete or outdated information. Dreamteam aims to eliminate much of that work by automatically capturing context from sources including emails and customer conversations. It also uses AI agents to handle tasks such as follow-ups, meeting preparation, and pipeline monitoring.

Dreamteam co-founder and CEO Anand Venkatraman (pictured above, right) told TechCrunch that he had repeatedly encountered problems with traditional CRM platforms over more than 25 years in enterprise software, including at Salesforce and Freshworks. Salespeople spent considerable time updating records, yet managers still struggled to get a reliable picture of their business. “In all these years, I have never come across an instance where I could click a button and get a forecast from the CRM,” he said.

That experience led Venkatraman to co-found Dreamteam in 2025 with Senthil Kanthaswamy (pictured above, center), who spent more than nine years at Freshworks, most recently as a product leader at Freshservice, and Ramesh Parthasarathy (pictured above, left), who previously worked on AI and platform engineering at Freshworks.

At the heart of Dreamteam’s CRM is what it calls the Company Brain. This acts as virtual memory, pulling together information from emails, meetings, and other customer interactions to build a shared record of customer relationships. Four built-in AI agents use that information to research prospects, draft follow-up emails, flag potential risks to deals, and configure CRM workflows, with sales teams retaining control over what gets sent to customers.

Dreamteam has signed up more than 100 customers across 13 countries in the three months since it began actively selling its product, with most paying for the service, Venkatraman said. The startup has also replaced established CRM platforms at several businesses, including Salesforce at healthcare company DMS Health and HubSpot at other customers.

The advertising sales team at media streaming company Plex also chose Dreamteam over HubSpot and other AI-native CRM alternatives, Venkatraman told TechCrunch. Plex’s co-founder and chief product officer Scott Olechowski said Dreamteam’s ability to integrate with AI tools already used across the company was a “game changer.”

Replacing established CRM platforms at scale may prove more difficult. In a blog post earlier this year, venture capital firm Andreessen Horowitz (a16z) argued that while AI could transform how businesses manage customer relationships, existing CRMs are likely to remain the systems where customer records are stored, with a new generation of AI-powered tools capturing much of the value by working on top of them.

Venkatraman acknowledged that Salesforce and HubSpot are unlikely to disappear anytime soon, but noted that their entrenched positions do not guarantee they will dominate the next generation of CRM software.

“We see this as an electric-car moment for systems of record,” he said, adding that AI requires a fundamental rethink of how CRMs are built — not simply adding new features to existing platforms.

Kanthaswamy believes startups have a window of two to five years to gain ground before established CRM providers can fully adapt to AI. Rebuilding software around a fundamentally different architecture takes time, he stated, giving newer companies an opportunity to attract customers while incumbents work to overhaul their existing products.

Dreamteam also plans to expand beyond sales into other parts of the customer lifecycle, including onboarding, customer support, and upselling. The startup already has a feature called Magic Apps that allows users to create custom applications and dashboards within the CRM using natural-language instructions, without writing code.

Currently, Dreamteam employs more than 35 people, with its engineering team based in Chennai and its go-to-market operations in the United States. Kanthaswamy said the startup aims to reach eight figures in annual revenue by the end of 2027, though he declined to disclose the startup’s current revenue.

#389. Small retailers brace for 'huge hit' this holiday season as shipping costs spike

When Jenni Shields began her small business eight years ago selling handmade hair accessories, she paid $2.93 to ship an order, or close to $4 in today's dollars when adjusted for inflation.

Last year, Shields said, an average shipping label for her products cost around $4.50. But when she took a typical batch of orders to her local U.S. Postal Service office last month in Kansas City, Kansas, she was shocked to see that it was an average of nearly $6 per package.

Shields is just one of the small- and medium-sized retailers who are struggling to keep up with rising shipping costs as the price of fuel continues to trend upward.

Small business owners such as Shields are facing a difficult holiday season. Customer demand is strong, but some retailers said their profits will be dented — if not eaten up entirely — by higher shipping and other costs.

Shields said she charges her customers $6 in shipping fees, which includes roughly $1.50 to cover her packaging supplies. The dramatic upswing this year means she's no longer breaking even on her orders and instead eating the added costs, she said.

"I am losing money at this point, and I've really taken a huge hit this year," said Shields, the owner of Evie Pearl Handmade, which sells bows, quilted headbands and other accessories. "I feel like it's just been this slow increase up [in shipping costs], and I feel horrible."

The surge in fuel prices since the U.S. and Israel attacked Iran in February has driven up the cost of shipping by sea, air, rail and road.

That means higher costs across the board, including for shipping giant Amazon, which reported an 11% increase in shipping expenses in the third quarter compared with last year, to individual retailers.

Globally, air cargo is going for about $3.37 per kilogram, up 43% from a similar period a year ago, according to transportation marketplace Freightos. Judah Levine, Freightos' head of research, said in addition to higher fuel, demand for fast shipping of hardware used to build data centers is helping to boost air freight rates, even weeks before peak last-minute holiday packages take to the skies.

For businesses, that runup is going to "squeeze their margins in the near-term," said Jason Seidl, managing director and transportation analyst at TD Cowen. "Larger companies tend not to be exposed to the spot market, and they'll have what we call dedicated deals in place."

But that doesn't help smaller companies, which are walking a delicate line of not wanting to alienate customers who have enjoyed free shipping for nearly a generation.

Todd Shelton, founder of the eponymous 23-year-old New Jersey clothing company that makes high-end men's jeans, T-shirts and button-up shirts locally, said he started charging customers for return shipping this week for the first time after absorbing the higher expenses this year. The shipping is deducted from customers' refunds.

Shelton said his costs have been rising for materials, too, such as his imported Japanese denim and shirt fabric from Portugal. The company is still profitable, but the higher costs have eaten into profit margins as they stay elevated, he's considering raising prices or charging for shipping to customers, he said.

Shields said she's considering raising her shipping fees for the first time in six years to keep up with the increases.

The holiday season is one of Shields' busiest. But she said she's seen a slowdown in sales because of shipping fees.

"I can't compete with Amazon on the free shipping. I can't do that. I can't afford that," she said. "I'm seeing so much of my percentage that's supposed to come back to me and my family being put towards the shipping costs."

With no sign of fuel prices declining significantly, Shields, who runs her business out of her spare bedroom at home, said she's not sure that small businesses will be able to emerge on the other side of the holidays.

Shippers including UPS, FedEx and USPS have all instituted holiday surcharges, with prices varying by package type and lasting through the end of the season. Though that's typical during the peak holiday period each year, those charges have continued to rise over the past few years.

But even as costs everywhere climb, consumers are still spending.

According to the National Retail Federation, retail sales through the end of the year are expected to grow 4.2%, translating to total spending of $1.01 trillion. That's compared with last year's holiday sales totaling $972 billion, which was 3.1% growth.

Some of that growth is from inflation, meaning consumers are simply paying more for the same goods.

"Customers all expect to spend more this holiday, but part of why they expect to spend more is they expect things to cost more, not because they'd like to spend more or they were hoping to get more quantity of stuff," said Aaron Cheris, the global retail practice head at Bain & Company. "So what I suspect happens is that non-discretionary categories do fine, because you buy the food you were going to need to buy anyway, but then the amount of cash that's left over for [discretionary] goods is less."

Still, Cheris said it's shaping up to be a record year for shipping costs, which is putting immense pressure on smaller retailers. That means many retailers may find themselves in financial distress in the first quarter of next year as they struggle to make it through the holiday season.

He added that larger retail companies will also have to reckon with rising costs, though they may not feel those effects until later quarters because they have shipping contracts that have already been locked in and have control over their own delivery fleets.

"If anything, it benefits the scale players over the little players, but it's kind of a little bit bad for everybody," Cheris said. "I think that is the challenge."

#387. Bedrock Energy raises $24M to build more AI-enabled geothermal drill rigs for enterprise buildings

Dig deep enough, and the temperature of the ground remains steady year round, a fun trivia fact that has the potential to improve efficiency and slash costs for heating and cooling across the country.

Geothermal heat pumps, which use the ground as a heat source in winter and heat sink in summer, have been around for decades, but they’re underutilized. Just a fraction of all buildings in the U.S. use them in part because it’s expensive to drill into the earth. But one startup, Bedrock Energy, says its AI-directed rigs can slash drilling times by up to 80%, significantly reducing costs.

That time savings recently helped Bedrock raise a new $24 million funding round, the startup told TechCrunch. Previously, it raised a $12 million Series A in 2025 and an $8.5 million seed round in 2023.

Bedrock has focused mostly on commercial properties to date, since they benefit from deep, accurate boreholes which maximize the use of their small footprints. But the startup has also been talking with data center developers, Joselyn Lai, co-founder and CEO of Bedrock Energy, told TechCrunch. Some might use it for cooling server racks, but others are interested in the technology’s flat load profile — deploying it on neighboring properties can open up significant capacity on the grid.

“We had someone in Michigan say, if we could free up a few megawatts, I think we might be able to operate that many months or even a year earlier,” Lai said. “A year of revenue for a data center is very meaningful.”

Bedrock says its modernized approach to shallow geothermal drilling — up to 1,0000 feet or so — sets it apart. Most geothermal heat pump drillers use rigs that are similar to those used for water wells. They work fine, but they’re crude instruments.

Instead, Bedrock uses directional drilling techniques borrowed from the oil and gas industry, guiding them using its own models of the subsurface. That combination allows them to optimize drilling and placement, the company says, helping to ensure performance and longevity. Sensors in the boreholes help to refine those models.

The startup currently operates in Utah, Colorado, New York, Alberta, and its home state of Texas, and the new round will help the company expand into New England, New Jersey, the upper Midwest, and additional states in the West, Lai said.

To power that expansion, Bedrock will add more rigs to its fleet to serve customers in those regions. Currently, it has drilled boreholes in nine sites, and it has secured payments from new customers in anticipation of drilling in the next year or two, she added.

Once up and running, a geothermal heat pump requires far less maintenance than traditional HVAC systems. “You only have to install it once and then it works for a very long time, which is very good for infrastructure from a financial perspective,” Lai said.

#384. After dodging death, battery startup Our Next Energy is now going public

It’s never been easy to run a battery startup in the U.S. There have been more failures than successes over the last 20 years, and whipsawing federal policy has caused more than a few battery companies to shut shop.

Not all of them die, though. Our Next Energy (ONE) had some close calls, but soon it will go public via SPAC, the company exclusively told TechCrunch.

ONE says it is valued at $600 million pre-money, and it expects the merger with Wen Acquisition Corp. will bring $400 million in gross proceeds assuming no redemptions. The combined company will trade on the Nasdaq under the ticker “ONEE”, and the pro forma implied equity value is $1 billion.

Over the years, ONE has raised $483 million, according to PitchBook, and at one point was valued at $1.3 billion. It has also received tens of millions of dollars in incentives tied to investments and job creation in Michigan.

ONE is the brainchild of former Ford and Apple engineer Mujeeb Ijaz, who designed a way for electric vehicles to draw power from two batteries of different chemistries. In ONE’s first setup, it paired lithium-iron-phosphate (LFP) and with a manganese-rich chemistry, which would let each chemistry play to its strengths: LFP could be used day in, day out, while the more fragile but energy-dense, manganese-rich one would work for long, infrequent road trips. An initial prototype traveled over 600 miles on a single charge.

But the EV market didn’t take off as quickly as anticipated, and automakers wanted to buy at lower prices (usually from China). Both of those reasons helped sink ONE’s first attempt at a Series C in late 2023. The startup had to lay off a chunk of its staff, and eventually vacated its lease for a planned $1.6 billion factory in Michigan.

The rough patch led to Ijaz’s ouster as CEO in December 2023, only to have him return to the position in March 2025 after a new round of funding closed. (His brother’s investment firm co-led the round.)

Part of the critique aimed at ONE during Ijaz’s first tenure as CEO was that the company spent too much time developing new products instead of focusing on one or two, as most startups tend to do. When Ijaz came back to the helm last year, he said that ONE would leave the automotive market and focus on grid-scale batteries.

ONE isn’t alone in pivoting away from vehicle batteries. American automakers, which had similarly rosy views of EV adoption, also bet big on battery factories and supply chains. When sales didn’t pop in the U.S. like they have in China or Europe, they had to find an outlet for that capacity, and the grid started looking like the obvious target once it started groaning under the weight of AI data centers. Both Ford and GM have announced their own energy storage products.

Many new grid-scale batteries use LFP, which is safer and cheaper than those used in most EVs in the U.S., and Ijaz saw an opportunity for ONE there as well. “We go back to the origins of LFP in this country and even to some of the factories that are in China,” he said. Indeed, just over a decade ago Ijaz led the automotive division at A123 Systems, the company that first commercialized the chemistry.

Soon, Ijaz saw other opportunities for ONE’s batteries, including defense and locomotives.

The defense play isn’t surprising. Nearly every battery company I talk with these days mentions defense, in part because it’s a market that Chinese companies can’t play in. Building an American supply chain means that a company can sell batteries at a premium, shielded as they are from Chinese competition. It’s a higher-margin market, though much smaller than EVs.

My recent conversation with Ijaz last week was the first time I’d heard a company pitch rail companies as potential customers, though. Ijaz said ONE is “finding a lot of traction in” that market. The company has built a hybrid locomotive that uses a 2.8 megawatt-hour battery, and another he calls a micro-hybrid, which uses a smaller battery to start the diesel engine faster, similar to a stop-start system on a fossil fuel car or truck.

“Our customers are seeing a 50% improvement in diesel fuel consumption, which is a big deal,” Ijaz said.

Currently, ONE has three business lines, which could be a sign of its old culture creeping back into the picture. But Ijaz said the addition of defense and rail were driven by the bottom line: Grid-scale battery customers are also “very much like automotive customers, where they were willing to buy from China as long as the price was low enough,” he said.

“Believe me, I would have loved to have made that grid business into the single business of the company. But the customers would look and then be taken away to a Chinese option because of the pricing, and the tariffs also leading them to that conclusion,” Ijaz said. “And so we decided that we needed a more durable market, and we looked for who in the customer base valued an American battery company.”

Today, ONE operates a pack assembly plant in Novi, Michigan, which can put together packs from cells at a rate of 3 gigawatt-hours per year. The plant uses cells from China-based and non-China-based suppliers, as well as ONE’s own cells from a smaller factory nearby, which is currently being upgraded to make 50 megawatt-hours of batteries per year.

In its investor pitch, which TechCrunch has reviewed, ONE distills its business lines into defense and rail. Drones and submarines make up the majority of what ONE sees as a $16.2 billion market. Defense-focused data centers get lumped in there, too, contributing $4.1 billion to the defense business. (That appears to be the main outlet for grid-scale batteries.) The other line, which serves the rail industry, is expected to be worth $28.2 billion.

Both markets are sizable, but they pale in comparison to the EV sector, which is worth about $73 billion today, and could grow to $200 billion by the end of the decade, according to Grand View Research. This year, ONE expects to bring in $30 million in revenue, and hopes to make double that next year. The company said it has $200 million in binding, multi-year contracts with railroads and defense contractors.

ONE hasn’t closed the door on the automotive industry, Ijaz said. “Automotive still is a race to the bottom on margins,” he said. “As time goes on, if we win an automotive contract for any purpose, we’ll take a look at it, but not at any cost.”

ONE’s pitch centers on the so-called foreign entity of concern (FEOC) restrictions: The U.S. government can’t buy batteries made in China or those made using minerals from China-controlled supply chains, so ONE will target them first.

But it’s a pitch that many battery companies are making today. Whether investors buy into ONE’s vision will depend on if they think the company has parted with its tumultuous past and found a focus. It will need both to succeed in the increasingly cutthroat battery industry.

#380. Mazama Energy has a solution for geothermal’s biggest drilling problem

Success in geothermal energy is guided by two basic goals — drill fast and drill deep. Hit both targets, and geothermal startups have a good chance at being cheaper than nearly every other source on the electrical grid.

But while the goals might be straightforward, they’re anything but easy. Drilling fast generates heat, which damages equipment, and so does drilling deep.

Those two foes are what led Mazama Energy to develop a new technology, called Carbon Chill, that uses carbon dioxide to cool the drill bit and bring cuttings to the surface. The technology will not only let the company drill deeper, but also faster. The startup gave TechCrunch an exclusive look at Carbon Chill.

“Our rate of penetration increased 20% over the already very high rates we were achieving,” Sriram Vasantharajan, co-founder and CEO of Mazama Energy, told TechCrunch.

Speed has other advantages in geothermal. Drilling represents about 25% to 30% of the total cost of building a power plant, so reducing drill time can bring costs down significantly.

If geothermal startups succeed in drilling faster and cheaper, they stand to help reshape the grid in the 2030s. Hyperscalers are already planning the facilities that will enter operation at the end of the decade.

Mazama has been working on the problem for years now. In October 2025, the Khosla-backed startup said it had drilled a well in Oregon that reached 629˚ F (331˚ C), and a more recent well has surpassed 700˚ C, Vasantharajan said. In September, the startup raised $135 million in a round led by Centaurus Capital and Doerr Capital.

Mazama hopes that its new carbon dioxide-based cooling technology will help it prove that geothermal energy from super-hot rock is a viable contender in the electricity market.

Already companies like Google and Meta have started to explore the use of geothermal energy, in part because the potential is enormous: a report from the National Laboratory of the Rockies said that geothermal could generate between 26.5 to 57 terawatts of electricity in the U.S. alone. If just 10% of the conservative estimate were developed, it would more than double the U.S.’s peak generating capacity.

Geothermal experts have identified super-hot rock — in which temperatures exceed 752˚ F (400˚ C) — as a promising way to improve the performance of individual wells. At those temperatures, water turns “supercritical,” a gaseous-like liquid state which carries five to ten times more energy than water from traditional geothermal wells. By harvesting more energy from a each well, developers have to drill fewer of them, bringing costs down.

Accessing that heat can be challenging for traditional drilling methods. Most geothermal drillers use “mud” — a combination of water and other compounds to keep the bit cool and clear cuttings from the borehole. But mud can only bring temperatures down so much. Eventually, they become so hot that electronics in the drill bit, which help keep it on course, start to fail.

High temperature-rated electronics exist, but they’d be cost prohibitive, Vasantharajan said.

Instead of mud, Mazama injects liquid carbon dioxide down the center of its drill string. As it is travels deeper, it gets heated by the rock around it, which turns it supercritical, too. Then, just before it reaches the drill head, Mazama has it flow through a valve that looks a lot like a rocket nozzle. As the gas expands, it escapes through the cone-shaped nozzle, rapidly cools, absorbing heat from the electronics and the drill bit.

“I look at [carbon dioxide cooling] as a binary,” he said. “Without such technologies, I am, today, not able to drill these horizontal wells at super hot rock temperatures.”

That capital injection will help Mazama drill more wells deeper. By next year, Vasantharajan said his company hopes to complete a single well that’s capable of generating up to 15 megawatts of electricity.

“What was just a dream or a quest, I can tell you with confidence is now a promise,” he said. “We see a path towards this commercial super-hot rock geothermal.”

#370. Cosm rides premium moviegoing wave with more immersive screenings and a wider library

The next big thing in premium large format theatrical viewing takes inspiration from the stars.

Cosm, an immersive entertainment technology company, got its start programming planetariums — its name a combination of "cosmos" and "colosseum." The company's massive, domed screens first offered guests a view of the universe, now they mimic courtside seats at sports venues around the world and drop film fans into some of Hollywood's most iconic sets.

At a time when the theatrical industry is seeing its biggest growth from the sale of premium large format, or PLF, tickets, Cosm seeks to offer a fresh take on an elevated viewing experience. And it's quickly expanding both its footprint and its content library.

Since 2024, Cosm has opened four theaters — near Los Angeles, Dallas, Atlanta and Detroit — with a Cleveland hub set to open in 2027. These experimental venues have garnered the attention of sports fans, screening live matchups in an arena-like environment. Showings often sell out, according to Cosm.

More recently, Cosm has partnered with Hollywood studios like Warner Bros., now a part of Skydance, to bring the likes of "The Matrix," "Willy Wonka & the Chocolate Factory," "Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone" and "It" to its locations. Cosm recently signed a deal with Universal, which will bring "Shrek" to its domes in December.

The agreements mark a lucrative new chapter for the privately held Cosm, expanding its audience reach and viewing opportunities beyond timed, live events.

As a privately held company, Cosm doesn't disclose its financials. Tickets cost anywhere from $18 for a matinee to around $70 for prime seats or in-demand showings. That doesn't include added fees for concessions or items from special, themed menus tied to screenings.

Sony invested $100 million in the company in June, taking a minority stake and placing Sony Pictures Entertainment Chairman and CEO Ravi Ahuja onto its board of directors.

Cosm also counts among its investors Cosm chairman Steve Winn's firm, Mirasol Capital; former Milwaukee Bucks co-owner Marc Lasry's Avenue Sports Fund; Rocket Mortgage co-founder Dan Gilbert's Rock; and David Blitzer, managing partner of the New Jersey Devils and Philadelphia 76ers, who invested through his firm Bolt Ventures.

"Cosm is really early in its growth trajectory at the moment, and we saw an opportunity to partner with the company at, what I would say is at, a very important stage in its development," Jay Levine, chief strategy and business operations officer at Sony, told CNBC. "They have about five locations with the goal of a pretty significant rollout. And the real reason why we did it was it gave us a lot of exposure to a growing distribution and what we deem an experiential platform that we think has long-term potential."

Sony has made several plays in the theatrical space in recent years. It bought Alamo Drafthouse in 2024 and entered a long-term lease to reopen and restore the iconic Cinerama Dome in Los Angeles in July of this year.

"Our experiences strategy is around tech, it's around fandom, and it's around new and innovative approaches to content distribution," Levine said. "For us, we're all about these differentiated experiences."

Cosm's 87-foot-diameter, concave screens drop viewers into the middle of the action, whether it's an NFL game or inside the world of "The Matrix." The company's forthcoming Cleveland location will have an even bigger dome, at 100 feet in diameter.

The experience is akin to that of the Sphere in Las Vegas, which has garnered headlines since opening in 2023 for hosting high-profile concerts and events.

"People absolutely love to live out what they love in fandom, just like wearing a sports jersey," Alexis Scalice, head of Cosm Studios, told CNBC. "And I love that I'm seeing that same behavior happen with cinema."

Stadium-style seating is nestled within each Cosm dome, with booths and tables set on each level. The dome's width is such that it fills much of the viewer's periphery, giving the sensation that they are part of the on-screen environment.

The Los Angeles and Dallas locations have capacity for around 350 seats each, while the newer domes in Atlanta and Detroit have up to 500 available seats. Cosm has not shared how many seats the Cleveland location will have, but with its larger screen it is expected to have a larger number of seats.

For live sports events, Cosm has specialty cameras mounted in arenas that broadcast back to the dome, creating the illusion of being on the field of play.

For theatrical features, the film is played in a large rectangle in the center of the dome and the background is transformed to extend the scene.

In Cosm's presentation of "Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone," for example, during the iconic scene where thousands of Hogwarts acceptance letters flood through the fireplace and into a living room, the background is swarmed with animated envelopes.

Cosm has partnered with experiential production company Little Cinema and creative studio MakeMake to create the visuals and environments for each scene of a film released in the dome. From storyboarding to post-production, this process can take up to a year as Cosm, the participating studio and the visual effects artists craft and render each moment.

"We really work hard not to take away from the story, so the film remains the hero in the narrative," Scalice said. "Working very closely with Warner Bros. and our creative partners to make sure that we're not detracting too much from the story, but we're enhancing those moments for more elevated emotional response."

With the exception of "It," which was released in 2017, Cosm's theatrical offerings thus far have been films that were released decades ago and that typically have long-established fan bases.

That helps Cosm leverage what Scalice called "that nostalgia factor" — "parents coming in that ... grew up on this, and they want to share it with their children."

"We can reinvigorate these legendary films," she added.

Cosm has dabbled across genres — horror, fantasy, science fiction — and also demographics — family-friendly and R-rated. "Shrek," set to debut Dec. 16, will be its first foray into animation.

"The thing that's so incredible about it is everybody thought initially it's just going to be working for sports, and when you look at what they've done, a lot of the titles with 'Harry Potter,' with 'Shrek,' and some of the other ones, I mean, the fill rates are actually incredible," Sony's Levine said.

Studios and theatrical exhibitors alike are looking for innovative ways to showcase Hollywood's product to moviegoers, especially as major shifts in viewing habits have disrupted the business.

In the post-pandemic era, the cinema space has been defined by fewer screens and fewer moviegoers, but those declines have been masked by higher movie ticket prices, particularly for PLF experiences.

These elevated offerings — think Imax, Dolby Cinema, ScreenX and 4DX — have bigger screens and better sound or introduce a unique experience, such as moving seats and fog machines. They're particularly popular with Gen Z and millennials, who've yet to be deterred by rising average ticket prices.

While an average standard movie ticket costs around $13, premium tickets average more than $18 each, according to data from EntTelligence.

Prices for Cosm's events fluctuate based on showtime and venue. Sony's Levine said the company's ticket prices and attendance rates are "higher than I think anybody really would have thought."

Unlike Cosm's sports offerings, which can also be viewed from outside the dome from its hall and deck viewing areas at a lower price, theatrical presentations are only available in the dome.

"They have no problem selling these tickets because people want to experience these things, like an immersive format," said Mike Polydoros, CEO at cinema marketing firm PaperAirplane Media. "That is something they can't get at home."

#347. Poppi founders are going after PepsiCo's Gatorade with new beverage startup Jetty

After disrupting the soda category with Poppi, founders Allison and Stephen Ellsworth are now taking aim at hydration with their new brand, Jetty.

Starting Monday, Jetty drinks and ready-to-mix electrolyte packets will be on shelves at Whole Foods grocery stores and available online at Target and Amazon.

The new, self-funded venture directly pits the Ellsworths against PepsiCo, which bought Poppi for $1.95 billion in 2025. Pepsi owns Gatorade, which dominates the U.S. sports drink category with about 60% market share, according to Numerator data.

Poppi shook up the soda category with prebiotic fiber, low calorie and sugar counts and its fun marketing aimed at Generation Z and millennials. Jetty looks to upend the hydration category through a similar strategy. To do so, it will face off not only against Gatorade but also a swarm of other upstarts looking to get in on the electrolyte-fueled trend.

For decades, Gatorade and other sports drinks have pitched their products to athletes who need electrolytes and fluids after an intense workout, although many consumers drink them every day without even sweating. Jetty is aimed at people who need a hydration boost, but not the same level of electrolytes or sugar as a traditional sports drink.

"No one has really created a product for just everyday hydration — literally everyday hydration for normal people," Allison Ellsworth, Jetty's chief brand officer, told CNBC. "Like I woke up this morning, I didn't run a marathon."

Following Poppi's sale to PepsiCo, Ellsworth stayed on as an advisor to the brand, although she said she is no longer involved in the day-to-day operations.

After 10 years building Poppi, Ellsworth said, she finally had free time. As Ellsworth tells it, she went to the doctor for the first time in a decade. She started using a GLP-1 medication and lost 60 pounds.

Ellsworth's husband, Stephen, now CEO of the new brand, noticed she wasn't hydrating, even when she came back from the gym.

GLP-1 medications such as Ozempic and Mounjaro can make people less thirsty. But because staying hydrated can also help mitigate some of the drugs' side effects, such as nausea and headaches, it can become more important.

"I physically struggle getting liquid into my body," Ellsworth said.

Plain old water is often less appealing to consumers, even dehydrated GLP-1 users, but more flavorful options with added benefits can be tempting. About 44% of GLP-1 users report consuming more functional beverages, according to EY-Parthenon's third annual survey of more than 3,600 current or former GLP-1 users.

Ellsworth sees a broader appeal for Jetty, too.

"Our core audience is going to be millennial moms and Gen Z," she said. "Gen Z just isn't relating to these old school brands, the legacy of the Gatorades and the Powerades. They're just not cool."

Consumers broadly have become more interested in staying hydrated.

Dollar sales of hydration and electrolyte products climbed 20% to $2.6 billion over the 52 weeks ended Oct. 4, based on data from SPINS, which tracks new universal product codes, or UPCs. The category also has a strong repeat buyer rate of 62%.

However, the Ellsworths aren't the only ones to notice the potential in hydration. Big food and beverage companies have been looking to hydration, too.

Kraft Heinz debuted Capri Sun juice pouches and Kool-Aid packets with electrolytes this spring. In April, Pepsi unveiled an overhaul of the Gatorade brand, focused on making it more appealing to a wider swath of consumers, with more potential occasions like a "fresh morning start" or an "afternoon walk." And Electrolit is a top performer for its distributor Keurig Dr Pepper, with 40% retail sales growth in the company's third quarter.

All of that means more competition for shelf space and consumers' "share of throat," an industry term for beverage market share.

To increase Jetty's visibility across the grocery store and to maximize convenience for shoppers, Jetty will be available in both powders and bottles.

Single-use electrolyte mixes are growing at a much faster clip than the ready-to-drink category, Ellsworth said, thanks to Unilever's Liquid I.V. and smaller upstarts like LMNT. On Amazon alone, powders are a $1 billion category, according to Ellsworth.

The ready-to-drink, or RTD, sports beverages are a much larger market, but the category's sales are shrinking as consumers reach for other hydration options instead. Unit sales of sports drinks have fallen 9.1% from 2022 to 2026, according to Numerator data. And Coca-Cola has written down the value of BodyArmor by more than $1.7 billion after buying control of the brand for $5.6 billion in late 2021.

"Me and my husband know how to do RTD," Ellsworth said.

Jetty will launch with five flavors, including watermelon, orange mango, fruit punch, black raspberry and white cherry. All of them contain 100 milligrams of sodium, 310 milligrams of potassium and 100 milligrams of magnesium in a 15.2-ounce bottle or 15-gram packet.

In addition to electrolytes, Jetty products also contain B vitamins, 3 grams of fiber and 6 grams of sugar. It doesn't use artificial sweeteners or dyes, its founders say.

For comparison, a 20-ounce bottle of traditional Gatorade Thirst Quencher has about 270 milligrams of sodium and 80 milligrams of potassium, plus 34 grams of sugar. The sports drink brand is also phasing out artificial colors and promoting lower-sugar versions.

"What's really interesting about this product and this opportunity is we now can capture all those lapsed drinkers — or that generation that didn't even ever drink it in the first place," Ellsworth said.

Poppi's new owner knows about Jetty and the market it is trying to capture, too.

"I don't think [Pepsi] gets it, but what they do get is that the movement is changing, and they think that just by taking dyes out or doing 90% less sugar, they're going to capture this audience," Ellsworth said. "But that is very big corporate thinking."

She added that she believes Pepsi has missed the mark on advertising to Gen Z consumers across its portfolio, such as in its campaigns for Sprite competitor Starry.

Perhaps as an extension of that, Poppi's performance has been underwhelming under PepsiCo's ownership.

The food and beverage giant now estimates the fair value of potential performance-based payments to Poppi's prior owners is $12 million, Pepsi said in a regulatory filing in October. At the time of the deal, Pepsi promised an additional payment of $300 million if Poppi hit certain targets by the third quarter of 2027. The Ellsworths declined to comment on the filing to CNBC.

This time around, with one successful brand under their belts, Jetty's founders say they have learned from past mistakes. Those lessons should help Jetty as it launches at a much larger scale than Poppi did.

Three different claims lawyers have signed off on Jetty's packaging, according to Ellsworth. Poppi paid $8.9 million to settle class-action litigation alleging false advertising over its "gut healthy" claims.

For now, Jetty is entirely self-funded with $2.5 million, although Ellsworth said that the brand will likely raise money in 2027. This time, though, she said investors will probably look less like traditional venture capitalists.

"I want to bring in investors that bring value," Ellsworth said. Instead of a venture capitalist who is willing to invest $15 million, Jetty would rather consider investors who can only write a check for $1 million but have access to celebrities. "So then you're getting access to talent on a cheaper level and people that are invested in the brand."

And Jetty has also learned a few lessons from Gatorade. For example, the brand is already at work on a lemon-lime version — the original flavor of Gatorade.

"We'll eventually do it," Ellsworth said. "But it was really important at the beginning just to stick to flavors that we love, for it to taste good."

"If Jacob was wearing a helmet that day, he would still be here," Carly, from Wythenshawe in Greater Manchester, says.

In the end, some 900 flights to and from both airports were delayed, some by up to 11 hours. Dozens more were cancelled. At airports across Europe, passengers expecting to fly into London also faced long waits and uncertainty.

The weather in the UK may now have cooled, and passengers may soon forget their experiences. But some within the industry believe what happened in June should be seen as a warning.

The timing in this case was clearly unlucky. But there are concerns that such incidents are only going to become more common as the effects of climate change mount up around the world and weather systems are likely to become increasingly unstable, making disruption more likely and adding to costs for airlines, airports and travellers.

Tim Atkinson, an airline risk consultant and former pilot, says the very crowded skies over the UK and northern Europe mean the region is particularly exposed.

He thinks there is a real risk, for example, that all of London's airports could be affected by storms at the same time, potentially with major consequences in the air and on the ground.

"What we're seeing now is very rapid climate change," he says.

"We're going from the kind of weather activity which takes out an airport, and which the system kind of copes withâ¦ to a situation where, with very little predictability, it would be possible to lose all of the London airports from real-time operation quite swiftly and with little notice".

Such a scenario, he thinks, would make the disruption for passengers caused by the incident in June seem relatively minor â and could even put lives at risk. But how likely is it?

For controllers at National Air Traffic Services's (NATS) main control centre in Swanwick, Hampshire, thunderstorms are a major preoccupation. When I visit, it is a hot and humid day, there are ominous dark clouds in the sky, and heavy, thundery downpours are hitting the surrounding region.

If planes are unable to land at one airport, for example due to high winds, they may need diverting to another. But that can create its own problems; if too many unscheduled aircraft land at an already busy airport, it might not have sufficient infrastructure to accommodate them all.

Heavy rain and storms can make flying harder

Planes need fuel and somewhere to park. Passengers need food and facilities. Ultimately, if there isn't space, it can simply refuse to accept all but emergency traffic.

This is where the scenario set out by Atkinson comes in. He warns of a situation where traffic is diverted from Heathrow, Gatwick, Luton and Stansted at the same time, leaving aircraft coming in across the Atlantic and a large number of European flights all looking for somewhere to land at the same time, and some with dwindling fuel reserves.

This would at the very least create serious disruption for passengers, who might end up hundreds of miles from their destinations. But it could also, he thinks, create "a very serious risk event, such as an aircraft running out of fuel in the sky".

Aircraft are legally required to carry enough fuel to take them to an alternative airport if necessary, and to allow them to fly for an extra 30 minutes on top of that.

Not everyone shares Atkinson's concern. Former airline executive Andrew Charlton, who manages the Swiss-based consultancy Aviation Advocacy, thinks the risk is low.

Thunderstorms are caused by what is known as convective activity, where moist air close to the ground warms rapidly and rises quickly. As it rises, it expands and cools, allowing the moisture it carries to condense, forming large clouds. More heat means more convective energy, potentially creating instability. In addition, for every degree the Earth warms, the atmosphere can hold about 7% more moisture, increasing the probability of heavy rainfall.

It is not clear whether this means we will actually get more thunderstorms in the UK and across Europe, as so many variables are involved beyond the simple matter of energy and moisture. Some parts of the world may even get fewer. However, studies have shown that higher latitudes, particularly in the northern hemisphere, will see the biggest increases in favourable conditions for storms to form, and those that do may be more violent. According to the UK's Met Office, "while we may not see more thunderstorms overall, the ones we do experience could be more impactful".

Where possible, pilots will avoid flying through convective weather. Even though aircraft are designed to cope with severe conditions, storms can produce intense turbulence, capable of damaging the structural frame of the plane, as well as throwing people around the cabin if they are not strapped in. Hail can also be a big problem, potentially cracking windscreens or causing engine problems. So, however much inconvenience it may cause passengers, crews will invariably put safety first.

New technology helps pilots navigate tricky flying conditions

Onboard weather radars can help aircrew to see up to 320 nautical miles ahead. They also have access to live weather forecasting resources online. But thunderstorms can move quickly and, according to Prof Guy Gratton, an expert in aeronautics at Cranfield University, these systems are essentially "tactical rather than strategic". In other words, although they enable pilots to see storm cells on their screens from a relatively short distance away, and request a diversion, they do not allow for detailed long-distance planning.

The result is that unplanned diversions often happen late in flight, which is likely to mean burning more fuel. If storm delays do become more common, says Andrew Charlton, then aircraft will have to load more fuel as a matter of course, in case there is trouble brewing ahead. "Aircraft are going to have to fly further, and they're going to have to factor in the assumption they're going to fly further," he says. The condition affects nearly 1 billion adults globally, including 80 million people ages 20 and over in the U.S., according to the National Council on Aging. Most have never been diagnosed, Lilly said in a release.

Lilly's blockbuster weight loss injection Zepbound became the first medication to win U.S. approval for OSA in December 2024. Patients have limited treatment options outside of that drug and many use cumbersome machines that provide positive airway pressure, or PAP, to allow for normal breathing.

"Foundayo is already making a difference for people living with obesity, and with plans to submit these results to the FDA by the end of the year, we could have the opportunity to help people living with OSA," Dr. Thomas Seck, senior vice president of product development of Lilly Cardiometabolic Health, said in a release.

The trial tested Foundayo in two independent studies, the first of which included 363 patients who were unable or unwilling to use PAP therapy. The second study included 349 people who were on PAP therapy for at least three months at the time of screening and continued that treatment during the trial.

Patients in both studies who took Foundayo started at a low dose and increased it gradually at four-week intervals to different maximum dosages.

Researchers specifically examined how much the pill reduced the so-called apnea-hypopnea index, or AHI, which records the number of times per hour a person's breathing shows a restricted or completely blocked airway. The index is used to evaluate the severity of obstructive sleep apnea and the effectiveness of treatments for the condition.

People who took Foundayo and were not using PAP therapy had an average of 21.1 fewer AHI events per hour at 52 weeks, compared with an average reduction of 4.8 per hour for the placebo, according to the results.

Foundayo also met the trial's second goal in that group, leading to an overall AHI reduction of 43.8% compared with a decrease of 6.3% for the placebo. Lilly said 31.5% of the participants in the Foundayo group met the criteria for remission or improvement to mild OSA.

Meanwhile, patients who were on both Foundayo and PAP therapy had an average of 18.3 fewer AHI events per hour at 52 weeks, compared with an average reduction of 5.5 per hour for the placebo.

Foundayo led to an overall AHI reduction of around 27.5% in that group, compared with a decrease of 3% for the placebo. The company said 19.6% of the participants on Foundayo and PAP therapy also met the criteria for remission or mild OSA.

Foundayo also reduced what's known as the sleep apnea-specific hypoxic burden — a measure of how deeply and for how long oxygen levels fall during sleep. That metric fell by 55.9% in people not using PAP therapy and 45.8% in those using it.

#304. My hometown shows that high streets have to change or die

My hometown shows that high streets have to change or die

The empty building that used to be the home of Marks & Spencer in Aberdeen is one of the first things you see when you arrive off the train and come into the city centre. The sign has gone, though the shape of the lettering can still be made out; the windows are blacked out and the doors boarded up.

This is my hometown. As a teenager, I spent hours on Union Street, its main thoroughfare, nicknamed the Granite Mile after the local silver-grey stone. It was the place to try on all the latest fashions from the likes of Clockhouse and Chelsea Girl.

The street used to be packed with shoppers from across the north-east of Scotland.

"I come to Union Street to take a bus somewhere else... there's nothing to shop for nowâ¦ it's horrendous," complains Marie Sim, a lifelong Aberdeen resident.

It's a similar story across the country: High Streets, once bursting with shops, hit first by the rise of out-of-town retail centres and then by online commerce.

But Aberdeen is in the thick of an ambitious turnaround plan. The port city has essentially declared war on its empty shop problem, taking an unusually hands-on approach to filling vacant units and bringing long-neglected upper floors of buildings into use.

The Marks & Spencer flagship store in Aberdeen closed in August, 80 years after it opened there

On top of that, the council is pouring tens of millions into trying to make the city centre a destination again. Like many other places, it's leaning into the "experience economy", with a new indoor food and drink market and a major redevelopment of the seafront.

High Streets are integral to how we feel about where we live. The sight of vacant shops and graffiti can quickly become a vicious circle, deterring shoppers, discouraging investment and leading to anti-social behaviour.

Politicians of all stripes have been trying for years to breathe new life into town centres - and Andy Burnham, who has made a focus on "place" part of his political identity, is the latest. He argues they should become "symbols of Britain's renaissance".

But can it be achieved? And if they're to survive, do town centres need a fundamental rethink?

One in eight properties on British High Streets are currently vacant, according to Green Street, the property and retail analytics company, a number that's stayed stubbornly high for years.

"If there was a silver bullet, it would have been fired years ago," says Jackie Sadek, a former government adviser on urban regeneration, who has spent nearly four decades working in town centre renewal.

One of the biggest obstacles is fractured ownership. Shops and buildings are often owned by dozens of different landlords, sometimes through offshore companies or complex structures, each with their own interests and priorities. That can slow decision-making and make large-scale regeneration difficult.

But even with empty units that are available, you need to find someone willing to take them on.

One in eight properties on British High Streets are currently vacant

The cost of running a High Street business has also risen; employment costs are higher, and there's the burden of business rates.

Firms need customers. Footfall on many High Streets has been in long-term decline as shopping and services move online, and this enormous structural shift has yet to play out fully.

Two-thirds of town centre experts think there is still 20% to 40% too much retail space, according to a survey by retail body Revo and real estate consultancy Lambert Smith Hampton.

In Aberdeen's boom years of North Sea oil, from the late 1970s through to the early 2010s, it didn't have to work hard to fill its hotels and restaurants and get the cash tills ringing because of the wealth the energy industry brought to the city.

I remember weekends with friends as a teenager, spending whatever cash we had in the fashion chains and shoe shops. Later came the pubs and bar-hopping, and trips to the art deco Capitol Theatre (now office space), where I saw Duran Duran.

But by the end of 2022, Union Street had hit rock bottom. Aberdonians had long deserted the High Street for a shopping centre, which opened in 2009 next to the railway station.

"They'd fallen out of love with their city," says Bob Keiller, head of Our Union Street coalition, a community-led group formed after an emergency summit to reverse the decline.

The first thing it did was listen to what residents wanted, receiving thousands of submissions. Restoring a bit of civic pride and filling empty units became the focus.

It's been a co-ordinated effort, involving letting agents, the council, landlords, businesses and a big team of volunteers, led by Keiller, a former FTSE 100 CEO, who isn't getting paid. He has a map on his office wall, detailing the status of every property, unit by unit, with colour-coded Post-it notes.

They audited the street, tracking down absent landlords, and logging what was empty and why. Volunteers offered to give the empty properties a clean and a lick of paint, to make them more attractive to let. They also spruced up the streets, picking litter, weeding, and painting bins. A small team still goes out once a fortnight.

Bob Keiller heads a community-led group set up to help revive Aberdeen's Union Street

There's a website where all the available properties are collated; crucially, all of the financial incentives are also listed. These can include council fit-out grants (where a new business gets money to decorate premises), and a two-year discount from business rates.

In 2023, 57 of the 198 units on Union Street were empty. As of today, 33 have been filled, with a further 14 in the process of being let.

But it can feel like a game of whack-a-mole because since the initiative started, another 20 units have become vacant. "The key thing is, we're filling quicker than emptying," Keiller insists.

Keiller's operations director, David McKeith, describes it as putting an arm round the shoulder of those they can help and being a "wasp in the ear" of people who just say no.

At one end of the office, a small, young team produces social media content, linking visitors and residents to a new Our Aberdeen app, showing what the city can offer.

For Keiller, the "small wins" - like tackling graffiti - are needed to help change public perception.

"We've realised the psychology is not to do it once but be prepared to do it two or three times before people that do these things eventually say, 'there's no point in putting it there because somebody's going to paint over it.'"

Attention is also turning to the often-forgotten upper floors of buildings along the once-iconic Union Street. In a separate initiative run by the business group Aberdeen Inspired, experts are getting inside those vacant high-up spaces to identify the barriers to bringing them back into use.

Aberdeen sought the help of a High Street troubleshooter, Iain Nicholson, who's worked with dozens of towns and cities through his Vacant Shops Academy.

Out of more than 1,000 empty units he helped audit nationally, on average more than half didn't have a letting agent actively marketing the property. He says some councils don't even know who the landlords are.

Empty shops, he says, won't all be filled by the market alone. Towns and cities need to encourage a broader range of occupiers, from arts and culture to leisure, health and well-being.

He says what's striking about Aberdeen is its joined-up approach, and the fact people "rolled up their sleeves" and overcame differences. "That's not something you can yet say about everywhere."

Every town and city faces its own mix of challenges.

Nicholson also helped Crewe in Cheshire. As a smaller place than Aberdeen, it is focusing on arts, culture, and community, with a new women's centre opened this month.

And English councils are being encouraged to take a more interventionist role.

They can now force an empty commercial unit that's been vacant for at least a year to be offered to tenants through a High Street Rental Auction, even if the landlord doesn't want to let it.

A noodle bar (left) is one of the new businesses in the area. Parts of the city centre have been undergoing construction work (right)

It's early days but the mere prospect of losing control of a property is delivering some results. Bassetlaw Council in Nottinghamshire, an early adopter of the scheme, says more than 30 shops across the district have been let or are currently under offer as a result of the policy.

But filling empty units is only part of the challenge. The bigger question is what town and city centres are actually for.

Changing habits mean we won't need so many shops. Instead, it's argued, town centres will need more people living and working in them, with new homes and office space alongside services that can't be done online. With an ageing population, for example, bringing NHS health centres into the heart of a town could be a big opportunity.

In Aberdeen, the building that used to house BHS - a department store shuttered in 2016 - is being converted into an entrance plaza for the new food and drink market.

The physical appearance of Union Street is changing too. Like many Victorian shopping thoroughfares, it was built with a wide road (probably to give space to horses and carriages). Now parts of the pavements are being widened to make it a more pleasant place to spend time, though many Aberdonians are weary of the disruption, and of new traffic restrictions.

Money is a big barrier. Aberdeen's new indoor food and drink market is costing Â£40m, funded by the council and the UK Levelling Up Fund. In other places, regeneration schemes can struggle because the cost of redevelopment outweighs the likely financial return, making support from the public sector essential. And the economics are becoming more challenging.

"Higher interest rates, rising costs of construction and other issues are impacting the viability and delivery of new housing and big redevelopment projects," says Steve Norris, a director at Lambert Smith Hampton. He says that big projects usually require cash from both the public and private sectors.

He wants to see "more creative ways of funding".

Part of Burnham's plan is to give local leaders more freedom to invest in town centres and drive regeneration, building on the approach he has championed in Greater Manchester. But bigger projects take years.

Volunteers have played a role in the redevelopment of Aberdeen's Union Street

Making it easier and cheaper to open and run a High Street business is one of the more immediate solutions being called for, with business rates a particular concern.

Burnham has promised further rates reform. All eyes are on this month's Budget to see how far he will go. All eyes are on this month's Budget to see how far he will go in England. Business rates are devolved in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The chief executive of Next, Simon Wolfson, argues that the bigger challenge is not business rates, but planning reform.

"The most important thing that the government can do for high streets is let them develop," says Wolfson, who's also a Conservative peer.

Back in Aberdeen, there are some signs of traction.

Lauren Reid and her mother Rose moved their fashion boutique to Union Street in 2024. A council grant helped with the refurbishment after they decided to make the leap.

Can they make the sums add up? "We're getting there," she says.

She feels the same about the future of her street. "I always say Rome wasn't built in a day."

Lauren Reid and her mother Rose moved their fashion boutique to Union Street with the help of a council grant

Right now the area feels like a construction site - but another gaping hole will soon be filled. Robert Keane is a local property developer who in 2023 bought the Trinity shopping centre, once home to Debenhams. The space is now set to be re-occupied by a national leisure brand. A notoriously tiring stairway leading to the Trinity from the train station is also being replaced by an escalator.

"We saw a real good opportunity, because it was dying," he says.

He reckons he can make a go of it, cutting the cost of parking and overheads, resetting rents and being more nimble than previous far-flung owners.

It was a bold move given Lambert Smith Hampton suggests that more than one in 10 UK shopping centres is ripe for demolition.

But he's betting the dynamics of Aberdeen are finally changing.

"I think by 2027 it should look and feel like a different city."

That is certainly the hope. Yet walking along Union Street today, past the boarded-up shopfronts and roadworks, it can still feel like a city caught between its past and whatever comes next.

#290. Honda to invest $3 billion in new vehicle assembly plant in Ohio

MARYSVILLE, Ohio — Honda Motor plans to invest $3 billion to build a new vehicle assembly plant in Ohio, the automaker announced Monday.

Honda said the new plant, which will be near its other facilities in Marysville, Ohio, is expected to begin production in 2029, have a capacity for 240,000 vehicles annually and create more than 2,000 jobs.

The facility is expected to be capable of producing traditional vehicles with internal combustion engines, all-electric vehicles and hybrid models, which have been growing in popularity in the U.S.

Honda declined to disclose what vehicles the company plans to produce at the new facility.

The plant is expected to increase Honda's light-duty vehicle capacity to 1.3 million units annually in the U.S. It currently has 12 manufacturing plants in United States, eight of which produce engines or light-duty vehicles for the auto industry.

"This is not simply an investment in another plant. It is an investment in the future of Honda manufacturing in America," Bob Nelson, executive vice president of American Honda Motor, said during the announcement at the company's U.S. heritage center in Ohio.

The auto industry has been trying to localize more production amid President Donald Trump's tariffs and inflated logistics costs that have cut into profits.

Nelson told CNBC after the event that tariffs are affecting the company's business but the new plant is more about Honda's ambitions to grow in the U.S.

"Tariffs are an impact to us, even with the high localization rate that we have," he said. "But the real impact is that we have customers out there wanting Honda product and we can't give it to them. That's the real area."

Nelson said Honda did not work with the Trump administration ahead of Monday's announcement.

Honda's plans for a new plant come roughly two weeks after Volkswagen-owned luxury brand Audi announced it would invest more than $4 billion in a new plant at a rally with President Donald Trump, which Audi CEO Gernot Dollner faced criticism for attending.

Honda's new plant is the company's first new mass production U.S. vehicle assembly plant since it opened a facility in Indiana in 2008. It also opened a $70 million performance vehicle facility in 2016 and a multibillion-dollar joint venture battery plant earlier this year, both in Ohio.

The announcement comes after the automaker in May indefinitely suspended an $11 billion investment plan to produce all-electric vehicles and batteries in Canada. It also canceled three planned EVs for the U.S. market.

American Honda Motor Co. executive Jennifer Thomas recently emphasized the Japanese automaker's commitment to the U.S. and need for additional capacity.

"In order to be competitive in this market, you're going to need to build products here," Thomas, senior vice president of corporate affairs, said Oct. 27 in Detroit. "We are focused on trying to maximize the existing capacity that we have, but we are at capacity, and so we're exploring all options."

Thomas also warned the automotive industry faces a "catastrophic" scenario if the U.S. and Canada cannot reach a new trade deal by the end of this year, when Trump has threatened to double tariffs on Canadian vehicles and parts from 25% to 50% on Jan. 1 if the two countries don't reach a deal.

Honda said it has built more than 33 million vehicles and more than 39 million engines in the U.S. since it started auto production in the U.S. in 1982.

The Japanese automaker currently has more than 30,000 employees in the U.S.

#289. Thrift shop asks shoppers to nominate a charity

Julie Rindam (front) and Jeanette Worthington (back) plan to be transparent with donors by having a book in the shop which will evidence where money is going.

Shoppers and donors at a new charity shop will be able to help decide which organisations benefit from the money it raises.

Twisted Sisters Thrift Store, in Tunstall opened last week, and is inviting customers to nominate causes they would like to support, with a different charity set to receive donations each month.

Owner Julie Rindam said the idea came after the closure of a charity shop; she had run for eight years.

"The pastor is retiring and it was either another empty shop or I took it on."

Rindam said many of the shop's regular donors wanted to continue supporting charitable causes, leading to the idea of allowing the community to decide where the money goes.

"All my donators still wanted to drop off to us, so we thought, well, how about a different charity every month?" she said.

"We ask all the donators who they'd like us to donate to, so every month it will be a different charity benefiting."

Rather than supporting a single organisation, the shop plans to keep a record of suggestions, being completely transparent with customers and donors before selecting a charity at the end of each month.

"Everyone who drops off, we ask and we keep a book and every month we'll pick a different charity to benefit from, whether that's City Dogs Home, Cancer Research, Diabetes or other charities," Rindam said.

The owner hopes that the store will also be a centre for the community.

Shop manager Jeanette Worthington said the approach was designed to ensure local people remained involved in how the money was distributed.

As well as raising money for charity, the store also aims to act as a community hub, offering free food, sanitary products, contraception and school uniforms when available.

Worthington said supporting local people was just as important as supporting charities.

"People know they can come here, it's a safe space," she said.

"We will help anybody if we have the time and if we can't do it, we'll say come back at such and such a time."

Janet Smart had been a regular donater to the shop before it was taken over by the new team.

Regular customer and donor Janet Smart said the shop had become a valuable part of the community.

"When I'm decluttering everything, it comes to here because I know they don't charge a lot. People haven't got much money today," she said.

"You meet such nice people in here as well."

She added that the social side of the shop was equally important.

"I think for people on their own, it's nice to be able to come in somewhere and people chat with them."

Jack Spencer is managing to find clothes and items that he wouldn't be able to find in more mainstream stores.

Shopper Jack Spencer said the store offered affordable options for people who might otherwise struggle to buy certain items.

"It feels a lot more personal, a lot more out there. You go in, you don't know what you're going to walk into, it changes constantly," he said.

"You might find something that you could have been looking for, but you just couldn't afford it."

For Rindam and Worthington, the hope is that both the charities and the local community will benefit.
