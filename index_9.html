#472. Hims & Hers launches new peptide treatments — while admitting there's more to learn about efficacy

Hims & Hers Health on Wednesday said it will offer five formulations of peptides and longevity treatments compounded in the U.S. as part of the initial rollout of its Peptides+ platform, all of which are not approved by regulators.

Even as it announced the new products, Hims & Hers acknowledged it has more to learn about how best to use the peptides and how effective they are — underscoring the uncertainty surrounding the growing space.

The new offerings come after the federal government eased restrictions on compounding certain unapproved peptides, which are short chains of amino ​acids. Those products have exploded in popularity among fitness and longevity enthusiasts who attest that they help with muscle growth, rapid injury recovery and anti-aging, even though most lack evidence from robust human trials.

Hims in late October launched an early access list for the upcoming peptide offerings, and more than 10,000 people signed up within 15 days, a company spokesperson said in a statement.

None of the company's specific compounded products are approved by the Food and Drug Administration. But Hims said they are authorized by law and formulated by pharmacies "that adhere to rigorous quality standards and are licensed and inspected by state boards of pharmacy."

The products include compounded formulations of sermorelin, a peptide that stimulates growth-hormone release and is marketed for recovery, body composition and anti-aging; glutathione, a peptide promoted ‌for anti-aging and athletic recovery; and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide, or NAD+, a coenzyme that plays a role in cellular metabolism.

The company will also offer compounded versions of vitamin B-12, a vitamin needed to make red blood cells, build DNA, and maintain healthy nerve function, and L-carnitine, an amino acid derivative that helps the body turn fat into energy. The FDA has approved certain prescription forms of both peptides for established medical uses, primarily for diagnosed deficiencies in those compounds.

Hims said it plans to add more peptides and therapies made in the U.S. over time. That includes BPC-157, an unapproved peptide derived from a protein found in human gastric juice that is marketed for healing processes. The company also aims to introduce another unapproved peptide called TB-500, which is derived from a naturally occurring protein in the body and is marketed for broader, systemic soft-tissue recovery and flexibility. Most of the research on both has been conducted in animals, not humans.

To receive the products, members on the Hims platform will start with an assessment of health history and goals with a licensed provider trained in peptide therapies, who can tailor a personalized plan to their needs, if appropriate.

The company said it intends to use its communication platform to check in with patients and ensure they are seeing desired results.

"If they're not, we need to do something about it," Dr. Anant Vinjamoori, Hims chief medical officer, told CNBC. "We need to, you know, adjust the dose when that's appropriate, or in some cases consider other compounds that's maybe a better fit. And of course, there will also be cases when we need to refer people to other care options."

However, Vinjamoori said there's still uncertainty over how to best use the peptides.

"We don't have all of this data that we're used to having with compounds like this," he said. "We have the opportunity to get it right, and I think we see that as our responsibility with a company of our size, our skill, and resources to really lead."

Vinjamoori added that people who have serious illnesses, such as cardiovascular disease, liver disease and kidney disease, will not be candidates for the compounds for now.

Meanwhile, Andrew Huberman, a well known neuroscientist, podcaster and Stanford University School of Medicine professor who recently joined Hims' board of directors, in a statement called compounds "real medical treatments, not just a wellness trend." He said Hims provides safe access through "trusted sourcing and physician oversight."

But health experts raised concerns about the broader mainstream marketing of peptides in the U.S. and accessing them on telehealth platforms.

"With peptides, I think you have to view them the same way you would view any drug that hasn't been rigorously tested," said Joshua Kritzer, a chemistry professor at Tufts University. "Without actual testing, all you have is anecdotal information, and that's just not enough information to put a substance in your body."

Kritzer said the lack of large clinical trials means researchers can't rule out potentially serious, rare complications from peptides — the kinds of risks that have led regulators to reject or pull otherwise effective drugs from the market after they underwent comprehensive testing in humans.

Without that testing, it's also unclear how telehealth firms, physicians and pharmacies can make decisions about what doses patients should take, said Paul Knoepfler, a cell biology and human anatomy professor at UC Davis Health. While some people rely on anecdotal data from real peptide users, "the dosing for these products really has not been established," he added.

Knoepfler said one risk is that injecting too much of a synthetic peptide into patients could trigger "weird immune responses that are really bad for one's health" because the body might recognize it as foreign.

"With the peptides, it's very loosey-goosey," Knoepfler told CNBC. "How does one even know what would be the effective amount for whatever you're hoping it will do, and what amount would have more risks?"

But he said the more contact patients can have with physicians around taking a compounded peptide, the better. Providers, including those tied to telehealth firms, should make sure patients are well-informed about the possible risks and unknowns of a given product and consider their preexisting conditions, cancer history and other medications, Knoepfler said.

That appears to be consistent with the approach Hims & Hers is taking. The company says patients who use the peptide and longevity products can also use its Care+ offering, which pairs treatment with ongoing support. That includes twice-a-year lab testing to track their progress and the ability to message providers trained in peptide therapy around the clock with any questions.

Still, Kritzer argued that having a doctor who only knows a patient from online, essentially mail-order platforms or websites "is not enough supervision to put you on a prescription medication," especially an unapproved one.

Hims' ability to legally sell some of these peptides stems from a major shift in federal oversight by the Health and Human Services Department. It issued a policy in October that says the Food and Drug Administration will not take enforcement action against licensed pharmacies, federal facilities or physicians that compound and dispense six unapproved peptides while formal rules are reviewed.

In practice, that means these pharmacies and other firms can now offer them, even though the peptides aren't yet on the FDA's official list of ingredients compounding pharmacies are allowed to make. It does not translate to formal approval of the specific peptides.

The policy followed a 2023 decision by the Biden administration where the FDA restricted compounding pharmacies from producing popular peptide formulations due to safety, impurity and immune-reaction concerns.

HHS Secretary ​Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has supported broader access to peptides, calling himself a "big fan" of the treatments and arguing that restrictions were driving Americans to potentially unsafe products from overseas. Kennedy has previously described using BPC-157 to treat a muscle injury.

The announcement also comes after Hims in February 2025 acquired a California-based peptide manufacturing facility equipped with advanced peptide synthesizers.

The evidence for peptide efficacy varies by compound.

A 2026 systematic review screening 113 studies, including both small trials on humans along with rodent research, found zero eligible clinical outcome trials proving that administering IV or intramuscular NAD+ improves anti-aging, energy, or wellness endpoints in humans.

There is some evidence for specific uses of certain peptides, such as potential weight loss. A review of 37 L-carnitine trials found participants lost about 2.7 pounds on average, though that analysis does not demonstrate that a compounded injection works the same way.

Meanwhile, vitamin B-12 injections are most commonly used to help people with a diagnosed deficiency rather than preventative care. U.S. survey data shows only 3.6% of adults are B-12 deficient.

Glutathione's evidence is thin, too. IV injections of it are widely marketed for skin whitening, yet not FDA-approved for this purpose.

A small study published in the Journal of Pakistan Association of Dermatologists evaluating IV glutathione showed that the treatment is "not very effective" for skin-lightening, and that it loses efficacy with time.

The FDA also said in August that it received reports of at least 30 patients who experienced adverse events after they received compounded IV glutathione alone or in combination with other ingredients from different compounding pharmacies.

All of the glutathione tied to the reports were from the same wholesale distributor and was labeled as dietary supplement grade, which is not an appropriate ingredient to make injectable drugs. Two of the Texas pharmacies conducted recalls related to elevated endotoxin levels in their glutathione injectable drugs.

These studies and reports don't speak to how Hims' exact combination will perform, but they do highlight the current limits on data studying the treatments and the questions surrounding them.

Other sportswear majors have taken a different approach. Adidas signed with Mercedes in 2025 and added Audi for its debut season this year, while Puma still has its longstanding deal with Ferrari, covering racewear and licensing for Ferrari-branded products.

Unlike football leagues such as the English Premier League, where sportswear brands spread their budgets across numerous clubs, F1 has just 11 teams, limiting the number of apparel partnerships up for grabs.

"F1, by contrast, is a closed market with only a handful of teams," Lewis said, adding that the scarcity of deals makes brands more willing to pay higher prices as contracts come up for renewal.

What makes F1 particularly attractive, however, is its growing influence.

"For major sportswear and lifestyle brands, F1 has grown beyond motorsport, into a powerful, year-round pop-culture and storytelling platform," Joey Khong, global consumer trends manager at market research firm Mintel, told CNBC.

F1 brings together fashion, music, luxury and celebrity culture with athletic performance and technological innovation, offering brands multiple avenues to engage with consumers, Khong said.

Team apparel has also crossed over into everyday streetwear, with social media amplifying the visibility of what drivers and team members wear off the track.

Some 34% of people who engage with F1 do so through social media, while 22% engage through online content, according to Lewis.

That constant stream of content has turned team apparel into what Lewis described as a "living billboard" for sportswear brands.

Nearly a third of F1 fans are now under the age of 35, with that share rising 18% since 2023, according to Lewis. Meanwhile, 45% of F1 fans fall into the two highest-earning socioeconomic groups, compared with 36% of the general population.

For sportswear brands, F1 offers a way to build loyalty among younger, affluent consumers.

Asia-Pacific has been a key driver of F1's fan growth, helping deliver a larger audience for its commercial partners, analysts said.

Markets across the region have gained more than 25 million F1 fans since 2023, up 36%, according to Ampere Analysis. That outpaced growth in other markets by 11 percentage points and accounted for 84% of overall fan growth.

That offers sportswear brands a growing audience to engage with in a region where F1 already races in Melbourne, Suzuka, Shanghai and Singapore.

Research also points to why that engagement could resonate. In a 2026 Mintel survey, 29% of respondents in China said sporting events would prompt them to pay attention to or try related brands, rising to 37% in households earning at least 24,000 yuan ($3,600) a month.

Fans are spending more on F1 merchandise than ever before.

F1 merchandise sales have grown by almost 200% since 2017, according to Fanatics, the sport's e-commerce partner, cited in a License Global report.

Retail sales of F1-licensed consumer products also rose 44% in 2024, one of the fastest growth rates among the sports properties tracked.

The appeal may stretch beyond race-branded gear. F1 fans were 29% more likely than the average consumer to say they planned to buy sports apparel in the next year and 27% more likely to say the same about non-sports apparel, according to Ampere's consumer research.

Mintel's Khong said F1 merchandise alone is unlikely to move the needle quickly for sports apparel giants the size of Nike or Adidas.

Instead, the bigger potential payoff could come from the partnership boosting demand across the brand's everyday product lines outside F1-specific collaborations or the sport's licensed merchandise.

"There is a route to revenue growth, but being associated with the sport is only the starting point," Khong said.

#450. Latin America has the copper and energy the AI build-out needs. Infrastructure is the test

Latin America’s copper, energy and mineral resources could play a growing role in supplying the global artificial-intelligence build-out, as data centers and electrification drive demand for power and raw materials.

But for investors, it's less about whether Latin America has the resources than whether governments can turn them into viable projects.

"We are going through an acceleration in energy demand growth. We need every possible energy source available," Michele Della Vigna, head of natural resources research for Goldman Sachs, told CNBC. "Ultimately, what drives economic growth is energy," he added.

Ben Kumar, head of strategy for wealth, investment and public policy at 7IM, suggested Latin America could benefit, given the physical materials needed for AI equipment and data centers. "We are currently neutral in the short term, but if the materials and mining cycle continues, we could see this moving to positive in a few months," he said.

The investment case varies across the region. Chile has copper; Argentina is expanding oil and gas exports; and Brazil has abundant renewable power and significant rare earth reserves.

Chile's copper sector illustrates both the supply potential and the constraints to expanding supply. Copper production fell 8.5% in the first nine months of 2026, according to the state copper commission Cochilco, as lower ore grades, maintenance, and operational disruptions constrained supply.

But demand for refined copper is set to grow 1.6% this year and 2% next year, according to the International Copper Study Group. Supply is struggling to keep pace with demand from expanding electricity grids.

Chile's copper commission has raised its 2027 price forecast to $5.95 a pound, from $5.10. Codelco, the state-owned copper producer, may also have scope to increase supply, including through proposed public-private partnerships.

Rafael Ch, partner and senior analyst for Latin America and emerging markets at Signum Global Advisors, sees demand continuing to grow. "As countries expand electricity grids, they need more copper regardless of which power source they choose," he said.

The scale of the opportunity will depend in part on how quickly energy demand grows. Goldman Sachs forecasts that global power demand for data centers will grow by 170% by 2030. The IEA projected in April that global data center electricity consumption would rise from 485 terawatt-hours in 2025 to 950 TWh in 2030.

The investment case also depends on whether the region can capture more value from the resources it produces. Latin America and the Caribbean refined only 26% of the copper they mined in 2025, according to the IEA.

"Cautious optimism is justified and there will be massive critical mineral demand," said Monica de Bolle, senior fellow at the Peterson Institute for International Economics.

"But improving refining capacity will be crucial to capture more of that value," she added.

Some projects have already secured financing. Chilean explorer Marimaca Copper Corp selected a preferred lender for a $400 million debt facility to build a mine on Oct. 28. The funding will go towards developing an open-pit mine in the north of Chile that will produce 50,000 metric tons of refined copper annually. Production is expected from 2029.

Argentina shows the other side of the opportunity: strong oil and gas resources, but insufficient infrastructure to get more production to global markets.

"I see an opportunity in the infrastructure that gets Argentine oil and gas to market. A producer in Vaca Muerta can have excellent wells and still struggle to expand exports without pipeline capacity and access to ports.

That gives investors a reason to look at transport and export infrastructure alongside production," Rafael Ch, partner and senior analyst for Latin America and emerging markets at Signum Global Advisors, added. Vaca Muerta is Argentina's vast shale basin.

Argentina is also attracting investment in drilling and export infrastructure. International capital markets have reopened to investment in drilling and pipelines in Argentina. That investment has already driven the build-out of a pipeline and port. Argentina will then be able to export far more crude oil. Chevron and American oil tycoon Harold Hamm have also increased their investments in Argentina.

Brazil's case rests partly on its renewable-heavy power mix. About 89% of the country's electricity was generated from renewable sources in 2025, according to the country's Ministry of Mines and Energy.

"That puts Brazil in a strong position to benefit as AI and data centers drive up demand," Stephanie Link, chief investment strategist and portfolio manager at Hightower Advisors, told CNBC.

But while Brazil has the world's second-largest reported rare-earth reserves, its estimated mine production is far smaller than China's. USGS estimates put Brazil's output at 2,000 metric tons in 2025, compared with China's 270,000 metric tons. Brazil's reserves are estimated at 11 million metric tons by the USGS, second only to China's 44 million metric tons.

For investors, the gap between Brazil's vast reserves and limited current production is both the opportunity and the execution risk.

For investors, the policy question is whether governments can make projects viable and keep them on track.

Luis Costa, Citi's global head of emerging markets strategy, said governments need to offer incentives, particularly in the early stages of projects, because infrastructure lags the region's resource wealth. He also stressed that support must last: infrastructure projects can take several years to develop.

The World Bank's Latin America and the Caribbean Economic Update for 2026 projects growth for the region of 2.2%, and highlights the "restricted fiscal space, sticky inflation and high borrowing costs."

"The economic policy and fiscal policy needs to get in line as markets are globally becoming more sensitive to fiscal stories," said Costa.

Brazil will need to establish fiscal credibility to attract investment, particularly if higher public spending puts additional pressure on debt and borrowing costs. "If investors doubt the government can stabilize public debt, companies' long-term financing costs could stay high even as the central bank lowers its policy rate," Ch added.

With Argentina set for national elections in October 2027, political continuity will also matter for energy investment. "A return to tighter currency controls or persistent deficits would make companies more likely to delay projects, even in an oil field with attractive economics," said Ch.

Pro-business President Javier Milei faces a tight race for re-election, though a change in leadership would not necessarily undermine medium- to long-term investment.

"There is also the possibility of a pragmatic Peronist ticket emerging, which markets would be satisfied with," said Nicolas Jaquier, emerging-market fixed-income portfolio manager at Ninety One.

For those governing across Latin America, policies to unlock its wealth of resources will be key for it to emerge as a winner from the AI demand boom.

"Supply chain security is becoming more and more relevant, and if commodities take off Latin America can be a clear and obvious winner," said Kumar.

#423. Spain’s Pablo Hernández de Cos set to lead European Central Bank

Pablo Hernández de Cos is set to succeed Christine Lagarde as president of the European Central Bank next year.

He received backing from euro zone leaders at the European Council Summit on Thursday. The role still requires formal appointment after consultation with the European Parliament and the ECB's Governing Council, but neither has veto power and the decision follows intense political wrangling to reach a consensus on who should serve the eight-year term.

De Cos is a 55-year-old economist and career central banker who rose through the ranks of the Bank of Spain, starting as a researcher in the 1990s and working in various departments before becoming governor from 2018 to June 2024.

He chaired the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision from 2019 to 2024, and has been General Manager of the Basel-based Bank for International Settlements since July 2025.

Both de Cos and his main rival for the ECB top job, the Netherlands' Klaas Knot, were widely seen as candidates with deep knowledge and experience in monetary policy and financial stability — attributes that took on particular importance at a time of resurgent inflation, fragmentation and political uncertainty.

Lagarde had extensive experience in financial institutions but was criticized by some early in her tenure for a lack of central banking experience.

De Cos gained sufficient political backing in closed-door discussions and negotiations since Lagarde said she would step down on Feb. 7, 2027, over seven years after she became the first woman to helm the ECB. That was despite a final push by the Netherlands at the European Council meeting this week backing Knot.

Representatives from the euro area's biggest economies — Germany, France, Italy, Spain and the Netherlands — have been horse-trading as they look to secure representation on the central bank's six-member Executive Board.

Influential roles as chief economist, expected to go to Germany's Markus Brunnermeier, and head of the Markets Portfolio, for which France's Laurence Boone has reportedly been tapped, also change hands in 2027.

Analysts say de Cos's commentary and actions over more than two decades at Spain's central bank paint him as moderately dovish — inclined to favor lower interest rates to support economic growth — but also as a pragmatist who has been willing to adapt.

That has included support for strong rate hikes during the energy price shock that followed Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, but also giving staunch backing for rates below zero and the huge stimulus package launched by then-ECB President Mario Draghi to reboot the euro zone economy in 2019.

De Cos quipped in 2024 that he was an "owl" after being asked if he was a hawk or a dove, emphasizing his focus on data and the situation at hand.

"It is true that Hernández de Cos supported the ECB's uber-dovish monetary policy during the period when inflation was persistently below target. However, his monetary policy views may well be regime-dependent," Christian Kopf, head of fixed income and foreign exchange at Union Investment, said in emailed comments.

"We are now clearly in a higher-inflation regime, with core inflation in the euro area expected to remain above the ECB's target through 2029."

Kopf added that de Cos was the candidate with the "strongest technical understanding of monetary policy and central banking," which he said was important at a time when the world is "witnessing an assault on central bank independence, not only in the United States but also in countries such as Japan."

"By appointing a career technocrat such as Hernández de Cos as ECB President, the European Council would send a strong signal that Europe will not waver in its commitment to price stability and that the euro will remain a hard currency," Kopf said.

Spain's de Cos as president, Germany's Brunnermeier as chief economist and France's Boone as head of market operations represent a "carefully crafted package deal," Kopf also noted, because it balances representation from northern and southern European countries. The result was partially due to the knock-on effects of the German government pushing hard for the chief economist position, he said.

Along with a desire among politicians for influence, a north-south balance is seen as important as the northern countries are seen as leaning more hawkish — more inclined to opt for rate hikes and restrictive policy to tame inflation — while southern are perceived as more dovish.

John Orchard, chairman of the Digital Monetary Institute at think tank OMFIF, said that as a "central banker's central banker," de Cos had "managed to earn respect from typically hawkish northern observers."

Another supportive factor of de Cos's candidacy was the stellar growth rate of many of those southern economies in recent years.

Once the "sick men" of the euro area's 2010s sovereign debt crisis, Portugal, Italy, Spain and Greece have become some of the bloc's strongest growth drivers in recent years.

Factors such as strong tourism, relative political stability, increased foreign investment and strengthening public finances have boosted southern economies. Meanwhile, the euro zone's traditional powerhouse Germany has stuttered amid soaring industrial costs and a struggling autos market.

Orchard said that some believed that Lagarde's presidency had ushered in an "esprit de corps" at the ECB, with policymakers focused on the interests of the whole European economy.

That would, in theory, reduce the importance of nationality among leadership and of traditional notions of hawkish northern nations and dovish southern ones. However, Orchard also noted the recurrent theme of national representation in the recent debate and the perception of Knot as a hawkish choice.

Diverging performance is a core challenge for any leader of the ECB, which must set monetary policy for 21 economies all growing at different speeds and with their own domestic priorities and inflationary pressures.

But it has become particularly pressing this year as the bloc's second-biggest economy, France, is teetering on the edge of its own potential debt crisis.

Moves in French markets and whether the ECB needs to take more extreme measures beyond continuing to urge fiscal discipline will remain in focus for de Cos.

The ECB Executive Board's view of what measures could be taken to prevent market disorder and under what conditions was a key topic under discussion when choosing who should fill the roles, according to insiders.

Despite political efforts to pass a budget that will reduce its budget deficit to around 5% of GDP, investors have been driving up French borrowing costs on concerns over its mounting public debt-to-GDP ratio, high budget deficit and contracting economy.

Risks are being compounded by political uncertainty ahead of France's presidential election in April, which is stymying business activity and raising wider questions about EU unity as far-right and far-left candidates pull ahead.

Rising French yields and the widening spread in France's borrowing costs over Germany's have led to speculation over whether the ECB might need to deploy tools such as liquidity injections or its never-used Transmission Protection Instrument to protect other countries from contagion and support the euro.

This would be politically controversial unless France agreed to stringent fiscal discipline measures akin to those that southern nations signed up to during the Sovereign Debt Crisis — which could be complicated if far-right presidential frontrunner Marine Le Pen, who has a combative relationship with EU institutions, wins.

Ken Egan, head of European sovereigns at ratings agency KBRA, said fragmentation was always a challenge for the ECB, which does not want to see divergent financing conditions and high sovereign spreads across the bloc.

However, he said that in the case of France he believed we were "some way from having a serious conversation about ECB intervention."

"If anything, sovereign spreads have pulled back a bit and tightened, and some measures of market liquidity have also improved in recent weeks," he said by email.

"That doesn't mean the underlying risks have disappeared, but I think it's important to distinguish between periods of market volatility and a more fundamental breakdown in monetary policy transmission."

De Cos also takes up the mantle as Europe faces yet another surge in price pressures.

Euro area inflation rose to 4% in October, its highest level in more than three years. Energy was the biggest contributor, as the Iran war drives up oil and gas prices and tightens the supply of refined fuels.

That leaves the central bank facing a difficult policy balance, preventing higher energy costs from spreading into wages and wider prices while avoiding unnecessary damage to growth.

De Cos will inherit major supranational projects, including plans for a digital euro and Europe's long-delayed capital-markets integration. In a recent speech, Lagarde warned that each year without capital markets integration meant "more of our savings are invested abroad and more of our growing firms look elsewhere for funding."

France is not the only euro area country heading for a pivotal election next year, with votes in Finland, Greece, Italy, Estonia, Slovakia and Slovenia also expected, adding to the politically uncertain climate.

It is also not the only country grappling with higher government borrowing costs, public debt and a struggle to find fiscal headroom, a growing problem among many developed countries.

The ECB's Financial Stability Review meanwhile warned this month of mounting risks from European investors' growing exposure to the U.S. economy, and equity and credit markets' vulnerability to AI.

Outgoing head Lagarde, meanwhile, told the European Banking Congress in Frankfurt this month that her biggest concern was now cyber threats, an area she said policymakers had not paid enough attention.

"The one [worry] that keeps me awake, and many of you awake ​as well ... ​is ⁠AI applied together with cyber threat," she said.

#346. Private markets are more accessible than ever for investors. Getting out is another matter

PwC research has found that 77% of fund distributors surveyed said access to private markets is becoming important or very important to what they plan to offer clients, but just 15% of asset managers said they provide comprehensive education for retail investors.

Unlike the public markets retail investors are more used to, some private-market vehicles limit when investors can get their money back to set repurchase windows, and even then may return only part of what they request.

A spike in redemption requests at several private-credit funds earlier this year exposed the tension between giving investors regular opportunities to cash out and holding assets that can be difficult to sell quickly.

Blackstone restricted withdrawals from its $79 billion flagship nontraded BCRED private credit fund to 5% of shares in June, after redemption requests hit 10% during the second quarter. Apollo also capped withdrawals in its Apollo Debt Solutions vehicle at 5% after second-quarter redemption requests surged to almost 17%. Blue Owl, Morgan Stanley and Partners Group also limited investor withdrawals from some of their funds.

Limitations on redemptions are a key trade-off in structures such as LTAFs in the U.K., ELTIFs in the EU and interval funds in the U.S., which broaden access to less-liquid assets.

It's emerged as a major pressure point as those structures come under closer scrutiny.

"Private markets can have very different characteristics from the products many wealth clients are used to, particularly around liquidity, valuations and holding periods," said Albertha Charles, global asset and wealth management leader at PwC.

"Investors and advisers need to understand how these products work, including their liquidity and valuation characteristics, so they can make informed decisions about whether they are appropriate."

Distributors said better advisor and client education would help expand access, while liquidity, lock-ups and suitability requirements remain major barriers. Private equity and private credit were also the areas where distributors saw some of the biggest gaps in advisor knowledge.

Private credit appears likely to draw the strongest demand — two-thirds (66%) of distributors expect it to lead client interest in private markets, compared with 46% for private equity.

Regulators in several major markets are creating structures to widen access to long-term and illiquid assets. The U.K. uses long-term asset funds, or LTAFs, while the EU's European long-term investment funds, known as ELTIFs, can be marketed across member states.

In the U.S., interval funds provide periodic, rather than daily, liquidity. Singapore, Japan and Hong Kong have also taken steps to broaden retail or listed access to private assets. The frameworks differ, but all aim to make less-liquid investments workable within existing distribution, suitability and liquidity requirements.

Yen Sum, global chair of the private capital practice at Latham & Watkins, and a member of its executive committee, said access to private-market funds is generally limited to investors who certify they are qualified or sophisticated.

#325. Rejecting cheap fabrics, higher prices: Why shoppers are asking for better clothes

Clothing isn't made like it used to be, and more shoppers are taking notice.

The makeup of apparel imported into the U.S. has shifted in recent years. In 2010, about 60% of clothing was predominantly made from cotton. Now, that total is around 45%, while predominantly synthetic fabrics such as polyester and nylon comprise the majority of imports.

Apparel prices generally declined over the two decades leading up to the pandemic, according to the consumer price index. Since 2020, they have soared to record highs.

It's starting to change habits for consumers who are paying more for food, gas, rent and other staples. As shoppers shell out more for clothes, they're increasingly making sure the garments are worth the cost.

Consulting firm AlixPartners' 2026 Consumer Sentiment Index, which surveyed more than 9,000 shoppers, found that consumers are scrutinizing labels more closely and rejecting cheap materials sold at a premium.

"If it's not a natural material, a retailer brand who passes it off as it is, they're really becoming very low on consumers' minds," said Sonia Lapinsky, head of fashion at AlixPartners.

Fast fashion brands like Forever 21 and H&M exploded in popularity during the early 2000s and 2010s, bringing trendy clothing to shelves quickly at low prices. Then came ultra-fast fashion, with brands like Shein and Fashion Nova pumping out new garments at even quicker speeds and lower price points — made possible in part through the use of inexpensive, synthetic fabrics.

"We got to a point where the pendulum just swung too far in that direction. And we've actually begun to see a reversal," said U.S. Bancorp BTIG consumer analyst Janine Stichter. "Quality matters a lot more ... over the last three or four years than it has in the previous 10 or 20."

But quality, which shoppers often tie directly to material, can come at a higher cost.

The average unit price of apparel imported to the U.S. between January and August was $2.64 per square meter for garments that were predominantly synthetic fiber, like polyester and nylon, compared with $3.67 for items that were largely cotton, according to data from the U.S. Commerce Department's Office of Textiles and Apparel.

When comparing those 2026 figures to 2010, synthetic fiber apparel import costs have climbed around 11%, while cotton apparel costs have risen 21%.

"If you're appealing to a more value-sensitive customer, you either reduce the quality, or you negotiate with suppliers to try to share some of the cost or you maybe absorb the cost … and perhaps hope to crank a higher volume of product to make up for it," said Bernstein Research senior retail analyst Aneesha Sherman.

Though polyester is cheaper, experts point out that's not necessarily a bad thing. It can help clothing hold its shape and wick away sweat.

Sherman believes the athleisure boom is a primary factor in the shift toward polyester. She said that from 2015 to 2025, sports apparel was growing at a 4.9% rate in the U.S., versus 2.6% for all apparel and footwear, according to Euromonitor data.

"There probably is some quality trade down as well happening there," Sherman said. "But I think both these trends are conflated."

Nonetheless, backlash to synthetic materials is growing. Gen Z has even repurposed the word "polyester" as an insult to mean generally low-quality or fake.

Concerns have also grown about the environment and human health surrounding petroleum-based clothing, as more research indicates microplastics can leach from polyester clothing when washed.

It's led consumers to reexamine what materials are in their clothing.

Apparel prices have risen coming out of the pandemic, as demand outpaced supply and allowed companies to raise prices, said BTIG's Stichter. Tariffs and the Iran war followed, raising costs for businesses — and prices for consumers.

Petroleum is the basis of many synthetic fibers, and oil prices have skyrocketed since the start of the Iran war.

The same factors that helped drive up prices of clothing — Covid, tariffs and war — also affected clothing quality.

"Companies had to move around their supply chains in the post-Covid period," said Stichter. "They had to find ways to take costs out, and sometimes that meant using less expensive fabrics."

Those factors compounded a more-than-decade-long shift away from manufacturing in China.

Sheng Lu, a fashion and apparel studies professor at the University of Delaware, whose research focuses on the economics of the global textile industry, said China's established history with clothing production means it has higher-skilled workers, state-of-the-art-equipment and better material management compared with emerging manufacturing destinations such as Bangladesh, Sri Lanka and Cambodia.

"They mostly serve the value market and the mass market. And I think the reason why they're not ready to make higher-quality luxury and premium products really reflects the kind of skill sets you can expect when you source from these countries," said Lu.

AlixPartners' Lapinsky said these countries that manufacture at a lower cost sometimes have to still ship the raw materials from China, which can extend the timeline to make clothing. That means taking earlier bets on trends and then discounting if products don't sell well.

"If you have to mark down that product because the consumer doesn't want it as much, your net margin might actually be worse than if you were to just invest in quality in the first place," said BTIG's Stichter.

And as material costs rise for synthetic fibers, Stichter also said she believes it could further influence companies to use more natural fabrics moving forward.

Companies prioritizing quality are winning in this environment.

Ralph Lauren's stock is up around 85% over the past two years. Around 90% of its apparel is primarily made of cotton, leather, wool, viscose, cashmere or silk, according to its 2026 sustainability report. In its fiscal 2026, the company said it sold more products at full price and reduced markdowns.

Meanwhile, direct-to-consumer clothing brand Quince, which centers its brand identity around natural materials, reached a $10 billion valuation this year.

The brand jumped 16 spots to No. 3 for best resale value in online consignment retailer ThredUp's 2025 report. Organic cotton retailer Pact jumped 20 spots on that list to No. 8, illustrating the potential role materials are playing in brand recognition.

Alternatively, the 2026 AlixPartners report, which ranks sentiment for brands in different retail categories, found shoppers are viewing some fast fashion brands less favorably. Both Shein and Zara dropped three spots — to fifth and seventh place, respectively — in terms of sentiment in the young adult specialty category.

Shein passed higher tariff costs onto shoppers in 2025 and then blamed a sales slowdown on those higher prices. The ultra-fast fashion retailer saw its year-over-year share of U.S. apparel, accessories and footwear card spending decrease in the fourth quarter of 2025 and first two quarters of 2026, according to Consumer Edge.

Shein did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

Though Zara fell in AlixPartners' Consumer Sentiment Index, it has continued to grow sales and profits year over year. Even so, Lapinsky said worsening consumer views are something to watch, even if the company is doing well.

"What we found over the years with this consumer sentiment index is that sometimes it will be a bit of a leading indicator of what's to come," said AlixPartners' Lapinsky. "I'm not saying that this is going to be true for Zara, but I think that's one thing that we'll be watching as we look in the upcoming quarters and into 2027."
