#465. 42AI comes out of stealth with $62.5M and a world model that can ‘predict human behavior’

Buy One Founder Summit Ticket, Get a Second for Free. Register now.

Founder Summit begins Nov. 4, 9:00 a.m. ET. Get your pass savings & deals before it starts. REGISTER HERE.

Here’s a question straight out of sci-fi: What if AI could predict what you were going to do before you decided to, even in a completely new situation?

Israeli startup 42AI says it is developing a “behavioral world model” that can do just that, and it has just come out of stealth with $62.5 million in seed funding. The company is initially running its predictive system at a handful of financial brokerages, where it says the model can help predict how individual traders will respond to volatile conditions.

“Behavior is not deterministic; it’s constantly probabilistic,” 42AI’s CEO and co-founder Amit Gal-Or told TechCrunch when I asked him to justify his company’s tall claim. He argues that people have underlying psychological tendencies that influence their decisions even when their circumstances change, and that’s what the startup is trying to measure and convert into data that the model can understand.

“A week from now, you might be a different person, but your tendencies will always play out in certain areas to a probability or distribution,” his co-founder and CTO Ehud Ezra added. And that, the company argues, is what the model does: show the probability of a person making a certain decision.

42AI says the model tries to figure out people’s internal motivations instead of extrapolating patterns based on their previous actions. To do that, 42AI examines how people react to seemingly unrelated events. For an investor or trader, for example, such events might include completing KYC processes, onboarding, support calls, money transfers, or trading stocks.

“When the person is talking to support or sales in the initial process […] we’re looking at the sentiment, we’re looking at the voice, we’re looking at a lot of interactions that they’re doing, how many times do they call, and so forth,” added Ezra. “And all of those data points are going into the model. Later on, you have the interactions: how you buy and sell, and when, and we’re looking at the market at that stage.”

“So we’re trying to look at how they’re looking at the world. In a world model, every individual is the world,” he added.

The company then converts those observations into mathematical representations of traits, which are then fed into a transformer-based world model that lays out probabilities for possible decisions under different circumstances. (The company uses a separate language model to generate explanations for its predictions.)

Ezra said the model is trained to preserve a person’s underlying tendencies during simulations. So if someone is usually risk-averse, but the model suddenly treats them as a risk-taker, “the target is off,” he said, explaining how the model’s training rewards are structured.

42AI spent three years developing this approach, combining psychological research with neuroscience and genetics, as well as advice from experts in psychology and PTSD, and Dan Shechtman, who was awarded the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Gal-Or said the team first studied thousands of people via psych assessments and behavioral scenarios, before adding government and biobank datasets covering more than 2 million people. Its customer datasets have since expanded to cover almost 30 million people, he said.

In live deployments, the model analyzes customers’ existing records, including transactions and support calls. Gal-Or pointed out the system doesn’t need personally identifiable information, arguing that “the model never knows who the person is.” However, it seems the company still creates individual behavioral profiles from linked customer activity.

In predicting market trades, 42AI claims its model achieves 98% accuracy, but Gal-Or clarified that this is more a measure of whether trading flows fall within a predicted range, not if each trade was predicted correctly.

He also said the model consistently achieves an F1 score of 0.8 to 0.85 for predicting trading direction and volume. (F1 score is a metric in machine learning that measures how well a classification model can balance correct predictions against false alarms and missed events.)

And it’s not like the model can work everywhere, given it’s still a machine in a box. “There’s a natural ceiling because you really don’t have access to everything that a person experiences,” Gal-Or said. Ezra explained that the system’s accuracy also suffers when it misses important situational context, like if someone chooses to change their trading choices based on advice from a friend or a colleague.

Gal-Or said 42AI is working with 10 large brokerages, who are using it to manage liquidity, margin requirements, and hedging exposure. The company is onboarding more customers, he said, and is generating more than “seven figures” in revenue, though he wouldn’t specify a number.

Customers pay the startup a flat fee, or a percentage of the value the platform helps them gain. He claimed one brokerage generated approximately $30 million in additional value through dynamic hedging.

The $62.5 million seed funding came from the founders and Infinity Group over three years, and wasn’t entirely raised from external investors, Gal-Or said. Infinity was founded by his father, Amir Gal-Or, who is also 42AI’s chairman and a co-founder. 42AI now plans to raise more money.

Beyond finance, 42AI is now exploring applications for its model in gaming, fraud prevention, and cybersecurity, and one day wants to use it to help robots and AI agents understand people. But that may be a ways off yet: Ezra acknowledged that the model needs to be trained on rules and context for each new domain.

And like with all AI tools, there’s a potentially troubling side to 42AI’s technology: If a brokerage can anticipate what its customers may do, what’s to stop someone from using that knowledge to their own benefit? Ezra admitted that this was “a valid concern” when I raised it, and the founders pointed to regulations, monitoring, and an internal ethics committee, though they didn’t explain what, if anything, would stop their customers from using the technology to gain an advantage over the very people it’s profiling.

Amit Gal-Or, however, was confident that the technology could help address such risks. “We are very proud that actually we are part of the solution; we are the solution to this problem,” he said.

#459. Marc Lore’s Wonder bets on Texas, automation and user-built restaurants as it mulls IPO

Less than a decade after its founding, food startup Wonder has seen its footprint and valuation soar.

Still, the company has even more ambitious plans ahead, like a subscription program to let diners run their own "restaurants," an aggressive expansion in Texas and a potential initial public offering.

Created by serial entrepreneur Marc Lore in 2018, Wonder advertises itself as "the best restaurants from across the country, all in one place." Diners can place takeout or delivery orders from its food hall-style locations on the Wonder-branded app or third-party platforms, including Grubhub, which Wonder now owns.

As Lore described it to CNBC, "Wonder is a vertically integrated food technology platform to make great food more accessible."

To achieve its goal, Wonder plans to open locations in urban, suburban and rural areas nationwide and aims to keep prices lower than the cost of delivered groceries. Thanks to its ownership of Grubhub, Wonder customers who order through the startup's app don't have to pay delivery fees, and its menu prices aren't inflated to cover the cost of commission fees to the delivery platform. Plus, Wonder is automating some of its kitchen functions, like assembling salads and bowls and soon sauces and beverages.

The company aims for food preparation and delivery to be fully autonomous by 2040, Lore said.

Now, Wonder has to convince diners its platform built on cost savings and choice makes it worth their while in a competitive industry.

Lore's lofty vision for Wonder has helped the company raise $3.3 billion to date, from investors like DoorDash, Alphabet's venture arm and Gwyneth Paltrow. Its Series D fundraising round earlier this year raised its pre-money valuation to $9 billion — just under sandwich chain Subway's sale price two years ago.

Wonder's next pitch to investors could be on an IPO road show, assuming that the window for companies across industries to go public reopens in the coming months.

"[The mission] is really why I want to go public and raise the capital that it'll take to build that kind of generational business, also to have a public currency to be able to do more M&A," Lore said. "But I can't talk about timing."

Lore's expertise lies in e-commerce, not restaurants.

He helped found the parent company of Diapers.com and sold it in 2010 to Amazon for $545 million. Just six years later, Walmart paid about $3 billion for his follow-up venture, Jet.com. Lore stayed at the retailer until 2021 to accelerate Walmart's e-commerce growth.

Basketball fans might also recognize Lore as an owner of the Minnesota Timberwolves. In 2021, he and baseball legend Alex Rodriguez agreed to buy the team for $1.5 billion, although the deal was not finalized until 2025. Lore recently struck a deal to shrink his investment to a minority stake "to focus on his business," Rodriguez said in a statement in August, referring to Wonder.

Lore's latest startup began with the idea of bringing food delivery to consumers' homes through sprinter vans outfitted with mobile kitchens. The trucks lasted roughly two years before Wonder phased them out in favor of brick-and-mortar locations, which could serve more food options and lower costs.

Lore's successful track record has helped him win over investors for Wonder.

"I joke around at GV that I do enterprise software investing, AI investing and anything that Marc Lore is running as an active CEO," said Dave Munichiello, managing partner of Alphabet's venture capital arm GV, who has known Lore since his Diapers.com days and invested in Jet.com through GV.

Lore himself thinks that his vision is the reason for Wonder's billions of dollars of funding.

"Everybody eats," he said. "It's defensible from AI."

Wonder has spent more than $1 billion on acquisitions, not including the deals where financial details were not disclosed. Its purchases of Grubhub, delivery and logistics provider Relay, meal-kit provider Blue Apron and buzzy New York restaurant concepts have been expensive.

But Lore said he sees a "tremendous total addressable market" for Wonder if it's able to sell high-quality food at accessible prices, particularly in exurban and rural areas with few dining options beyond fast food and gas stations.

From Virginia to Massachusetts, 170 Wonder storefronts have appeared over the last three years. Next year, the company plans to enter Texas for the first time — with a goal of at least 100 locations in the state by the end of 2027.

Wonder prepares its food at commissary kitchens, then transports it to each individual location, where employees unseal, cook and finish the menu items. Its storefronts are all electric, without gas or venting, so Wonder can quickly move into a new space and cut down on renovations and red tape.

Its locations serve menu items from more than two dozen "restaurants," including Dabba Indian Kitchen, Tejas Barbecue and Pop Salad. Celebrity chefs including Bobby Flay, Michael Symon and Marcus Samuelsson receive up-front payments and equity in Wonder to let the company use their names for some of those concepts. More recent additions include acquisitions such as Blue Ribbon Fried Chicken and Salt Hank's, brands with established followings.

With its wide variety of options housed under one roof, Wonder has drawn comparisons to ghost kitchens, the once-buzzy startups without dine-in seating that flamed out as food delivery orders fell after the Covid pandemic. Once considered a potential disruptor to the restaurant industry, ghost kitchen companies such as Kitchen United have sought bankruptcy protection and wound down their operations.

Wonder has never marketed itself as a ghost kitchen, instead calling itself "a new kind of food hall" or a "modern food court" before landing on a "vertically integrated food technology platform."

Starting Nov. 17, the company plans to take the next step with Wonder Create. Influencers, entrepreneurs and brands can pay $10 a month to start their own "restaurant" based out of a Wonder location and earn a portion of the revenue from orders. Artificial intelligence will help them make their brand, including name, logo, pricing and recipes.

"It could be a restaurant for you and your friends, or you could be an influencer that has a following," Lore said.

He wants Wonder Create to democratize restaurant ownership — minus the capital, overhead costs or oversight of kitchen operations. The platform will eventually be available to the public, with users deciding menu items based on Wonder's pantry.

Besides Wonder Create, the company is also using AI for another initiative. MEL, a proprietary AI platform, can use a customer's health information, like data from an Oura ring or Whoop tracker or even blood biomarkers, to prepare breakfast, lunch and dinner for its user.

MEL is not currently available to Wonder customers, although Lore uses AI to advise his own meals, like the chicken muffin he ate for breakfast on a Tuesday in October.

While Wonder has won over venture capitalists, the battle for diners' stomachs and wallets is tougher.

Budget-conscious consumers have been dining out less often and becoming pickier when they do so. And while food delivery is still more popular than it was before the pandemic, consumers have pulled back on delivery orders compared with their Covid-era peak. Fees and high menu prices, which offset eateries' commission fees with apps, make delivery even more expensive than dining at a restaurant.

"They made this company work well in the hardest place in the country to stand up this model: New York," Munichiello said. "You have so much choice in New York."

Ultimately, Wonder wants to win over the rest of the U.S. — and public market investors.

In March, the company hired Gabrielle Scheibe Rabinovitch, previously at payment processor Worldpay, as its chief financial officer as it prepares to go public.

Lore declined to comment on the timing of a potential IPO to CNBC, although he has previously told Fortune that the company will be "ready and prepared to go public early next year."

Since buying Blue Apron and Grubhub, Wonder has been operating at a financial loss, The Information previously reported. It plans to bring down the price of its meals by leaning into automation and drone delivery. In the long term, that plan could cut costs, although laying the groundwork won't be cheap.

In recent years, investors have grown less tolerant of startups pursuing growth at all costs, according to Avery Marquez, director of investment strategies at Renaissance Capital. To sway investors, unprofitable companies have to show they are taking steps toward profitability — or that the growth will continue long after the IPO.

However, few indie developers have gone so far as to launch an alternative to Amazon’s Kindle e-books store, which remains the industry leader, with an 83% share of the market when Kindle+ (formerly Kindle Unlimited) is included in the stats.

One of Kindle’s bigger advantages is that it offers an end-to-end solution for everything related to reading e-books, from the hardware devices to the book-tracking log. The session tracking taps into a different psychological mechanism. It makes the reading you’re doing legible and, as the adage goes, that which is measured is improved.”

The company adds that the most consistent feedback from users is that the app has led them to read more without willpower tricks like goal-setting, streaks, or scheduling.

With publishers’ permission, the app will support AI-powered features as well, like session recaps, definitions and term lookups, word and phrase translations, and a location-aware ability to ask questions about the part you’re reading. (So far, only HarperCollins has opted in, though.)

Unlike Goodreads’ locked-down ecosystem, Readwise is embracing open technology. This includes a public API and an MCP server. The latter supports AI agents that could be used to research book recommendations on your behalf, use your Readwise data to create a books page on your personal website, or even wire up your hackable e-reader to log your sessions automatically, depending on how involved your AI use cases may be.

The new Readwise Books app will remain free, with some AI features later going behind a paywall. Its prior apps for reading and tracking highlights are either $7.99 per month each or $9.99 per month for both.

RSI is the point at which AI grows so good at training itself that it spawns a dramatic acceleration of AI capabilities. To the AI doomers, RSI represents the cataclysm from which there is no return, in which AI builds itself into something powerful enough that it slips beyond human control and may be indifferent to the damage it causes while achieving its goals.

To AI enthusiasts, RSI, or some version of AI reliably using itself to improve, represents the technology’s promise, where it becomes a tool of unimaginable productivity, scientific discovery, and more.

Anthropic seems to believe that RSI is both a promise and a peril. It says in the S-1 that it uses AI in its R&D, but it cautions: “Using AI to help build more capable AI could also pose a considerable safety risk, particularly if it sharply accelerates the pace of progress. For example, our internal estimates and our expectations regarding future models, rather than our current models, indicate that we may imminently meet our Responsible Scaling Policy’s definition of “dramatically accelerated progress attributable to AI.” We have not designated any of our current models as exceeding this threshold, and we continue to assess the underlying data.

Skeptics might point out that an S-1 functions as both disclosure and marketing, and that claims of being close to a model so powerful that it can build itself could fall into the latter category.

Nevertheless, there’s no question that Claude’s capabilities, especially in terms of coding, math, and cybersecurity, have grown leaps and bounds this year. If Anthropic thinks the possibility of RSI is close, maybe we should believe it and act — perhaps govern — accordingly.

The startup aims to solve this problem through an approach known as continual learning. Businesses using Trajectory can start with an open source AI model and connect the platform to their applications, allowing it to collect feedback on how the model performs. Apple’s AI-powered “Audio Intelligence” technology for the Apple Watch is now rolling out into beta testing, initially for developers in the iOS 27.3 update, after first being announced at the company’s Surprise and Shine hardware event in September. The technology includes a set of four opt-in AI features that allow Apple Watch Series 12 wearers to detect important sounds, identify songs, catch up on conversations, and take notes.

The sound recognition feature will help deaf and hard-of-hearing users identify important sounds like sirens, alarms, or doorbells from their watch, while the song recognition feature taps into Apple’s Shazam app to detect music playing in the background and identify the song name and artist.

However, the more potentially controversial features are those that listen to human conversations: Live Rewind and Siri Recaps.

With Live Rewind, the idea is to let you go back in time to see what someone just said, in case you missed it. For instance, if someone was giving you verbal directions or reading a long list of specials at a restaurant, you could double-press on the Watch’s digital crown to see what was said in the last 15 seconds as a text snippet displayed on the watch face. You can either view this snippet and then do nothing, and it will disappear permanently. Or you can save it to the standalone Siri app that shipped with iOS 27 for later access, which might be helpful if you’re trying to remember someone’s name or take a quick note from a fast-paced presentation.

Apple says that users will also be able to tap an “Ask Siri” option that appears below the text snippet if they have questions about something that was said — like a technical term they don’t understand, or perhaps some sauce option the waiter mentioned that they’re unfamiliar with, among other things.

When you double-press the digital crown, an audible chime appears and an animation displays on the watch. This is meant to signal to people through the combination of the gesture, sound, and the visual display that you’ve recorded something that was said.

These snippets will be deleted automatically after 30 seconds if you don’t save them.

The other option, Siri Recaps, takes this ambient listening technology even further by allowing Apple Watch owners to take high-level notes of their conversations.

Importantly, Apple says it’s not identifying speakers, saving the raw audio, or providing word-for-word transcriptions. Instead, the feature summarizes a conversation with a short sentence and some bullet points that identify key points. For instance, depending on the topic, the bullet points might include things like important dates or deadlines, pricing or payment plan information, and other factual information.

Apple suggested the feature could be used in scenarios like work meetings, parent-teacher conferences, personal training sessions, or other places where you may need to remember what tasks you have to complete or things that were said. Essentially, it’s supposed to mimic a user’s ability to jot down their own notes — without adding emotion or tone.

This could be helpful particularly at times when you aren’t able to write up your own notes, either because your hands are full or you’re trying to concentrate on whatever’s being said at the time. The recaps won’t record conversations when you’re listening to music with your headphones or using their noise cancellation features.

At the end of the day, you’re prompted to review your saved Siri Recaps when the feature is enabled, and then choose which, if any, you want to save. These notes can be saved to your Notes app or even exported to your Journal app, if they’re more personal in nature. If you do nothing, the recaps will delete themselves within seven days.

This reminder to review the Siri Recaps you’ve recorded also appears when you first unlock your Apple Watch in the morning, too.

Users can opt to enable the Siri Recaps manually, or they can configure a schedule, such as only having the recapping take place while at work, or making sure that they never run at night. Even if they’re running on a schedule, you can still turn off the recapping feature at any time from the Control Center.

Apple notes that Siri Recaps has been designed to omit sensitive information like government-assigned identifiers, financial and authentication data, certain personal identifiers, and any language that would promote self-harm.

The Audio Intelligence features for the Apple Watch Series 12 and Apple Watch Ultra 4 rely on a combination of Apple’s hardware, software, and services. This includes the S11 chip, built-in microphone, and the Apple Intelligence models that run on the iPhone, as well as Apple’s Private Cloud Compute.

The always-on processor includes a secure enclave — a hardware-protected memory space that can’t be accessed by the rest of the operating system — where a dedicated audio buffer processes the raw audio. This allows the audio to be processed without it actually being accessible to anyone, including Apple. The audio is only accessible to the paired iPhone through a secure pairing mechanism between it and the watch.

To work, lightweight AI models on the S11 chip on the Apple Watch detect if there’s a conversation happening that the wearer is a part of, which is then transferred to the secure enclave on the iPhone. On-device processing on the iPhone turns the audio into condensed text, meaning the text of the conversation with the filler words removed. This condensed text is then encrypted and sent to Apple’s Private Cloud Compute (PCC) to create the recap of the conversation that is sent back to the phone. (The recap and any summarizations used to create it are deleted from PCC immediately afterwards).

The recaps you can then access on your iPhone are only accessible to you, not to Apple. And they’re encrypted when they’re synced between your multiple Apple devices.

Similarly, Apple Watch transfers audio to the iPhone for processing to create the 15-second snippets for Live Rewind.

Apple says none of these features should impact the battery life of your watch or phone. The features will later roll out to the public beta for broader testing before becoming publicly available to all.

#333. A 'canary in the coal mine': Measles resurgence exposes weaknesses in U.S. public health, experts say

For nearly two years, the U.S. has had to swallow an uncomfortable reality: A highly infectious disease it once eliminated is spreading again.

More than 4,400 measles cases and at least three deaths have been reported nationwide this year, marking the country's worst outbreak in more than three decades. The spike followed nearly 2,300 cases in 2025. But many public health experts argue that the return of measles isn't the only concerning development — it's also what the resurgence signals about America that's causing alarm.

Measles is often described as one of the most contagious viruses, and it is uniquely unforgiving of weaknesses in public health defenses. A safe and highly effective vaccine has existed for decades.

But the virus's spread exposes declining vaccination rates, eroding trust in health institutions and growing challenges in persuading people to protect themselves, their children and their communities, experts said.

Together, those vulnerabilities have made measles a revealing test of the country's public health defenses. For some experts, the resurgence of a disease the U.S. eliminated more than two decades ago is a warning sign of its ability to contain future threats, from other vaccine-preventable diseases to emerging pathogens.

"Measles is the canary in the coal mine for so many different reasons," said Jennifer Nuzzo, an epidemiology professor and director of the Pandemic Center at Brown University School of Public Health. "It's the canary in the coal mine for other vaccine-preventable illnesses, where we could see a return of things that used to harm kids, but also for very acute, no-notice dangerous infectious disease emergencies."

The U.S. is on track to lose its official measles elimination status during an upcoming review by the Pan American Health Organization, which will meet in Washington, D.C., in mid-November.

"It says a lot about a country if it is unable to control measles," said Nuzzo, who is one of 10 experts CNBC spoke with about the ongoing outbreak and its implications.

The U.S. has the tools to prevent measles from spreading. But pockets of low vaccination coverage are leaving the country vulnerable.

The measles, mumps and rubella, or MMR, vaccine is about 97% effective against measles after two doses, while a single dose is 93% effective, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Yet data aggregated by the CDC shows that vaccination coverage for that shot among U.S. kindergarteners fell to 92.4% in the 2025-2026 school year, which is below the 95% threshold public health officials generally recommend to prevent sustained transmission.

That translates to 280,000 kindergarteners who did not complete their MMR vaccination series, according to the CDC. Childhood vaccination exemptions in the U.S. also reached a record high 4.2% for incoming kindergartners during the 2025-2026 school year, up from 3.6% the previous year, the agency says.

"The bottom line is that not enough people are vaccinating their kids," said Brown's Nuzzo.

While some cases stem from international travel, much of the recent spread has been driven by local transmission in "too many" communities where vaccination coverage falls below 95%, she added.

Among states across the U.S. collecting MMR vaccine data, 67% of counties and jurisdictions now have immunization rates below 95%, according to a September investigation by NBC News.

Recent U.S. outbreaks have often occurred in "closely knit communities" where people have frequent contact and low vaccination coverage, said Dr. William Moss, an epidemiology professor at Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health.

But declining vaccination rates do not mean most Americans have turned against immunization. Polling suggests broad support for childhood vaccines. A July KFF survey found that many Americans sit in a more uncertain middle ground about the shots, leaving room for conflicting information to influence their decisions.

"This is not an edge-case issue," Nuzzo said, adding that the vast majority of parents still vaccinate their children.

Even if outbreaks are concentrated in a minority of communities, it can create a "dangerous situation" far from those areas, she said. Infants too young to be vaccinated and people who cannot receive immunizations for medical reasons can be particularly vulnerable when the virus spreads into communities with high vaccination rates.

Those risks come as the Trump administration reshapes federal vaccine policy.

President Donald Trump signed an executive order in August suggesting that the combined MMR vaccine be split into separate shots, while repeating false claims about its safety. Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has also overhauled a key federal advisory committee responsible for recommending childhood immunizations, though the changes are frozen amid ongoing litigation.

Several experts told CNBC that such moves risk further eroding confidence in vaccines and weakening efforts to protect children.

Public health infrastructure was also "really decimated by Covid," and the CDC in particular lost many experienced scientists and physicians "who had spent their lives working on vaccine-preventable diseases," said Scott Weaver, a virologist who is the research director of Galveston National Laboratory.

He added that the CDC, as of September, is also no longer immediately incorporating state-reported "measles-associated" deaths into its national published tally, making it harder to track outbreaks in real time. The agency lists three measles-related deaths in 2026, but Pennsylvania has reported at least five deaths.

"We have folks in federal health leadership right now that are throwing gasoline on the fire, and that is not helping the situation," said Jess Steier, a public health scientist and founder and CEO of Unbiased Science, an organization that aims to make complex scientific concepts understandable.

If the U.S. fails to materially improve vaccination rates, the measles resurgence could extend beyond ongoing outbreaks.

At "current" state-level vaccination rates, measles could become endemic again in the U.S., resulting in an estimated 851,300 cases over 25 years, according to a large-scale epidemiological model published in JAMA in April 2025 by researchers at Stanford and other institutions. Under that scenario, the model predicts the measles cases could lead to 170,200 hospitalizations and 2,550 deaths over that period.

The model, which simulated infectious disease spread in the U.S. at various childhood vaccination levels, relies on immunization data from 2004 to 2023 for that baseline scenario. The model assumes that diseases are introduced by someone who travels abroad and brings them back, most commonly a U.S. citizen who isn't vaccinated.

While many people are currently immune through vaccination, outbreaks can increase in size and frequency if immunization rates decline over a longer period.

Michael Osterholm, an epidemiologist and the director of the Center for Infectious Disease Research and Policy at the University of Minnesota, said he views the measles situation in Canada as a direct warning for the U.S.

Canada lost its official measles elimination status in November after a multi-province outbreak that generated more than 5,400 cases across 10 jurisdictions in 2025. Canada has a population of more than 40 million people.

"We have a population of 350 million. If you extrapolate, if we had the very same experience that Canada had over the next year or two, that would be up to 50,000 cases of measles in the U.S.," Osterholm said. "I think that's a potential future we have to be mindful of."

An even larger measles outbreak isn't the only potential problem.

Vaccination coverage for other childhood diseases has similarly declined. The share of U.S. kindergartners vaccinated against polio fell to 92.4% in the 2025-2026 school year, while coverage for diphtheria, tetanus and acellular pertussis, or DTaP, dropped to 92%, according to CDC data.

The U.S. has already seen a sharp rise in pertussis, or whooping cough. U.S. reported cases reached roughly 43,300 in 2024 and nearly 28,000 in 2025, up substantially from pandemic-era levels, according to the CDC. Brown's Nuzzo said the resurgence reflects, in part, fewer people getting vaccinated and the fact that protection from the pertussis vaccine wanes over time.

The simulation model published in JAMA also examined how falling childhood immunization rates could affect the future spread of several diseases previously eliminated from the U.S. through vaccines. Under current vaccination levels, the researchers found that diseases including rubella and polio were unlikely to become endemic in the U.S.

But in a hypothetical scenario in which childhood vaccination rates fell by 50%, the model projected 9.9 million cases of rubella, 4.3 million cases of polio and 197 cases of diphtheria over 25 years.

Rubella can cause devastating complications when an infection during pregnancy passes to a developing fetus, while polio can cause paralysis and death, said Dr. Nathan Lo, assistant professor of infectious diseases at Stanford Medicine.

The projections are not predictions that these outbreaks will occur, but they illustrate how much risks could grow if vaccination coverage deteriorates.

"What vaccines often buy you is not just the protection, but also less uncertainty," said Mathew Kiang, an assistant professor of epidemiology and population health at Stanford Medicine who also worked on the simulation model. "I think even more frightening is just the really wide uncertainty that we just wouldn't be sure of what could happen."

The measles resurgence is also raising questions about how effective the U.S. can be at detecting and responding to emerging public health threats before they spread.

"Public health is all about prevention," said Dr. Manisha Juthani, president of the Association of State and Territorial Health Officials and commissioner of the Connecticut Department of Public Health. She said the country must maintain its ability to detect threats, invest in surveillance, and recognize that infectious diseases do not respect national borders.

Those capabilities are crucial for curbing emerging pathogens, especially when scientists initially know little about how a new disease spreads, how severe it is or how to treat it. A pathogen could spread before vaccines or treatments are available, making early detection, clear public communication and coordination especially important.

Other experts argue that weaknesses in those capabilities could also have implications for the country's defenses against deliberate biological attacks.

In an April 2025 op-ed, Steph Batalis, a research fellow at Georgetown University's Center for Security and Emerging Technology, argued that the measles outbreak exposed a broader vulnerability in the U.S.' biodefense strategy and, specifically, a weakened ability to deter potential biological attacks.

She argued that the country's struggle to contain a known, vaccine-preventable disease "might only embolden those who see our public health vulnerabilities as an opportunity."

But Osterholm, who works extensively on bioterrorism, said low MMR immunization rates and rising measles cases do not necessarily indicate that Americans would refuse a vaccine or antibiotic during a biological emergency. The shots and drugs used to counter biological threats can differ from those routinely administered to the public, he said, and the prospect of a severe outbreak could prompt urgency among the public to seek protection.

That point highlights an important limitation: The measles resurgence by itself does not establish whether the U.S. could withstand entirely different or novel public health threats.

But as a disease the U.S. knows how to prevent spreads, experts say the stakes of addressing those vulnerabilities extend to whatever might come next.

#286. Stellantis stock rises 13% as automaker's turnaround plan, cost cuts gain traction

Shares of Stellantis were up Wednesday as the European car giant's third-quarter results showed it was making progress with its 60 billion euro turnaround plan.

Third-quarter revenues rose 1% to 37.6 billion euros from a year ago, the Chrysler and Jeep owner said on Wednesday, while adjusted operating income rose 184% to 256 million euros from 90 million euros a year earlier.

The automaker's industrial free cash flow improved to negative 4 billion euros, up from negative 4.3 billion the year prior.

Stellantis on Wednesday also reconfirmed its 2026 guidance and target to achieve positive free cash flow next year, which is a main focus for investors.

"Q3 was soft, but consensus expectations were far lower. Key here is that '26 Guidance was maintained while other auto OEMs and suppliers are cutting on elevated costs," RBC Capital Markets analyst Tom Narayan said Wednesday in an investor note.

Stellantis stock on the New York Stock Exchange rose 13% on Wednesday to close at $4.69 a share. Shares in Milan closed at 4.28 euros, up 15.4%. Shares in both markets remain sharply down since the beginning of the year.

Stellantis CEO Antonio Filosa told investors Wednesday that the company is ahead of schedule on its turnaround plan, which was announced in May. He said Stellantis expects to exceed its previously announced cost-cutting efforts of 6 billion euros under the plan by 2028, including 2.4 billion euros to start 2027.

"We are very confident in reaffirming our '26 guidance. Those include '27 free cash flow positive," Filosa said Wednesday in response to concerns about the carmaker's ability to achieve its free cash flow target.

Stellantis' 2026 guidance includes a mid-single-digit percentage increase in net revenue and a low-single-digit adjusted operating margin.

Stellantis' turnaround plan includes investing 36 billion euros in its portfolio of automotive brands, with 60% of the cash to go towards its struggling North America sector. It also plans to introduce new vehicles, refresh models, and incorporate new technologies in its products.

Stellantis is targeting roughly 23% revenue growth, from 154 billion euros last year to 190 billion euros by 2030, with a 7% adjusted operating margin by then.

European carmakers, including Stellantis, have been struggling in China as domestic brands like Xiaomi, BYD and Geely gain ground and intensify competition.

Just this week, German luxury carmaker Porsche said it would reduce its exposure to China as car sales decline in the region. Meanwhile, Mercedes-Benz said its third-quarter car sales slumped due to a "challenging market environment," in the country.

Earlier this month, Filosa told CNBC that European regulators need to do more to incentivize carmakers to manufacture vehicles in the region to fend off Chinese rivals.

Filosa called for regulation that "incentivizes, rewards, and protects the ones that are able to demonstrate that they design cars, engineer cars."
