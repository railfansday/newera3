#472. Ferrari raises 2026 guidance after Q3 earnings beat

Ferrari on Tuesday raised its 2026 guidance after the sports car maker slightly beat Wall Street's third-quarter expectations.

Here's how Ferrari performed in the third quarter compared with average estimates compiled by LSEG:

Ferrari raised its guidance to revenue of 7.65 billion euros, up from 7.6 billion euros; adjusted earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization to 3 billion euros, up from 2.97 billion euros; and adjusted EPS to at least 9.85 euros, up from at least 9.68 euros.

The automaker also upgraded its expectations for industrial free cash flow to up to 1.65 billion euros, up from previous expectations for at least 1.55 billion euros.

This is the second consecutive quarter Ferrari has raised its 2026 guidance, despite vehicle shipments being lower by 318 units through the first nine months of the year, at 10,170 vehicles, compared with last year. "Our order book now reaches into 2028, and we still have two more launches to come this year. In addition, we continue to create engaging events around the world to further tighten the bonds with our clients and partners across Racing, Sports Cars and Lifestyle," Ferrari CEO Benedetto Vigna said in a release.

RBC Capital Markets analyst Tom Narayan said Tuesday that Ferrari's results were largely in line with Wall Street expectations. However, he said shares may be "under slight pressure given some were hoping for a bigger guidance raise, but expect it to get better once folks realize these results are healthy."

Ferrari's U.S. stock initially declined on the results before turning positive to close Tuesday at $414.77 a share, up 2.9%.

#442. Amazon’s new basic Kindle proves you don’t need all the bells and whistles

In October, Amazon announced its refreshed Kindle lineup, its first significant design overhaul in years. The highlights included a new edge-to-edge design that eliminated traditional raised bezels, thinner and lighter Paperwhite and Colorsoft models with better displays, and page-turning accessories like a Bluetooth remote and a magnetic cover with page-turn buttons.

However, given the impact of the global memory-chip shortage driven by the AI boom, the new Kindles also debuted with pricing that had increased by $40 to $50 across the lineup. That makes it more difficult for the average consumer to consider buying one of the more premium devices.

For that reason, the Kindle I most wanted to get my hands on was the 6-inch entry-level device, which starts at $169.99 for the ad-free version. With the price hikes, this is the Kindle that will remain the most affordable to new e-reader buyers.

The question I weighed was whether or not the basic Kindle is even worth buying at this price when there are arguably better Kindles on the market. The answer, after a couple of weeks of testing, is yes — if you can live without the bells and whistles and prefer a smaller, lighter device.

On the 6-inch Kindle, there’s no warm light to reduce eye strain, no color display, no auto-adjusting brightness, no option to use the page-turn cover, and it’s not waterproof. Obviously, because of its size, the screen is smaller, as is the battery. Battery life is 6 weeks compared with the Paperwhite’s 12 weeks or Colorsoft’s 8 weeks.

Still, even this basic Kindle has seen improvements of its own, mostly from a physical design perspective.

The plastic 2026 Kindle is 11% lighter and 14% thinner than the 2024 model, weighing 140g versus 158g and measuring 6.85mm thick versus 8mm. While this seems like a small change, the 18-gram difference is noticeable and particularly welcome for a device you’re likely to carry in your purse or bag. Plus, it makes it easier to hold for hours on end when you’re really binging your way through a book.

(The aluminum version, meanwhile, is slightly heavier at 162g, or 4 grams more than the 2024 Kindle, though it still benefits from the slimmer design.)

And despite being the “starter” Kindle, the 6-inch basic device still received the new flush-front screen update. This makes it more comfortable to hold in your hand, especially for those long reading sessions. Amazon explained that you don’t have to worry about no longer having a bezel to rest your thumb upon, either, as the new Kindles have been updated to detect a dead zone in the capacitive touch that will readjust to account for the fact that your finger is there.

In practice, that doesn’t mean you can just press your thumb down anywhere on the Kindle’s screen as you hold it — doing so would register this as an attempt to highlight or look up a word. Instead, it means you can rest your thumb or fingers on the outer edge of the Kindle’s screen (the colored border) without triggering an unintentional tap, even though some of your finger will be touching part of the Kindle’s capacitive screen.

In terms of the software, the new basic Kindle is a bit snappier than its 2024 predecessor. This is most noticeable when opening books, which Amazon claims to be 30% faster.

If you’re coming from the 2019 Kindle Basic, the 2026 version is an even bigger upgrade, as you gain USB-C, more storage (from 4GB to 8GB to now 16GB or 32GB), the new flush screen, and the screen resolution jumps from 167 ppi to 300 ppi. It’s faster, thinner, and lighter, too, and the battery lasts weeks longer.

Another perk about the basic Kindle beyond its small size (this thing fits in your jeans’ back pocket!), is that you can now pay a little more for an upgraded version with an aluminum backing instead of plastic and more storage. The new $189.99 aluminum option comes in Seagrass and Alpine Sky hues, and ships with 32GB of storage. Meanwhile, the $169.99 plastic model offers 16GB of storage.

However, even if you go for the less expensive option, you can at least now pick a fun color other than black (aka Graphite), as Amazon has introduced a cheery purple option called Ube. (I almost wish I’d gone for this one, just for the whimsy of it, even though I prefer the feel of the aluminum.)

I should point out that you can use the Kindle Click, the new Bluetooth remote for page turning, with the basic Kindle, but I’m not sure why you’d need to, if not for some accessibility-related reason. The 6-inch Kindle was already the smallest, and now it’s lighter and easier to hold, too. (But perhaps I just don’t get the appeal of the remote? I’ve barely used the remote with my Kobo either!)

The big thing to consider here is whether you want to spend this much on a Kindle that lacks a warm light. While this is something of a personal preference, for some readers, no warm light is a dealbreaker. The warm light shifts the color tone of the Kindle’s display from a cooler bluish-white to a softer amber glow, which is meant to help reduce eye strain and improve reading conditions in low light or at night.

Whether you need this feature depends on where you like to read and how long your sessions tend to be. If you read nightly in bed with little light, you might prefer paying extra for a $199.99 16GB Kindle Paperwhite. (That version only comes in Graphite. The 32GB Paperwhite can come in Seaglass, too, but is $249.99).

If you often read by the pool or on vacation, you also might want to upgrade to a waterproof Kindle.

But if you can live without the extra features and want the best purse- or pocket-sized device, the 6-inch Kindle is still a viable option.

#434. Parents want safer phones for kids. These companies are answering the call.

Buy One Founder Summit Ticket, Get a Second for Free. Register now.

Founder Summit begins Nov. 4, 9:00 a.m. ET. Get your pass savings & deals before it starts. REGISTER HERE.

As parents become increasingly concerned about the potential dangers of unrestricted smartphone access for children, a growing number of companies are developing devices specifically designed for kids.

Today’s kid-friendly phones typically feature touchscreens, cameras, and communication capabilities similar to regular smartphones. However, the software on these devices is heavily modified to remove or restrict access to web browsers, social media, app stores, and other distractions.

Parents can usually manage these devices through a companion app, allowing them to approve contacts, monitor locations, set screen time limits, select available apps, and, in some cases, receive alerts regarding cyberbullying, explicit content, or online predators.

In addition to these modified devices, some companies offer a more minimalist approach, creating devices that focus solely on calling and texting.

There are also home phones designed specifically for children, like Tin Can and the recently launched Pinwheel Home.

Here’s a look at the major players shaping the kid-phone market.

The Bark Phone is probably one of the most popular kid-specific smartphones on the market.

Built on Samsung Galaxy hardware and powered by Bark’s monitoring software, it only allows calls and texts with approved contacts. Bark continuously scans texts, emails, photos, and supported apps for signs of cyberbullying, grooming, suicidal ideation, sexual content, and other potential concerns, then sends alerts to parents.

A big standout feature is that parents can gradually unlock web browsing, apps, and other features as their child matures. The phone also includes GPS tracking and screen-time management.

Current pricing for the standard model is $240, plus a required wireless plan beginning at $29/month. Higher-tier plans add internet access and unlimited texting.

Gabb is a well-known name in the kid-friendly phone category. Unlike Bark, however, Gabb focuses less on monitoring content and more on eliminating online risks altogether.

Its phones don’t include social media, a browser, or an app store. Instead, children get calling, texting, and a carefully curated selection of preloaded apps, including a camera, calendar, and calculator. The company also touts its “worry-free” music streaming library with millions of songs.

Additionally, parents can view location and manage certain device settings, while optional services such as “Gabb Guard” help block spam calls and unwanted texts.

Current phone pricing starts around $159.99, with cellular service starting around $24.99 per month.

With Pinwheel, parents approve every app, control contacts, schedule screen time, create custom routines, and monitor location history.

One standout feature is the ability to create different “modes” throughout the day. For example, limiting the phone to calling and navigation during school hours before unlocking additional features after homework.

Pinwheel phones start around $119, while the Caregiver Portal subscription begins at $14.99 per month without cell service. (Cellular service can be purchased separately through participating carriers.)

The company recently launched two landline phones for $68 and $79. It also offers a smartwatch at $160.

Launched in 2022, Teracube offers a tailored version of Android known as Thrive OS. This enables parents to approve every app download, filter web browsing, set app-specific and overall screen-time limits, monitor location, and create customized routines for school or bedtime. However, because it has more traditional smartphone features, it’s often recommended for older children.

Thrive currently sells for $99, with plans starting at $35 per month.

Unlike the other products in this roundup, Ooma MyPhone isn’t a mobile phone at all. This kid-focused home phone is designed as a modern replacement for the traditional household landline.

The service allows calls only between approved contacts through its Trusted Circle feature, offers scheduled Quiet Hours, provides address-based 911 service, sends emergency alerts when 911 is dialed, and lets parents review call logs through an online portal. There are no apps, internet browsing, texting, or social media.

The phone costs $99.99, while service starts at $7.99 per month.

For $100, Tin Can resembles a landline phone with a distinctive tin can design, which is the inspiration behind its name.

A third reason is his résumé, which includes a stint with the Coast Guard, nine years in the Secret Service, and CISO jobs at TIAA and Home Depot before he ever thought about investing.

Now Ward is raising a second fund, targeting roughly the same size as the first one. This time, though, he’ll be doing it without Talpaz, who left in April along with another early colleague, leaving Ward as the firm’s sole general partner. He’s betting on himself at a moment when early-stage cybersecurity startups are raising more money than later-stage rivals for the first time in more than a decade.

His unemotional approach to exits traces back to an unlikely career. Ward says he grew up in northwest New Jersey between two farms, in a family that couldn’t afford college for his older brothers. After high school, he joined the Coast Guard, inspired by a commercial of a man jumping out of a helicopter, though he says he barely knew how to swim at the time. He spent four years in search and rescue.

Afterward, he studied criminal justice at Seton Hall in New Jersey and thought half-heartedly about law school. “I didn’t know what I wanted to do,” Ward told me last week over Zoom, wearing a pale hooded sweatshirt and looking more like a basketball coach than a venture capitalist. “I was kind of lost. After graduating in 1998, he says, he mailed out some 300 résumés but no one responded. A Seton Hall professor who was also a chaplain to Secret Service personnel, as well as to members of the FBI and CIA, began introducing him around, and the Secret Service called first.

The Secret Service has two primary missions: protecting people and investigating financial crimes. Ward gravitated to investigations, then to “cyber” after the September 11 attacks in 2001. That year, he married a former college classmate, an American-born Syrian Muslim. Colleagues warned him the background check would be “miserable.” (They’re still happily married.)

What came next took Ward’s life down an unexpected path. Stationed in New York, he arrested a hacker who’d pulled off the largest credit card data theft of that era. #403. Coordinated disturbances haunt grids worldwide. Here’s how researchers solved the mystery.

Buy One Founder Summit Ticket, Get a Second for Free. Register now.

Founder Summit begins Nov. 4, 9:00 a.m. ET. Get your pass savings & deals before it starts. REGISTER HERE.

For years, mysterious electrical anomalies have been flummoxing grid managers around the world. Every 60 seconds, the frequency on the grid would drop a tiny bit.

Even weirder: The sags happened at the exact same time on power grids across countries and continents.

It was a mystery that Dirk Witthaut, a researcher at Forschungszentrum Jülich in Germany, couldn’t pass up.

Germany’s largest transmission system operator, Tennet Germany, brought the puzzle to Witthaut, and it took years of investigative and modeling work to determine the cause. From Europe to Australia, the U.S. to Malaysia, the same pattern shows up in the data.

Such frequency drops aren’t unheard of, but they are troubling. And the regularity with which they have been occurring can make the phenomenon feel even more ominous.

The grid’s orderly operation relies on the frequency being kept within a certain range. If the oscillations get get too large, or if grid operators don’t know the cause and respond improperly, they can lead to blackouts. Even if the sags don’t cause blackouts, they can damage equipment in power plants if they go unchecked, especially in hydropower turbines.

Hydropower plants have historically been a main driver of so-called ultra-low frequency oscillations (UFLOs). Every turbine vibrates, and if those vibrations occur with a certain regularity, known as the resonant frequency, they can magnify, sometimes to dangerous levels. Think of a wine glass breaking before an opera singer’s mighty pipes — that’s resonance.

So Witthaut and his team started investigating hydropower, the most obvious potential culprit. They used a computer model of a hydropower plant control system on the Scandinavian grid to test the hypothesis.

While they managed to find conditions that could generate a resonance that peaked once per minute, they couldn’t explain why it persisted for so long. Typically, resonances in mechanical systems will come and go, and so will the accompanying signal on the electrical grid. But this frequency drop kept happening minute after minute after minute. Every 60 seconds, without fail.

It then dawned on the team that anything operating with such clockwork required, well, a clock. How else could they explain the uncanny timing?

To that hypothesis, the researchers started close to home, installing more than 80 smart power quality meters around the Jülich campus. The research site is outfitted with medium- and low-voltage distribution lines, transformers, solar panels, and battery energy storage systems that serve as uninterruptible power supplies (UPS). It also has a high-performance computer system as well as the usual office computers powered by much smaller UPS devices. The power quality meters sampled a range of attributes, including frequency, voltage, current, and power, once every three seconds.

Before long, the research team had its answer. The measurements recorded on the transformer attached to the battery energy storage system peaked every minute, just like on the bigger power grids.

It turns out that many stationary batteries, from data center UPSes to large grid-scale batteries, check their state-of-charge every minute. Since these devices are internet-connected, their clocks are in near perfect synchronicity. Every time one checks its charge, the disturbance is imperceptible on the wide grid. But when they all do it together, that disruption becomes apparent.

To find evidence that UPSes were the main culprit on the grid, Witthaut and his team turned to data provided by grid operators. They found their answer in Ireland.

There, data centers have grown to consume a large fraction of the country’s electricity. By 2022, data centers represented 18% of the load on Ireland’s grid, and each facility is served by a fleet of UPSes. Sure enough, between 2016 and 2021, the minute-by-minute frequency drop on the Irish grid quadrupled from 3 milli-Hertz (mHz) in 2016 to 12 mHz in 2021. More recently, some drops have exceeded 20 mHz.

Ireland’s 50 Hz grid is built to withstand swings of plus or minus 200 mHz, though the grid operator would rather limit the swings to 100 mHz. In that range, 20 mHz isn’t a lot, but data centers aren’t the only loads on the grid. If other loads also cause the frequency to sag, the extra bump from every UPS checking its state-of-charge could push the grid toward blackout conditions.

The problem is entirely preventable. Rather than check state-of-charge exactly on the minute, Witthaus and team recommend that UPS and grid-scale battery manufacturers add some randomization to the schedule.

While UPSes are driving the problem today, there’s a risk that the phenomenon could persist. The researchers also tested IoT devices. In devices as diverse as a smart oven and Raspberry Pi clusters, they were able to detect periodic power fluctuations. The fix is also a simple software patch, but many IoT devices go unpatched. A recent study found that less than 3% of IoT devices are running the latest firmware.

Regulators may soon have to step in to demand changes, but they can only address their corner of the grid. Given the global nature of the issue, it’ll ultimately require global coordination. The mystery may be solved, but the problem could remain for years to come.

Checking a first bag for travelers booked in economy will now be $50, up from $45, if purchased more than 24 hours before departure during a nonpeak travel period. If it's paid within 24 hours of departure, the fee will be $60. During a peak period, such as during the holidays or the summer, it will be $55 if purchased more than 24 hours before departure, up from $49, and $70 for within 24 hours of departure.

There are exceptions to the checked bag fees, such as for customers who hold a co-branded JetBlue credit card or who hold elite frequent flyer status.

"We're adjusting checked bag fees to help offset rising fuel and operating costs," JetBlue said in a statement. "This helps us keep our base fares competitive while giving customers the choice to pay for a checked bag only if they need one."

The price of jet fuel, airlines' biggest cost after labor, has roughly doubled this year since the start of the Iran war in February, costing airlines billions and driving up the price of tickets. Jet fuel was going for $4.44 a gallon on Tuesday for the U.S. Gulf of Mexico, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration.

Other airlines often follow when one carrier raises fees. Major U.S. airlines did so earlier this year after JetBlue raised its bag fees.

The changes were reported earlier by the Thrifty Traveler blog.

You open your phone to check the time or a quick message. The next thing you know, an hour has passed and you’ve scrolled through endless celebrity drama, cat videos, awful news stories, influencer rants, and whatever else the algorithm decided to throw at you.

Even though you probably don’t want to keep wasting your time and energy on this mind-numbing content, you do it again the next day.

Doomscrolling, the habit of spending excessive amounts of time consuming content on social media, has become incredibly widespread. A survey from last year found that 64% of Americans say they doomscroll.

Researchers have warned that doomscrolling can negatively affect several aspects of your well-being, including your mental health and attention span. Spending long periods scrolling can lead to brain fatigue, difficulty focusing, and disrupted sleep. And if a lot of the content you’re consuming is negative or stressful, it can leave you feeling disheartened, anxious, and emotionally drained.

It’s hard to break the cycle of doomscrolling, but there are plenty of apps that provide content that’s engaging and productive.

Of course, you could always read a book or go for a walk (we have a guide on how to stop doomscrolling), but this list is for when you have a few spare minutes and want something to do on your phone that isn’t endless scrolling.

Finch is a self-care pet app that’s designed to help you build healthy habits. You care for a cute virtual pet by completing small daily goals, such as drinking enough water, going on a walk, and practicing mindfulness. You can write about your day, complete a breathing exercise, or send good vibes to a friend by adding them to the app.

The more self-care you do, the more your pet thrives. The app offers a fun way to trade mindless scrolling for small moments of self-care.

Finch is available for free on iOS and Android.

Libby is a free reading app that connects you to your local public library’s digital collection of ebooks, audiobooks, and magazines. All you need is a library card to borrow thousands of titles, which makes it easy to turn screen time into something productive.

You can explore a new book during a commute or while waiting at a doctor’s office, turning moments when your fingers might otherwise itch to scroll into opportunities to read.

Libby is available for free on iOS and Android.

If you want to take a break from doomscrolling and try something creative, Dudel Draw might be a good app for you. Each day, the app gives you a random shape that you turn into a drawing. These daily shapes vary from basic geometrical forms to more complex and abstract designs.

You can also get your friends in on the creativity with some fun competition by comparing your different creations, which could provide a nice break from sending each other endless TikToks or Instagram Reels.

If you still want to feel connected to the world without scrolling through social media, you can check out Radio Garden. The app lets you listen to over 25,000 live radio stations from across the globe.

Once you open the app, you’ll see green dots marking cities and towns. Tap any dot to listen to radio stations broadcasting from that location. You can add favorites or search for radio stations, countries, and places.

Radio Garden is free, but it also offers an ad-free premium plan for $2.99 per month. The app is available on both iOS and Android.

Elevate is an app designed to help improve focus, memory, reading, math skills, and other cognitive abilities. It includes over 40 games to train different abilities you use in everyday life, from reading faster to comparing prices more efficiently.

You can track training streaks and compare your mind’s performance over time.

The app offers a free version with access to three games per day, or a yearly subscription of $39.99 for unlimited access. It’s available on both iOS and Android.

Vocabulary is an app that helps you learn new words every day. You can pick your difficulty level and choose categories that interest you, like emotions, the human body, business, and more. Each word comes with a definition, example sentences, and a guide on how to pronounce it.

The app also includes mini-games to help you review the words you’ve learned. You can set a goal of how many words you want to learn each week and create a regular learning routine.

Vocabulary offers a free trial, and then costs $4.99 per month or $59.99 per year. It’s available on iOS and Android.

If you’re a geography nerd looking to test your knowledge or just want to improve your geography skills, Seterra is perfect for you. The app features over 300 different games to test your map skills. You can test your knowledge of world flags; discover oceans, seas, and rivers; and explore mountain ranges and volcanoes across the globe.

Seterra lets you track your progress across categories and see leaderboards for top scorers for each game.

The app is free and available on both iOS and Android.

The app costs $5.99 per month for unlimited access and archives, but some games like Wordle, Strands, and the Mini Crossword are available to play for free.

NYT Games is available on both iOS and Android.

The app uses visually engaging mini‑games to help you build vocabulary and common phrases in more than 45 languages, with bite‑sized lessons designed to be completed in about five minutes a day.

Drops is designed for both beginners and experts who want to grow their foreign language vocabulary.

The free version of the app offers five-minute lessons per day. You can get unlimited access and premium features for $11.99 per month or $79.99 per year. The app is available on both iOS and Android.

#388. Is Jensen Huang’s job on the line? Yale’s Jeffrey Sonnenfeld estimates it’s “about a 50-50”

Buy One Founder Summit Ticket, Get a Second for Free. Register now.

Founder Summit begins Nov. 4, 9:00 a.m. ET. Get your pass savings & deals before it starts. REGISTER HERE.

Nvidia’s board announced Sunday that it’s launching an independent investigation after CEO Jensen Huang told staff he was unfaithful to his wife. But this isn’t a case of just another CEO having an extramarital affair. The romantic interest is Megaspeed executive Alice Huang, whom U.S. officials believe is a Chinese spy who was feeding information back to Beijing. In short, the stakes reach well beyond Nvidia, given how many tech and financial partners depend on its place at the center of the AI economy. “I’m responsible for an entire industry,” Huang recently told Bloomberg in a sit-down.

A spokesperson for the Chinese Embassy in Washington dismissed the allegations in late October as “groundless smears and slanders,” and no one has been charged. But U.S. intelligence officials reportedly briefed President Donald Trump about their concerns a year ago, and unnamed senior officials inside the Trump administration told the Times that they doubt Huang could have remained unaware all that time of Alice Huang’s intelligence-gathering role for China. When Trump was first briefed, the Times reported, officials didn’t believe Huang knew, and it isn’t clear whether their assessment has changed. The board, meanwhile, says it sees no sign that national security or company secrets were compromised.

The question is whether Huang can survive this. To think through what could come next, this editor spoke Sunday with famed professor Jeffrey Sonnenfeld, senior associate dean for leadership studies at the Yale School of Management and founder of the Chief Executive Leadership Institute, which has hosted Huang at its CEO summits. Sonnenfeld likes Huang, he said, and he called Huang’s career salvageable but put the odds at “about a 50-50.” He also had a lot more food for thought.

First, the damage. Until now, Huang has been “revered and trusted by everybody in technology,” Sonnenfeld noted. But he called this episode “remarkably poor judgment.” Even if Huang was merely naive, he said, “any employee in Nvidia other than him would be fired over this,” and the recklessness alone would mean “an irretrievable loss of confidence.”

Sonnenfeld suggested that Huang’s saving grace could be what social psychologists call idiosyncrasy credits, if he can prove he was being duped. The term comes from Edwin Hollander, a social psychologist who proposed in a 1958 paper that people earn latitude in a group by showing competence and following its rules. Huang has been earning latitude since he co-founded Nvidia in 1993. But all that goodwill can run out when a screw-up looks self-serving or touches the group’s core norms. Here that group includes Nvidia’s board, its roughly 42,000 employees who are bound by security rules, and, in a looser sense, the U.S. government, given it restricts its exports.

We also talked about precedents, except, as it turns out, there really are none for what’s unfolding right now. Executives who survive personal scandals sometimes pick up where they left off at another company, Sonnenfeld observed. Former HP CEO Mark Hurd is one example. Between 2005 and 2010, he drove HP to the top of the tech industry by revenue, then was pressured to resign after he filed inaccurate expense reports that hid a personal relationship with a contractor. Oracle hired him about a month later. Huang has no equivalent because Nvidia is his company

As for the bench at Nvidia, that doesn’t really exist either. While there is typically a hierarchy inside companies of Nvidia’s scale and importance, Huang has kept things as flat as possible, with about 60 direct reports by his own account. That structure makes it hard to figure out who would succeed him (though his children, both of whom work at Nvidia, are sometimes the subject of succession speculation). That won’t stop the worldwide guessing game that’s sure to follow. And boards in crisis often reach for a “messianic solution” from outside, Sonnenfeld said, floating former Google CEO Eric Schmidt as one idea.

Nvidia’s role at the center of a massive and complex circular financing web is what makes this particular predicament so tricky and, undoubtedly, terrifying for a wide number of constituents. Nvidia isn’t just the biggest provider of chips driving the AI boom but also largely the bank behind the buildout. In addition to stakes in hundreds of smaller startups, Nvidia has invested in OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, CoreWeave and Nebius. In August, it also signed preliminary agreements with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR aimed at mobilizing more than $500 billion over time, and Huang has said Nvidia could backstop up to a quarter of it. The move was widely interpreted as a way to spread risk. Still, a leadership crisis at the center is how a stumble becomes a collapse.

So what’s Nvidia’s board likely doing right now? Sonnenfeld noted that Huang’s constant, public advocacy for selling advanced chips to China had already made people anxious. Anthropic’s Dario Amodei, whose company Nvidia has backed, had a lot to say about selling chips to China at Davos back in January, likening it to “selling nuclear weapons to North Korea.”

Now the board’s independent members — meaning everyone but Huang — will have to establish what technology reached China if any, and by what routes, including by better understanding Nvidia’s relationship with Megaspeed. The Singapore cloud company, which grew out of a Chinese gaming business and became Nvidia’s largest buyer in Southeast Asia, bought roughly $2 billion of its chips, the New York Times reported in October 2025, raising broader questions about whether it was helping China circumvent U.S. export restrictions by re-exporting the chips to China or renting them to Chinese customers remotely. (An Nvidia spokesperson told The Hill after the Times report that the allegations were “thoroughly investigated and debunked last year.”)

In the meantime, Huang will keep running the company, Nvidia said Sunday. That’ll be a tall order, even for a CEO who already spends much of his time traveling the world to promote Nvidia. For starters, three House chairmen want a closed-door briefing from Huang on November 18. Nvidia is also reporting its newest earnings on November 17. Sonnenfeld expects that even if Huang keeps the title, he’ll have to hand off some of the day-to-day decision-making.

Only one thing is certain right now. Huang doesn’t own the kind of super-voting stock that would cement his power at Nvidia. Sonnenfeld often argues for dual-class structures that empower founders, and he says Huang’s success without one has been the best counterargument to that case. But if Huang had one, Sonnenfeld continued, removing him would be harder.

This episode may be remembered as a pivotal moment for how the tech industry guards its secrets. Sonnenfeld argues tech has been “way too lax” compared with defense contractors that face clearance regimes. The arrest last month of an OpenAI engineer points to the same problem. He was accused of hiding a recording device behind a TV in an executive conference room, and while OpenAI has said it has no indication that model weights, source code, or customer data were accessed, the case highlights how exposed tech companies are to people who get close.

Huang may well survive the investigation (Sonnenfeld hopes the board clears him of knowingly aiding anyone). The bigger test comes when Nvidia’s employees, customers and partners, and, indirectly, the millions of Americans with Nvidia in their 401(k) plans, have to decide whether an AI economy built largely on the judgment of Huang still makes sense.

CVS Health plans to split into two separate public companies by the first half 2028 in what would be one of the largest corporate breakups in history, the company announced Wednesday.

The plan would specifically separate CVS' roughly 9,000 retail pharmacies from its major insurance business and pharmacy benefit manager, Caremark, one of the nation's three largest PBMs.

The decision follows a "comprehensive strategic review" by the company's board of directors, according to a news release. Shares of CVS closed more than 12% lower on Wednesday.

It's a stunning reversal for the healthcare powerhouse, which has spent the last two decades working to turn itself into a one-stop health destination. The move also comes as CVS' aggressive turnaround plan under CEO David Joyner shows substantial progress, with the company pursuing $2 billion in cost cuts, store closures, leadership shake-ups and efforts to rein in Medicare Advantage costs.

But Joyner told CNBC's "Squawk on the Street" on Wednesday that the decision to split is "out of a position of strength," pointing to recovery in Aetna's Medicare business and solid performance in the retail pharmacy.

"So it's all about the strength of the business that affords us the opportunity to make what we think are the right strategic decisions for the future of this company," he later added.

CVS shares have jumped roughly 70% since Joyner took the helm in October 2024, bringing the company's market value to $117 billion.

The two new companies would both be publicly traded.

The insurance and PBM company will retain the CVS Health name and be run by Joyner, while the other company will be called CVS Pharmacy and be led by Prem Shah, who is currently group president of CVS Health. Additional leadership positions and board members will be announced at a later date, CVS said.

Existing CVS shareholders will own stock in both companies after the split. The Financial Times on Tuesday first reported the breakup plans.

The company's pharmacy business raked in $37.66 billion in 2025 sales, while its insurance unit booked $36.29 billion in revenue and the healthcare services segment generated $51.24 billion.

CVS on Wednesday also posted third-quarter earnings and revenue that topped Wall Street expectations. Total sales climbed to $108.40 billion, from $102.87 billion in the prior-year period.

Sales were boosted by year-over-year growth in the company's healthcare benefits and health services segments. Meanwhile, revenue in its pharmacy services business slid from the previous year.

On Wednesday, CVS said it will lump its insurer and PBM with its primary care business Oak Street Health, home visit provider Signify Health and the company's specialty drug business.

CVS also said the split will allow the two companies to experience a "focused strategic agenda" and greater ability to "innovate, adopt technology, increase efficiency," among other benefits.

It's not the first time CVS has weighed splitting the company. In October 2024, CVS engaged advisors in a strategic review of its business and considered breaking up its retail pharmacy and insurance units.

But Joyner told CNBC that the company was "in a turnaround" at that time without the flexibility, cash position or business performance to execute a split.

In an interview, Leerink Partners analyst Michael Cherny said the split shows "the stability of the pharmacy," which has won market share from competitors and is seeing gross margins stabilize thanks to CostVantage, a new pharmacy reimbursement model introduced by CVS last year that the company bills as more transparent.

"This business is a much different business from the one that's been considered three years ago, five years ago, 10 years ago, where there had been a litany of headwinds that the pharmacy just couldn't overcome," he told CNBC on Wednesday.

One potential concern for investors is whether the retail pharmacy will lose its competitive advantage of being directly linked with a PBM, which negotiates drug prices and manages prescription drug benefits on behalf of insurers and employers.

But Joyner told analysts Wednesday that the synergy between the two businesses "are going to be preserved in commercial agreements between the two organizations," adding that "we don't need the integration of an operating structure."

Cherny said the rationale for the split could also extend to the state legislative scrutiny over the company's level of vertical integration.

The potential breakup also comes as pharmacy benefit managers face mounting bipartisan scrutiny in Washington, D.C., and across states. Republicans in Tennessee and Arkansas have passed laws that would ban co-ownership of PBMs and pharmacies. Those have been challenged in court by CVS and other companies.

PBMs have been criticized for opaque pricing practices, their growing market power and potential conflicts of interest when they are owned by companies that also operate insurers and pharmacies.
