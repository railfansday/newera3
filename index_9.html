#456. Musk says Tesla will refund some Cybertruck deposits, defends decision to end production

Tesla CEO Elon Musk is defending the Cybertruck and the company’s decision to stop producing the polarizing steel-clad truck, claiming that the order rate far exceeds its production.

Tesla will shut down production of the Cybertruck in about eight months Musk said in an X post on Monday. He also said the company will try to make as many of the vehicles as possible before then, though Tesla will “have to return deposits for a lot of people.”

Musk argued the Cybertruck was not unprofitable, and that Tesla was ending production because the “opportunity cost of the complexity of Cybertruck is vastly outweighed by the value of incremental Cybercab production.”

In other words, Musk is arguing that Tesla can make more money by using the factory space and resources dedicated to the Cybertruck for building Cybercab robotaxis. Musk said last week that Tesla plans to use the factory space to build more two-seater Cybercabs.

The first Cybertrucks were delivered to customers in late 2023, when it seemed like it could be a breakthrough hit for the company. Reservations for the vehicle had ballooned to 1 million, according to Musk, who also predicted that the company would sell around 250,000 of the trucks annually.

But those reservations never translated into actual sales. Tesla doesn’t break out sales of the Cybertrucks, but industry experts estimate a total of about 71,000 Cybertrucks have been sold since the first deliveries began in November 2023.

Many of those sales came from inside Musk’s ecosystem of other businesses. For instance, SpaceX (now known as SpaceXSI), which he also leads and is the largest shareholder of, purchased $131 million worth of Cybertrucks from Tesla in 2025, according to the company’s S-1 registration statement ahead of its IPO. Another Musk company, xAI, also bought $430 million worth of Tesla Megapacks for its data centers, before it merged with SpaceX.

But sales of the Cybertruck, which lagged for months, could see a final bump now that it’s going out of production. Still, even a scarcity boost is unlikely to push the truck’s demand to the lofty projections from several years ago.

#453. Automattic debuts a platform to deploy vibe-coding apps amid a changing internet

WordPress has been a crucial technology and content management system for websites like TechCrunch. Now, WordPress.com owner Automattic is responding to the changing state of the web by debuting a new service called Spacefast, which will allow users to deploy vibe-coded websites and apps.

Spacefast is designed to provide infrastructure for the vibe-coded apps, including databases, authentication, file uploads, emails, and analytics, to make them functional websites, apps, or services. The product competes with the likes of Lovable and Emergent, which offer both interface and rails for vibe-coded apps, but in a more open way.

Users can connect Spacefast with other agents and chatbots like ChatGPT, Claude, Hermes, OpenClaw, Grok Bot, Muse, Pi, Cursor, OpenCode, and Amp. Alternatively, they can connect a Git repository to Spacefast to get started.

The product is headed by Batuhan İçöz, who worked on it during one of the company’s hackathon sprints earlier in the year. At that time, he worked on the company’s cross-platform messaging app Beeper and said that he often got Vercel links from his non-technical friends while exploring vibe coding.

“I thought there had to be a better way for everyone to set up and deploy their projects. Automattic already has good infrastructure, but we have been traditionally associated with just WordPress. I thought, why do I have to use Cloudflare when we have a CDN (content delivery network) just as good? So I started working on what was called Stattic and now Spacefast,” he told TechCrunch over a call.

İçöz eventually disseminated the solution to his friends and added more features to make the product more dynamic. Automattic soft-launched the product in September to get feedback before today’s launch. The company has seen varied use cases of their product, with people and organizations using it for internal mockups, slideshows, and offer-related websites. However, that’s not the only competition Spacefast has. Productivity and design software like Figma, Canva, and Notion have launched products that sit at the intersection of vibe coding, mockups, and presentations.

İçöz thinks that, compared to something like Lovable, Spacefast has the advantage of giving users a choice of their agent and using the solution their way. The counter-argument might be that a certain kind of user wouldn’t want to choose and just have everything figured out for them.

Spacefast said that it also has the advantage of being within Automattic to launch solutions like web analytics, WordPress integration, and e-commerce features (through WooCommerce) easily to compete better with the whole lot.

“For enterprises, the problem right now is IT sprawl, as their marketing teams are churning out landing pages on services like Lovable, and they want to consolidate and work with a company that understands the enterprise needs. With our existing products, we have experience serving enterprise customers,” İçöz noted.

A challenge for newly minted products in the AI world is to retain people post-launch. Users might get excited to try out a new product, and then move on to the new shiny thing within days or weeks. İçöz is cognizant of the fact and said that he expects people to churn, as they might not be interested in making apps at all.

“I think what we need to do as a product is make sure that when you build something with SpaceFast, it is so easy, and the results are so much better than without Spacefast,” İçöz emphasized.

Many websites and publishers are struggling with dwindling traffic due to AI chatbots and summaries, right now, to the point that Google itself saw its ad revenue growth drop. İçöz acknowledged that people are making websites with ChatGPT or Claude now as compared to deploying something on WordPress, and he said some sites won’t need a content management system at all as the web expands.

Spacefast comes amid turbulent months at Automattic. In an OpenAI/Sam Altman-style saga, the company’s board forced CEO Matt Mullenweg out, but he was back in control within days. The rebel board was eventually replaced, and the interim CFO left within weeks of appointment. In October, TechCrunch reported that the company had begun layoffs across several properties and more cuts were expected.

#447. Meet Drops, the new music app bringing back the fun of sharing songs with friends

There’s a new music discovery app borrowing the endless vertical-scrolling format of TikTok. But instead of watching videos, users swipe through short clips of songs, hoping to stumble upon their next favorite artist.

Launched Monday by Harman, a Samsung subsidiary, Drops aims to make discovering new music feel social again.

Available on iOS and Android in the U.S., the app lets users scroll through 10- to 30-second snippets of songs, accompanied by lyrics, album artwork, artist photos, and captions. The idea is to give listeners just enough of a track to grab their attention, whether it’s a particularly good lyric, a catchy hook, or a beat that makes them want to hear more.

The company says Drops is meant to feel like a return to how people used to discover music in the first place. Before every platform’s algorithm recommendations, people often heard about new songs through friends, music blogs, or online communities. Drops is trying to recreate that experience.

“You send a friend a song and say, ‘You have to hear this.’ Then you end up texting, ‘Skip to 1:47, that’s the best part.’ The part that hits usually isn’t the first few seconds, and there wasn’t a good way to share just that moment,” Clare Hunter, general manager of Drops, told us.

Hunter explained that the idea for Drops began taking shape after Harman acquired music management software company Roon in 2023.

“I used to spend hours making mixtapes for my friends, picking the songs, the order, and decorating the cover. Drops is the version of that I always wanted,” Hunter added.

The app is easy to navigate, with tabs including “For You,” “New,” “Showcase,” and “Following,” along with relevant and trending hashtags.

Users can like, comment, repost, share, and save tracks to collections called Droplists. They can also send songs directly to other users, follow other profiles, and exchange direct messages.

To listen to the entire track, Drops offers integrations with major streaming services, including Apple Music, Qobuz, and TIDAL. Users can jump directly into the full version of the song through their connected streaming platform.

Notably, the company says it doesn’t use generative AI for audio or visuals. Songs appear as they were originally released, using official album artwork and licensed artist photography. At a time when AI-generated music and slop are becoming harder to avoid, this will likely appeal to many users.

Another part worth noting is that Drops pays rights holders and reports plays back to its partners. Those partners include Universal Music Group, Beggars Group, Empire, Symphonic Distribution, and UnitedMasters.

Plays also count toward streams on those services, meaning Drops isn’t trying to replace existing music subscriptions.

Drops is currently invite-only, with U.S. users able to join the waitlist.

#443. Waymo robotaxis kept illegally passing school buses after software fix, feds say

Nearly a year ago, Waymo updated its self-driving software and issued a recall after numerous reports that its robotaxis were illegally passing school buses that had stopped and deployed flashing red lights. Now, federal safety regulators have opened a new investigation into why that behavior has continued.

The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) said in a November 6 filing that a majority of these reported incidents occurred after the company deployed a software update intended to fix the problem.

Reuters was the first to report the filing.

Waymo said last year that a software update was completed for all affected vehicles on November 17, 2025. However, NHTSA said the majority of the incidents occurred after the software update. NHTSA’s Office of Defects Investigation (ODI) has opened a “recall query” to “assess the adequacy of the remedy.”

The autonomous vehicle then turned left around the front of the bus before traveling down the street.

Numerous other incidents have been documented in the weeks and months since in Austin, where Waymo also operates a robotaxi service. Austin Independent School District officials reported at least five such incidents at the time, according to a letter filed with NHTSA.

Reached for comment, Waymo stressed the importance of safety to its mission. “We have made significant improvements to vehicle behavior around school buses and have received no citations since March 2026,” a spokesperson told TechCrunch via email. “The Waymo Driver has a clearly demonstrated positive impact on roadway safety. #441. Meta expands Instagram’s AI age-guessing tech globally

Meta announced on Monday that it’s expanding its AI-powered age-estimation technology on Instagram worldwide to identify more teens and place them under its Teen Account protections, as it faces numerous lawsuits accusing its platforms of harming teens.

The social media giant says it will use the AI technology to find people it suspects to be teens, even if they use an adult birthday.

Teen Accounts place users into a stricter account experience with additional safeguards, such as receiving direct messages only from people they follow or are already connected to, hiding harmful comments, and setting accounts to private by default.

Meta’s age-guessing tools use AI to analyze entire profiles for contextual clues, such as birthday celebrations or mentions of school grades. Meta also looks for these signals across different formats, such as posts, comments, bios, captions, and more. The company also looks at visual cues from photos and videos, including a person’s height or bone structure.

Meta said it’s continuing to roll out the technology on Facebook, with a similar global rollout planned for Threads later this year.

The global expansion comes as Meta reached settlements worth up to $18 billion with nearly every U.S. state and territory in August to resolve lawsuits alleging its platforms harm teens. As part of the settlements, Meta agreed to improve how it identifies teens and children under 13.

“We’ll continue to do our part to proactively ensure teens are in age-appropriate experiences on our apps,” Meta wrote in a blog post. “That’s why as part of our agreement with 52 bipartisan attorneys general across US states, territories, and the District of Columbia, we’re investing in even stronger technology to proactively catch accounts that may belong to children under 13. We’re also strengthening the technology we use to identify accounts that may belong to teens between the ages of 13 and 17 so we can ensure those accounts are placed in experiences designed for teens, even if they give us an adult birthday.”

Meta said parents in the U.S. will start seeing an in-feed video on Instagram explaining its approach to ensuring their teens are in age-appropriate protections.

#436. Medium’s new TK app brings AI assistance directly into the writing process

Online publishing platform Medium on Monday launched a new app, called TK, which aims to bring AI technology deeper into the writing process. The idea behind TK is to make it easier to turn ideas into written words by incorporating an AI assistant into the editor itself, allowing users to ask for help by typing “@TK” followed by their request or prompt.

In publishing and journalism, the term “TK” serves as a placeholder used to mark where missing information can be added later. The “TK” stands for “to come,” but uses a “K” instead of “C” as there are few English words where the “tk” letter combination exists, making it stand out more easily in written text where it could be caught by proofreaders.

In Medium’s app, TK takes on a new role as it tries to help users fill in the blanks.

The company suggests the AI assistant could help answer questions that you might normally have to switch to another tab to quickly look up, like how many days are in a particular month. The app could also pull in bits of writing you had previously saved to the app when you were still fleshing out your ideas.

In addition, Medium says TK will ship with particular skills for editorial feedback, headline feedback, and others that could match your draft to the style guide for Medium publications.

Medium’s move to embrace AI in the early stages of writing could prove controversial at a time when the publishing and literary worlds have been reckoning with the role that artificial intelligence has played in the creation of new long-form prose. Multiple authors have lost book deals over suspicions that they used AI technology to help them write, while other literary awards have come under scrutiny after winners were suspected of writing with AI assistance.

While writers have long used automated tools for proofreading and light grammatical edits, there is growing concern across the publishing industry that an AI assistant’s editorial “suggestions” can become the writing itself, instead of being reflective of the thoughts in the writer’s own mind.

But because AI models were trained on human writing, there are also questions around the reliability of AI detection tools in terms of their ability to properly determine when something was written with AI, rather than with its assistance, versus not at all.

TK leans into this debate, taking the side that these nuances will ultimately no longer matter in the end. Instead, the company celebrates what AI can bring to the world of writing, saying that “artificial intelligence makes writing more important, not less,” and proclaiming that “AI is a technology that has the power to amplify human intelligence and it uses the written word as a foundation.”

Still, Medium concedes that AI does raise the possibility of low-quality content and blurring the line between what’s human-written versus AI-written. To address these concerns, TK allows authors to publish notes that transparently explain how AI was used in their writing.

“This is a start of what we believe is required to publish in the AI era, which is to make the provenance of writing clear, transparent, and inspectable, right down to the prompting and source material,” Medium explained in a blog post about the launch.

In addition to writing assistance, TK will help users remember and organize what they had previously written and suggest writing prompts. The app can also optionally connect to your own AI agent through TK’s MCP server or Claude skill, so the app can better understand your projects, your positions, and other ideas that could be stored in your agent’s memory.

Meanwhile, the new app incorporates human feedback in a social layer, the company says, allowing writers to share their early drafts with others and get feedback.

At launch, TK is available on the web, but a desktop app is on the roadmap, Medium says. The app can import and export Markdown, but it’s not a Markdown app itself. The writing in TK is also not encrypted, as the app is designed for collaboration, but users will be able to set access control permissions on their work.

Medium, which is profitable, says TK will be a free tool for those with a Medium account.

#405. YouTube streaming data returns to Billboard charts, ending major disagreement

YouTube is officially returning to the Billboard charts, ending a disagreement over how music streams should count toward chart rankings.

Announced Monday, YouTube is set to resume sharing its streaming data with Billboard in 2027. The decision follows recent changes to Billboard’s chart formula, which now gives more weight to ad-supported streams.

The disagreement began in December 2025, when YouTube said it would stop sharing its streaming data with Billboard. The company argued that the chart formula favored paid subscriptions while not giving enough credit to fans who listen for free with ads.

At the time, Lyor Cohen, YouTube’s global head of music, criticized the system in a blog post, saying it didn’t reflect how people listen to music today.

“Billboard uses an outdated formula that weights subscription-supported streams higher than ad-supported. This doesn’t reflect how fans engage with music today and ignores the massive engagement from fans who don’t have a subscription. Streaming is the primary way people experience music, making up 84% of U.S. recorded music revenue,” Cohen wrote.

Under Billboard’s previous formula, 1,000 paid streams counted the same as 2,500 ad-supported streams. YouTube felt this gave too much importance to paid listening, especially when millions of fans discover and support artists through free streaming.

Billboard has now updated its formula, reducing the gap between the two types of streams. Under the new system, 1,000 paid streams will equal 1,500 ad-supported streams when calculating album consumption units, compared with 2,500 under the previous formula. Paid streams will still count for more, but free streams will now have a bigger impact on chart positions.

YouTube’s return could make a difference for many artists, especially independent musicians and those with large international fan bases. Music videos, live performances, lyric videos, and other content on the platform help artists reach new audiences. With YouTube’s data back in the mix, more of that activity will now count toward their Billboard performance.

For Billboard, getting YouTube’s data back also means having a more complete picture of how people listen to and discover music. With so many fans using the platform, leaving its streams out meant missing a major part of music consumption.

“We built Cape Station on our schedule. Google can develop a potential data center on their schedule, and neither party is waiting on the other,” he said.

Latimer’s statement also underlines an advantage that geothermal has over competing generating sources like large nuclear or natural gas power plants — the phased nature of its construction.

Fervo’s newer wells are deeper and hotter, each capable of generating 15 megawatts gross power, and several are linked together into what the company calls a GeoBlock. The first completed GeoBlock generated 33 megawatts, while recent ones hit 50 megawatts. They’re sized to match the demand of a typical data center hall, so hyperscalers can bring them online as they’re completed.

As a business, Fervo is still in the early stages. It ended the quarter with an operating loss of $43.7 million, though it holds cash and cash equivalents of $1.9 billion and long-term debt of $301.6 million, which should give it plenty of runway.

Much will depend on the company bringing down the cost of its power plants. While hyperscalers are hungry for power today, they may not always be as price-indiscriminate.

Yet if Fervo can deliver on its promises, it could end up winning on price. The first phase of its Cape Station project cost $7,000 per kilowatt of capacity, the company said. The cost for its Google project is $5,500 per kilowatt, a significant drop that suggests Fervo is on the right path. With subsidies, that’s about equivalent to a new natural gas power plant running on fuel that costs $6 per million BTUs. Still about twice what natural gas costs today, but trending closer to where it needs to be.

But if Fervo can hit its long-term target of $3,000 per kilowatt, it could significantly undercut natural gas power, even at today’s prices. That’s a pretty ambitious target, but the company has made significant strides in just a few years. No wonder Google is getting in early.

#402. The race is on to sue Nvidia

Nvidia’s stock slid more than 4% on Monday, the first trading day after its board said it would investigate CEO Jensen Huang over his relationship with Alice Huang, an executive U.S. intelligence believes was spying for China.

Securities lawyers were already moving. Several firms, including Schall, Brown & Schwartz, Levi & Korsinsky, Bronstein, Gewirtz & Grossman, and The Law Offices of Frank R. Cruz, announced investigations Monday into whether Nvidia made false or misleading statements or failed to disclose information that’s pertinent to investors. Robert Bartlett, a Stanford Law professor and securities law expert, expects them to have plenty of company.

“I would definitely wager that there’ll be a filing,” Bartlett said in an interview Monday morning, adding that he wouldn’t be surprised if a case had been filed by the end of day. Plaintiffs’ lawyers have good reasons to be first in line; under federal securities law, the first complaint triggers a public notice, and investors then have 60 days to ask the court to be named lead plaintiff. A firm that files early gets a head start on signing up investors with large losses, as well as on shaping the story of the case. Nvidia is headquartered in Santa Clara, and indeed, that’s where one of the first cases surfaced Monday afternoon.

Rosen Law Firm, a New York-based plaintiffs’ firm that specializes in securities class actions, filed the case Monday in federal court in the Northern District of California. (The firm was lead counsel in a $250 million settlement with Alibaba and ranks among the most active filers of such cases.) The suit was brought on behalf of Mark Kistenmacher, who’s described as an individual investor. According to the complaint, Kistenmacher made seven purchases totaling roughly $20,500 between February and November of this year. The proposed class covers anyone who bought Nvidia securities between Oct. 9, 2025, and Nov. 8, 2026, and the suit names both Nvidia and Huang.

The complaint accuses them of misleading investors about Huang’s ties to Megaspeed, the Singapore-based data center outfit where Alice Huang was considered a top executive. It points to three statements: Nvidia spokesperson John Rizzo’s claim that Huang’s interactions with Alice Huang were “not unusual or unexpected,” the company’s claim that it found “no evidence of diversion” of its chips, and Jensen Huang’s Oct. 30 comment to Bloomberg that Nvidia’s dealings with Megaspeed were “normal and not unusual.”

All three were false or misleading, the suit says, because Huang was having an affair with Alice Huang. Kistenmacher is asking for unspecified damages, interest and attorneys’ fees, and he wants a jury trial.

While much remains unknown, the legal theory here doesn’t depend on anything the board’s investigation turns up about Huang’s private life. When The New York Times first reported on Huang’s ties to Alice Huang last month, Rizzo really did tell the Times that the relationship was “not unusual or unexpected.” Rizzo separately told another outlet, The Hill, that “Megaspeed has been no different than many other regional cloud customers. Meetings with customers are part of a CEO’s job; Jensen talks with thousands of customer executives every year.”

Since then, Huang has acknowledged to the board that the relationship was intimate. Meanwhile, The Times reports that U.S. intelligence had been monitoring Alice Huang for more than a year.

From a securities perspective, Bartlett said, the company’s earlier statement was “clearly a misleading statement.” And because Huang is CEO, Bartlett said, “his knowledge gets imputed to the company.” In other words, Nvidia is treated as having known what its chief executive knew.

The Kistenmacher complaint makes the same argument.

Bartlett says the likely vehicle is a “classic fraud on the market” claim, in which investors argue they paid prices that reflected false or incomplete public information, and lost money when the truth emerged. This early lawsuit relies on that theory, and Monday’s drop of $7.97 per share, or 4.27%, gives the attorneys involved a lot to work with. Damages in such cases are tied to the stock’s decline, and Nvidia is one of the most valuable companies in the world, so you can imagine a settlement could prove exceedingly expensive.

The usual path is that the case is litigated for a long time and then settles, Bartlett notes. That means a large payment from the company and its directors-and-officers insurers, but typically no personal consequences for the CEO or the board. It is also a civil matter, so Huang isn’t facing prison over it. The irony, as Bartlett noted, is that the suit is brought in the name of Nvidia’s shareholders, and shareholders ultimately pay for the settlement, directly or through insurance premiums.

Bartlett sorted Nvidia’s wider exposure into buckets. The first is the securities fraud claim, which is the clearest immediate problem for the company and potentially for Huang. The second is governance: what the board knew and when. It’s possible directors were kept in the dark, but the board also has oversight duties, and once the Times began reporting on the Megaspeed connection, the company should have had reporting lines to bring a critical risk like this to directors. If it didn’t, or if directors didn’t act, board-level liability is possible.

A third category, one that would change everything, would arise only if the board’s investigation finds Huang knew about the intelligence connection. That would be “a whole other ball game,” as Bartlett said. (The board said in a statement to the public Sunday that it has no reason to believe national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised.)

There’s also the question of government action. The SEC can seek an officer-and-director bar for intentional securities-fraud violations, which Bartlett said is technically on the table. But he doubted the current SEC under Chairman Paul Atkins would have the appetite, given President Trump’s support for Huang. A roughly two-year clock after discovery of the facts would leave room for a future administration to act, though the window would be tight, and Bartlett posited that’s pretty unlikely when asked about it.

It’s worth underscoring that neither law firm announcements nor this newly filed complaint is a sign of a strong case. Plaintiffs’ firms are quick to publish these notices when they see an opportunity. Which firm winds up leading the case is decided later by the court, and that decision usually depends on which investor has suffered the biggest loss. Kistenmacher said in his filing that he’s willing to serve as lead plaintiff, but the court will ultimately choose among the competing investors. There’s no doubt they’ll materialize soon.

Hotel and resort giant Wyndham has acknowledged a cybersecurity incident after a good-faith security researcher discovered a bug that allowed anyone to access customers’ personal information.

Wiggins told TechCrunch that the credential exposed personal information including customer names, email addresses, and Wyndham member number. The database also stores customer login tokens (called JWTs) that keep customers logged-in to their apps without having to repeatedly enter their own passwords. The database also contained Wyndham files and documents.

According to Wiggins, the database had millions of entries, but he was unable to determine an accurate number of exposed customer records. Wyndham touts some 129 million enrolled rewards members.

The researcher said Wyndham has no way to report security flaws, and asked for TechCrunch’s help in alerting the company. Soon after TechCrunch made contact with Wyndham’s cybersecurity staff, the exposed credential was revoked, preventing any further access to the database.

“Our team has taken steps to address the issue,” a Wyndham spokesperson told TechCrunch. “Our security teams have no evidence of any unauthorized access,” the statement added, without providing any further details about the claim.

Wyndham’s spokesperson would not say, when asked, whether the company had the technical means, such as server logs, to determine if anyone had access to the customer database prior to Wiggins’ discovery.

TechCrunch also asked Wyndham whether it planned to notify customers whose information was exposed, or disclose the incident to U.S. regulators, such as filing notice with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, as public companies are required to do for material cybersecurity incidents. Wyndham would not say.

Wyndham’s spokesperson also would not say for how long the password was embedded in its customer app, or how Wyndham plans to ship a long-term fix for the app.

App and software developers generally avoid including passwords or credentials in publicly released software and apps — a process known as “hardcoding” — as anyone with a copy of the software can extract the password and use it to access its associated server or database. Given the simplicity with which credentials can be abused by anyone, many cases of hardcoded credentials are automatically scored with a maximum vulnerability severity rating.

For Wiggins, this is his second public discovery of hardcoded credentials in two weeks, after finding a near-identical vulnerability in Applebee’s app that exposed customer data. Wiggins relied on AI tools for both discoveries, which make it easier to identify simple security weaknesses, such as hardcoded credentials, by scouring thousands of lines of decompiled app code every second.

When asked about Wyndham’s response, Wiggins told TechCrunch that he was glad the company fixed the issue, but noted that without visibility into any past activity, someone with malicious intent could have leaked large amounts of customers’ personal information.

The company would not say whether it planned to set up a vulnerability disclosure program to allow outside security researchers and hackers to privately report security vulnerabilities.

Michael Francess, Wyndham’s cybersecurity director who responded to the incident, told TechCrunch that researchers can alert [email protected] with details of potential security issues. The company’s spokesperson would not say say whether it plans to change its website to allow researchers to alert the company.

This is the latest known security incident at Wyndham in recent years. In 2024, a security researcher found several Wyndham hotel check-in desks were running remote-access spyware that was leaking screenshots of guest records and booking information to the open web.

Soon after our reporting, the spyware app found on the hotel computers, called pcTattletale, was hacked and its banks of customer data stolen, forcing the company’s owner, Bryan Fleming, to shut down the company. Fleming, a former resident of Michigan, was later arrested by federal authorities and convicted of making, selling, and advertising unlawful spyware.

#301. CVS Health plans to split itself up. Here's why a breakup could make sense now

CVS Health plans to break itself up — and this time, the bet looks a lot more viable.

The healthcare conglomerate on Wednesday announced plans to split into two publicly traded companies by the first half of 2028. CVS would separate its pharmacy business – including roughly 9,000 retail locations – from its major insurance business and pharmacy benefit manager, Caremark.

The move comes more than two years after CVS considered a breakup but ultimately walked away from the idea. At the time, analysts questioned whether the retail pharmacy business could survive on its own.

“Back then, the idea of retail standing alone was unthinkable,” Brian Tanquilut, senior equity research analyst at Jefferies, told CNBC.

But the economics of the businesses have changed: CVS's retail pharmacy business has become more consistently profitable, while its insurer, Aetna, has improved its performance despite persistently high medical costs in Medicare Advantage plans. That could make it easier for the company to separate the units — and for investors to recognize the value of each on its own, rather than valuing them together as one sprawling healthcare conglomerate.

“For us, it's showing the stability of the pharmacy,” Michael Cherny, senior research analyst at Leerink Partners, said in an interview. “This business is a much different business from the one that's been considered three years ago, five years ago, 10 years ago, where there had been a litany of headwinds that the pharmacy just couldn't overcome.”

CVS CEO David Joyner told CNBC’s "Squawk on the Street" on Wednesday that back in 2024, the company was "in a turnaround" without the flexibility, cash or business performance to execute a split. Now, CVS has posted eight consecutive quarters of beating Wall Street earnings estimates and has raised its guidance several times over the last two years, thanks to improvement across its PBM, pharmacy and insurer.

Since taking the helm in 2024, Joyner has pursued an aggressive turnaround plan that involves $2 billion in cost cuts, store closures, leadership shakeups and efforts to rein in Medicare Advantage costs.

The company also now expects to generate at least $15.5 billion in operating cash flow in 2026, a roughly 70% increase from $9.1 billion in 2024, and says it has reached its leverage target ahead of schedule.

"What's changed in two years is the fact that we now can look at this business and the assets that are assembled and really determine, without any distress or without anything that we have to do, what is the right strategic decision to make," Joyner told analysts on a call Wednesday, adding that the decision comes "out of strength."

While investors have largely cheered CVS's progress in recent years, Wall Street appeared more pessimistic about Wednesday's announcement, as the company's shares dropped about 12%.

CVS's retail business has benefited from changes in the competitive landscape, including the widespread retrenchment and store closures of Walgreens, Rite Aid and other pharmacy operators over the last few years. That’s created opportunities for CVS to gain market share, some analysts told CNBC.

CVS has also introduced changes to its pharmacy reimbursement model. Its CostVantage program is designed to replace the traditional process with a more transparent, cost-based model. Some analysts say that has helped the company better align reimbursement with the cost of dispensing prescriptions and contributed to steadier profits.

Those developments represent a meaningful shift for a business that has faced years of pressure from declining prescription reimbursement, store closures and competition from online pharmacies and other retailers.

That doesn't mean the retail pharmacy has become a high-growth business. CVS's retail pharmacy segment remains a relatively low-margin operation, with adjusted operating margins of roughly 4% to 5%. But analysts say it may now be stable and profitable enough to operate independently – even if investors value it at a discount to CVS's other businesses.

In a note Wednesday, RBC Capital Markets analyst Ben Hendrix said the lower valuation investors assign to the retail pharmacy unit has weighed on CVS's overall value and obscured the earnings strength of businesses like Aetna and Caremark.

The underlying growth of the insurance and PBM businesses is also stronger than that of retail pharmacy, Tanquilut said, after accounting for changes to Caremark's rebate arrangements.

Aetna in particular has gotten a better handle on medical costs by exiting unprofitable markets, expanding value-based care models, and tightening utilization management.

The breakup also comes as Caremark faces mounting scrutiny over PBM practices, including state-level legal and regulatory challenges involving the businesses and their relationships with pharmacies.

Republicans in Tennessee and Arkansas have passed laws that would ban any one company from owning both a pharmacy and a PBM — thought the Arkansas measure has been held up in court. Cherny noted that legal hurdles involving PBMs in other states have continued to pile up.

“Through what seems to be a never-ending litany of state lawsuits, it feels like it's been under more pressure,” Cherny said. “It's unclear if it just became, for lack of a better term, too tenuous to move forward with those two entities combined as one. I’m not sure.”

Even so, Joyner told the Wall Street Journal that the company wasn’t splitting up because of regulatory issues. CVS has said the separation follows a strategic review and is intended to give each business greater focus and flexibility.

The move also marks a significant departure from the integrated healthcare strategy CVS spent years building.

CVS spent roughly $70 billion to acquire Aetna in 2018 and argued that combining health insurance, pharmacy benefit management, retail pharmacies and health providers under one roof would help coordinate care and create a competitive advantage. CVS was still promoting that model as recently as its investor day last year.

The proposed split suggests the company now sees more value in separating at least some of those businesses – though it is not abandoning vertical integration altogether.

Aetna and Caremark will remain together in the healthcare company, alongside CVS’s specialty pharmacy, its primary care clinic Oak Street Health and its home-visit provider Signify Health. The separate pharmacy company will include roughly 9,000 retail stores, mail pharmacy operations and MinuteClinic, a retail medical clinic located inside certain CVS locations.

Both sides are large enough that they can stand on their own, Cherny said. Caremark is one of the country's three largest PBMs, while CVS has one of the largest retail pharmacy footprints in the U.S.

But separating the company raises questions about how much of the commercial relationship between the businesses can survive.

The relationship has historically benefited both sides. As it stands, Caremark can use CVS's extensive retail footprint to offer members convenient access to prescriptions and steer them toward CVS locations through preferred pharmacy arrangements. Programs such as Maintenance Choice, for example, give eligible members the option to fill certain maintenance medications at CVS retail pharmacies or through mail order.

Those arrangements also helped direct prescription volume to CVS's stores, giving the retail pharmacy unit an important source of customers. Because the businesses shared a parent company, the financial benefits of that relationship ultimately went to CVS as a whole.

In a note, RBC's Hendrix also raised questions about how the split could impact Aetna's Medicare Advantage quality ratings, which determine whether health plans qualify for bonus payments from the government. Retail pharmacists have played a role in engaging Aetna members, and a separation could complicate that relationship.

But Joyner told analysts Wednesday that the synergy between the two businesses would be "preserved in commercial agreements between the two organizations," adding that "we don't need the integration of an operating structure."

Joyner said CVS will be working on those potential agreements over the next 15 to 18 months.

Preferred pharmacy networks are common across the industry, and separate ownership does not prevent Caremark from continuing to favor CVS pharmacies through negotiated arrangements, Cherny said. The difference is that under separate ownership, the profits from those arrangements would no longer accrue to a single company, he noted.

“The issue is not whether the companies can continue working together in some capacity, but whether any future contractual arrangements preserve the SAME competitive advantages and flexibility as today under common ownership,” Cherny said in an afternoon note on Wednesday.

Still, both businesses face challenges on their own.

Tanquilut cautioned that some of CVS's recent retail pharmacy gains have come as Walgreens and other competitors have closed stores, raising questions about whether the growth can be sustained. The business also remains exposed to consumer pressure and competition from online pharmacies.

Caremark, meanwhile, faces continued regulatory scrutiny and potential headwinds from changes to the federal 340B drug discount program, which requires drugmakers to offer discounted medications to eligible hospitals and other healthcare providers serving low-income and vulnerable patients.

CVS participates in the program by providing pharmacy services to those organizations, but tweaks to manufacturer data-sharing policies and potential reimbursement changes could weigh on that business in 2027, Tanquilut said.

Aetna, meanwhile, is still working through a multiyear plan to rebuild its margins, as it looks to improve the profitability of its Medicare Advantage business.

Ultimately, CVS is betting that the value of letting investors assess its businesses separately will outweigh the benefits of keeping them under one roof. The split will test whether each can thrive independently.

But instead of splashing the extra cash, or saving up for holidays or a house deposit, he's decided to squirrel more of it away into his workplace pension.

The reason? He doesn't think he'll get any kind of state pension. It's pretty stark to hear, but growing up with constant headlines about an ageing population, a proportionally smaller working-age population, and the pressure that government finances are under, Joel thinks it's his generation that will suffer.

"I don't believe that I'll be a recipient of a state pension. I know a lot of people my age don't think they're going to be... There just won't be enough money," he says.

Retirement has always felt distant when you're in your 20s - something to think about later. But what's emerging among today's underâ30s is something different: not just distance, but doubt.

The state pension age is shifting. At the start of April, the age at which you receive it began to gradually creep up, rising from 66 years to 67 years by March 2028. It's due to go up again in 20 years' time to 68, though that might happen earlier as the government has an ongoing independent review.

That's a frustration for 27-year-old retail manager Connor, who got in touch via BBC Your Voice, because he says "the goalpost keeps moving". "At the minute I'll be 68 by the time I can retire, but I do think I'll be probably closer to 75, if I'm honest."

More than 13 million people - 19% of the population - are currently of state pension age. By 2050, even with the state pension age rising to 68, that group is projected to exceed 15 million people, nearly a quarter of the population, with numbers projected to climb towards 17 million by the 2070s. In other words, there will be lots more people qualifying for the state pension, and fewer working people, as a proportion, paying taxes into the pot to cover the bill.

At the same time, almost half of workingâage adults are not paying into a private pension pot. That means many will be relying solely on the state pension for their retirement income - and with relative poverty rates among pensioners now at 14%, we can already see how difficult that can be.

Experts warn that if a whole generation stops believing the state pension will be there, it could push people towards more risky investments, prompt overly restrictive behaviour, or lead others not to save at all.

So, are we heading towards a major pension crisis for many in Gen Z? And if we are, might the Gen Z generation end up redefining what retirement looks like?

For those hitting the state pension milestone today, as long as they've made 35 years of National Insurance contributions, they're entitled to Â£241.30 a week.

That amount rises each year to help people keep pace with rising living costs. Since 2011, pension increases have been guaranteed by the triple lock - which means the rise will either match the rate of inflation, average earnings or 2.5% - whichever is higher.

But in recent weeks, several organisations have called for the rules to be rewritten.

The centre-left Resolution Foundation think tank has argued for scrapping the triple lock, saying that continuing to prioritise the incomes of pensioners over working-age adults and children would be unfair.

Meanwhile, the Tony Blair Institute (TBI), a think tank set up by the former prime minister, takes things a step further and has called for the whole state pension to be scrapped and replaced with a new "Lifespan Fund". Thomas Smith, director of economic policy at the TBI, argued: "Britain's state pension system was built for a different era. We can't keep pouring money into a system that is increasingly unaffordable."

It suggests scrapping the triple lock and allowing people to access some of their state pension early if they need it because of redundancy and frequent job changes.

That's an idea that might appeal to Connor in Chesterfield. He's facing redundancy from his job at a global cosmetics firm, and the ability to draw down a small amount - effectively a withdrawal from his future state pension - might tide him through.

"There's not that many jobs out there at the minute, unfortunately. I still live at home with my parents luckily, but I pay board to them. I still have a car payment, I still have my insurance to pay."

But former pensions minister Steve Webb argues those changes would be "a huge backward step". He says the benefit of the current system is its simplicity which shouldn't be replaced with "something fiendishly complex and highly intrusive, which would take many decades to implement in full."

The government says it has committed to the triple lock for the rest of this parliament, and that the Pensions Commission, an independent body set up to review the regime for UK private pensions, is examining "how we can ensure secure retirements for tomorrow's pensioners".

"I don't think a means-tested state pension is necessarily a bad thing. But it would be a bad thing if it only applies to people in 50 years and not now when we should be saving some of that money."

Engineer Joel is one of life's squirrels. His fear about the future of the state pension means he's doubling-down on his private pension. He contacted BBC Your Voice because he feels that consecutive governments have sheltered current pensioners, leaving the consequences for his generation to face.

"I'm going to have to increase the amount of my paycheck that goes into a private pension, which obviously isn't good with cost of living through the roof," he says.

Some experts warn that the gap between rich and poor in retirement could widen significantly for this generation.
