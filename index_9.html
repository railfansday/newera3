#486. Marc Lore's Wonder confidentially files for IPO

Food startup Wonder has confidentially filed for an initial public offering, the company said Thursday.

Created by serial entrepreneur Marc Lore in 2018, Wonder advertises itself as "the best restaurants from across the country, all in one place," available for delivery and takeout on its vertically integrated app.

Originally envisioned as a new take on food delivery through sprinter vans outfitted with mobile kitchens, Wonder has grown to roughly 170 brick-and-mortar locations throughout the U.S. Its locations serve menu items from more than two dozen "restaurants" across a variety of cuisines, some with branding from celebrity chefs.

Starting Nov. 17, the company plans to introduce more dining options through Wonder Create. Influencers, entrepreneurs and brands can pay $10 a month to start their own restaurant business based out of a Wonder location and earn a portion of the revenue from orders.

Wonder has spent at least $1 billion on acquisitions, such as delivery service Grubhub, meal kit brand Blue Apron and delivery and logistics provider Relay, along with some buzzy restaurant concepts. Wonder has been able to afford such expensive dealmaking thanks to more than $3 billion in venture funding.

Wonder's Series D fundraising round earlier this year raised its pre-money valuation to $9 billion.

With the proceeds from an IPO, Wonder will be able to make more acquisitions and work toward its goal of making food more accessible, Lore told CNBC in September.

But as a public company, Wonder will also likely face investor scrutiny. Weak consumer sentiment has weighed on restaurant stocks recently, plus market volatility has resulted in a shaky IPO market for companies across a range of industries.

Lore previously founded Diapers.com and Jet.com, which he sold to Amazon and Walmart, respectively.

#466. Cyberattack on Fireworks AI allowed hackers to access Notion customers’ chat transcripts

A cyberattack at Fireworks AI has exposed some of its customers’ data, the company has confirmed. Fireworks AI, a platform that developers use to access and use open AI models, said an attacker breached its cloud provider to gain access to a Fireworks development system, which allowed access to some of its customers’ data.

The company said in its latest security advisory that the cybercriminals took Fireworks’ customers’ billing information and some datasets. Fireworks touts tens of thousands of customers worldwide using its platform, including legal tech company Harvey and AI company Cursor.

At least one confirmed affected customer is online document editor and notetaker Notion, which said in a disclosure that the breach affected around 1% of its own customers.

Notion said that Fireworks alerted the company to a security incident and later confirmed that the hackers had stolen some Notion customer data. On November 3, Notion said it disconnected its systems from Fireworks, deleted the data stored in its systems, and stopped all connectivity between the two.

Notion confirmed this week that the hackers took chat transcripts of its Notion AI customers who used the “Insights” feature. The company said that the transcripts include “any input to or output from the model that was written to the chat,” but added that much of the data was unattributable and could not be determined from which customer it was obtained.

The breach at Fireworks AI shows how tech companies are reliant on other tech providers for their own offerings, and how cyberattacks can, as a result, spill over into other customers.

Fireworks said that it believes the hackers are financially motivated and that their goals are focused on selling tokens from breaching access to large language models, or LLMs. These schemes rely on selling to unauthorized third parties, who can burn through AI model usage without using their own resources.

This is the second breach affecting Fireworks in as many months, following a security incident in October.

#460. Jensen Huang is out at Nvidia — who is Colette Kress, his interim replacement?

#457. Whoop just launched a social network

Whoop, the maker of screen-less fitness bands, is launching a social network on Thursday that lets its members follow friends and family and share their health data.

The network, called Whoop Humans, will be rolled out in the Whoop app to members at every membership tier at no extra cost. Members can follow anyone on Whoop and choose what to share, from sleep and recovery to workouts and achievements. Followers can react and comment; members can also attach photos and commentary to their data. It’s all opt-in, CEO Will Ahmed tells us.

Whoop already has a social feature, Teams, which a little more than half its customers use, according to the company. But Teams is built around groups and leaderboards, and everyone in a group sees everyone else’s information. Humans is meant to be more intimate. Members decide who follows them and who sees what, so a trainer can track a set of clients without the clients seeing one another’s data, for example.

I found myself questioning how much a spouse wants to know about the other’s sleep score when Ahmed mentioned this particular scenario, so I asked him how else beta members used these newer social features. He said the company learned that people were more engaged when someone reported a low recovery score than a nearly perfect one, suggesting that the new feature may be particularly helpful for understanding when someone is struggling and might need attention.

Indeed, the bigger story here is where Whoop continues to be headed, which is into medical wellness. Like its most direct rival, Oura, the company is pushing faster and more aggressively into the wellness opportunity that an aging population presents.

Whoop had already added FDA-cleared ECG and atrial fibrillation detection, blood pressure insights, blood testing, and medical-record uploads. Asked if older customers are gravitating to Whoop, Ahmed said, without offering specifics, that Whoop has seen “huge growth” among people over 60 over the past 18 months. He also said his was among the first companies accepted into a Medicare program that reimburses qualifying wearables that can show they improve health outcomes, and that it now has Medicare members.

He added that he expects families to be big users of Humans. (You can imagine adult children checking in on aging parents.)

Ahmed also said Whoop doesn’t sell member data. “Our product is not you; it’s the membership,” he said, adding that the company doesn’t license data to third parties.

He was candid about dealmaking. Asked about Oura’s announcement this week that it will pay $625 million for Counsel Health, a service offering on-demand physicians, Ahmed said he expects “more consolidation” among AI-focused, clinician-focused, and wearable companies, and that Whoop is always on the prowl. Its deep relationship with members, he said, puts it “in a position to be an acquirer.”

He was more guarded about the company’s own finances, declining to share revenue or say whether Whoop is profitable. He did say that the company now has about 1,200 employees, membership on its software platform has increased more than 70% a year for three years running, and that it expects to have nearly 4 million members by year-end.

As for an IPO (the company raised a very big round earlier this year), he said only that Whoop “will certainly IPO in the future.” He also wouldn’t comment on Oura, which shelved its own IPO of up to $2.2 billion in September citing market uncertainty.

In the meantime, Whoop is widening its reach. The company announced that its devices will be sold through Ulta stores beginning next March (around 500 locations of the beauty retailer to start, and a target to retail in more than a thousand stores eventually). Indeed, Ahmed said Whoop’s female audience has more than doubled from last year.

As for competition, in addition to Google’s new Fitbit Air band, Whoop may soon have to contend with Apple, which is reportedly developing a Whoop-style band. But Ahmed mostly shrugged off those concerns, suggesting that building a product doesn’t guarantee success. Instead, he said, Whoop’s job is to stay true to the “aspiring” people it serves.

The client’s SOP and your own notes from stakeholder interviews give you plenty of information to get started. Upload your materials to Gemini and type a brief prompt:

Summarize the current steps, organizational roles, and key systems involved in my client’s workflow, as outlined in the attached SOP document. Answer as a process analyst, and provide your response in the form of a numbered list.

Gemini will take a lengthy client SOP and deconstruct it into a list of simple steps, helping you to more clearly see inefficiencies and potential sources of friction. Take a look at the attached stakeholder interviews. Examine them for frequently mentioned pain points, bottlenecks, and unnecessary manual effort. Rank the top five problems.

This step will likely uncover issues that are either hidden throughout your client’s SOP document or not mentioned by it at all. For example, stakeholders might note that disconnected systems are causing background checks to stretch on for multiple days, creating a cascade of other problems like delayed ticket generation. Provide the first draft of a current-state analysis for my client. Highlight the inefficiencies we have previously identified and quantify their business impact. Make sure the draft uses a professional, objective tone.

Gemini will translate the SOP and stakeholder input into an executive-level summary, but your own expertise is still critical. Continue the conversation to fine-tune your deliverable for tone, format, and content.

Create a list of the 10 most readily actionable recommendations for improving the process. Categorize these action items by the level of effort required to implement them, as well as by their potential impact.

#422. Small retailers brace for 'huge hit' this holiday season as shipping costs spike

When Jenni Shields began her small business eight years ago selling handmade hair accessories, she paid $2.93 to ship an order, or close to $4 in today's dollars when adjusted for inflation.

Last year, Shields said, an average shipping label for her products cost around $4.50. But when she took a typical batch of orders to her local U.S. Postal Service office last month in Kansas City, Kansas, she was shocked to see that it was an average of nearly $6 per package.

Shields is just one of the small- and medium-sized retailers who are struggling to keep up with rising shipping costs as the price of fuel continues to trend upward.

Small business owners such as Shields are facing a difficult holiday season. Customer demand is strong, but some retailers said their profits will be dented — if not eaten up entirely — by higher shipping and other costs.

Shields said she charges her customers $6 in shipping fees, which includes roughly $1.50 to cover her packaging supplies. The dramatic upswing this year means she's no longer breaking even on her orders and instead eating the added costs, she said.

"I am losing money at this point, and I've really taken a huge hit this year," said Shields, the owner of Evie Pearl Handmade, which sells bows, quilted headbands and other accessories. "I feel like it's just been this slow increase up [in shipping costs], and I feel horrible."

The surge in fuel prices since the U.S. and Israel attacked Iran in February has driven up the cost of shipping by sea, air, rail and road.

That means higher costs across the board, including for shipping giant Amazon, which reported an 11% increase in shipping expenses in the third quarter compared with last year, to individual retailers.

Globally, air cargo is going for about $3.37 per kilogram, up 43% from a similar period a year ago, according to transportation marketplace Freightos. Judah Levine, Freightos' head of research, said in addition to higher fuel, demand for fast shipping of hardware used to build data centers is helping to boost air freight rates, even weeks before peak last-minute holiday packages take to the skies.

For businesses, that runup is going to "squeeze their margins in the near-term," said Jason Seidl, managing director and transportation analyst at TD Cowen. "Larger companies tend not to be exposed to the spot market, and they'll have what we call dedicated deals in place."

But that doesn't help smaller companies, which are walking a delicate line of not wanting to alienate customers who have enjoyed free shipping for nearly a generation.

Todd Shelton, founder of the eponymous 23-year-old New Jersey clothing company that makes high-end men's jeans, T-shirts and button-up shirts locally, said he started charging customers for return shipping this week for the first time after absorbing the higher expenses this year. The shipping is deducted from customers' refunds.

Shelton said his costs have been rising for materials, too, such as his imported Japanese denim and shirt fabric from Portugal. The company is still profitable, but the higher costs have eaten into profit margins as they stay elevated, he's considering raising prices or charging for shipping to customers, he said.

Shields said she's considering raising her shipping fees for the first time in six years to keep up with the increases.

The holiday season is one of Shields' busiest. But she said she's seen a slowdown in sales because of shipping fees.

"I can't compete with Amazon on the free shipping. I can't do that. I can't afford that," she said. "I'm seeing so much of my percentage that's supposed to come back to me and my family being put towards the shipping costs."

With no sign of fuel prices declining significantly, Shields, who runs her business out of her spare bedroom at home, said she's not sure that small businesses will be able to emerge on the other side of the holidays.

Shippers including UPS, FedEx and USPS have all instituted holiday surcharges, with prices varying by package type and lasting through the end of the season. Though that's typical during the peak holiday period each year, those charges have continued to rise over the past few years.

But even as costs everywhere climb, consumers are still spending.

According to the National Retail Federation, retail sales through the end of the year are expected to grow 4.2%, translating to total spending of $1.01 trillion. That's compared with last year's holiday sales totaling $972 billion, which was 3.1% growth.

Some of that growth is from inflation, meaning consumers are simply paying more for the same goods.

"Customers all expect to spend more this holiday, but part of why they expect to spend more is they expect things to cost more, not because they'd like to spend more or they were hoping to get more quantity of stuff," said Aaron Cheris, the global retail practice head at Bain & Company. "So what I suspect happens is that non-discretionary categories do fine, because you buy the food you were going to need to buy anyway, but then the amount of cash that's left over for [discretionary] goods is less."

Still, Cheris said it's shaping up to be a record year for shipping costs, which is putting immense pressure on smaller retailers. That means many retailers may find themselves in financial distress in the first quarter of next year as they struggle to make it through the holiday season.

He added that larger retail companies will also have to reckon with rising costs, though they may not feel those effects until later quarters because they have shipping contracts that have already been locked in and have control over their own delivery fleets.

"If anything, it benefits the scale players over the little players, but it's kind of a little bit bad for everybody," Cheris said. "I think that is the challenge."

#398. Where's the growth? Legacy automakers face a tougher 2027 as costs rise, market tightens

DETROIT — There's a growing concern that the global automotive industry may be stuck in neutral, if not reverse, heading into 2027.

The auto industry — from the companies to their customers — has been surprisingly resilient this decade, but there are fears that rising costs, weaker vehicle pricing and a more challenging market could threaten profits and growth going forward.

Automakers also face pressure outside the U.S. from an onslaught of Chinese carmakers, a thinner new-product pipeline after retreating from some all-electric plans, and company- and region-specific challenges.

"With investor focus increasingly shifting to 2027, the setup appears challenging," Barclays analyst Dan Levy said in an investor note last month. "Rising inflation, continued China pressure, affordability concerns in the U.S., and a flat global production outlook suggest another year of limited earnings growth."

Several Wall Street analysts have said automakers will need continued pricing strength and additional cost savings to offset inflation, higher investment spending and a potentially weaker economy just to maintain their current levels.

"It's a little bit more optimistic in the U.S. and then very pessimistic in Europe," RBC Capital Markets analyst Tom Narayan told CNBC. "As a whole, there's some negativity on '27."

Automakers from the U.S. to Europe to Asia have been facing a growing list of headwinds.

For U.S.-based legacy automakers, Ford Motor said it expects next year's results to be in line with 2026, while General Motors grew more cautious about its ability to continue growing earnings as much as it had previously expected.

"We continue to believe that 2027 will be another strong year and better than 2026, assuming customer demand remains strong, and commodity, logistics and other recent cost pressures moderate," GM CEO Mary Barra told investors Oct. 20.

GM CFO Paul Jacobson said that while it's too early to officially forecast, the company is facing additional inflationary costs that could lead to another $2 billion headwind in 2027, like it expects this year. He specifically cited costs of commodity inflation, logistics and DRAM, or dynamic random-access memory chips as challenges.

Those chips are critical components in modern vehicles and are used for everything from temporary data storage and real-time processing from infotainment systems to safety-critical systems.

GM and others have been able to offset outside pressures through cost cuts, operational efficiencies and resilient consumer demand for higher-margin vehicles. However, there's a limit to what companies can do.

"There's only so much pricing you can push to consumers," Narayan said. "They're already at pretty high pricing."

European automakers including Volkswagen, Mercedes-Benz and BMW in recent weeks reduced their 2026 earnings forecasts in response to U.S. tariffs, the growth of Chinese automakers and rising costs.

Most recently, BMW this week said accelerated restructuring measures would reduce its automotive pretax earnings margin this year by 2.5 percentage points. The company said the restructuring should support "positive effects" into 2027 and a 3% to 5% pretax profit margin for 2028. That compares with a 5.3% automotive margin last year and 3.4% during the third quarter.

Chrysler parent Stellantis, which has large operations in Europe and the U.S., is facing its own challenges, as it's in the midst of a restructuring that aims for a low-single-digit adjusted operating margin this year. It has maintained a 2026 guidance that includes a mid-single-digit percentage increase in net revenue and a low-single-digit adjusted operating margin.

In Asia, South Korean automaker Hyundai Motor, which has experienced notable growth in recent years, also cut key metrics when it reported earnings late last month. The company lowered its 2026 wholesales target to 4 million vehicles, down from 4.16 million, and cut its operating-margin target to between 5.5% and 6.5%, down from a previous goal of 6.3% to 7.3%.

Toyota Motor is an outlier for its most recent quarter and outlook, as the world's largest automaker increased its full-year operating income forecast by 500 billion Japanese yen (about $3.2 billion) on Thursday.

Toyota's results were boosted by weakness in the Japanese yen as well as strong hybrid vehicle demand in the U.S. However, the increase in operating income was its first in six quarters.

Broadly, the changes to earnings and outlooks come as production for the global industry slows.

Consulting and insights firm GlobalData expects global light-vehicle production of about 90.96 million units in 2026, down 2.1% compared with last year. It forecast a production increase of less than 1% next year. That comes on the heels of strong annual growth earlier this decade, led by China.

Cox Automotive reported the average transaction price of a new U.S. vehicle in September was more than $50,500. That was just shy of the record of $50,614 in December 2025.

Lofty vehicle prices have led to record-high and record-long financing deals, as well as concerns that customers are getting priced out of the new-vehicle market.

The typical U.S. new-vehicle payment reached $776 a month in September, the highest since December 2023, according to Cox Automotive.

Cox Automotive chief economist Jeremy Robb, discussing the third quarter for the automotive industry, rated the overall economy, buyer ability and borrowing costs as cautionary "yellow lights."

But he did have some "green lights," such as good credit availability and employment conditions, which could give some relief for automakers and consumers, many of whom need vehicles for their everyday lives.

Robb said there's a "tug-of-war" between headwinds such as rising costs, interest rates and fuel prices and tailwinds such as growth in liquid assets and savings that has helped offset some of the additional costs.

"Consumers remain under financial pressure, but the vehicle demand continues to hold, showing more resiliency in the auto industry than we've seen in other parts of the economy," Robb said during a Sept. 24 call, noting consumer sentiment remains low.

#370. Cosm rides premium moviegoing wave with more immersive screenings and a wider library

The next big thing in premium large format theatrical viewing takes inspiration from the stars.

Cosm, an immersive entertainment technology company, got its start programming planetariums — its name a combination of "cosmos" and "colosseum." The company's massive, domed screens first offered guests a view of the universe, now they mimic courtside seats at sports venues around the world and drop film fans into some of Hollywood's most iconic sets.

At a time when the theatrical industry is seeing its biggest growth from the sale of premium large format, or PLF, tickets, Cosm seeks to offer a fresh take on an elevated viewing experience. And it's quickly expanding both its footprint and its content library.

Since 2024, Cosm has opened four theaters — near Los Angeles, Dallas, Atlanta and Detroit — with a Cleveland hub set to open in 2027. These experimental venues have garnered the attention of sports fans, screening live matchups in an arena-like environment. Showings often sell out, according to Cosm.

More recently, Cosm has partnered with Hollywood studios like Warner Bros., now a part of Skydance, to bring the likes of "The Matrix," "Willy Wonka & the Chocolate Factory," "Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone" and "It" to its locations. Cosm recently signed a deal with Universal, which will bring "Shrek" to its domes in December.

The agreements mark a lucrative new chapter for the privately held Cosm, expanding its audience reach and viewing opportunities beyond timed, live events.

As a privately held company, Cosm doesn't disclose its financials. Tickets cost anywhere from $18 for a matinee to around $70 for prime seats or in-demand showings. That doesn't include added fees for concessions or items from special, themed menus tied to screenings.

Sony invested $100 million in the company in June, taking a minority stake and placing Sony Pictures Entertainment Chairman and CEO Ravi Ahuja onto its board of directors.

Cosm also counts among its investors Cosm chairman Steve Winn's firm, Mirasol Capital; former Milwaukee Bucks co-owner Marc Lasry's Avenue Sports Fund; Rocket Mortgage co-founder Dan Gilbert's Rock; and David Blitzer, managing partner of the New Jersey Devils and Philadelphia 76ers, who invested through his firm Bolt Ventures.

"Cosm is really early in its growth trajectory at the moment, and we saw an opportunity to partner with the company at, what I would say is at, a very important stage in its development," Jay Levine, chief strategy and business operations officer at Sony, told CNBC. "They have about five locations with the goal of a pretty significant rollout. And the real reason why we did it was it gave us a lot of exposure to a growing distribution and what we deem an experiential platform that we think has long-term potential."

Sony has made several plays in the theatrical space in recent years. It bought Alamo Drafthouse in 2024 and entered a long-term lease to reopen and restore the iconic Cinerama Dome in Los Angeles in July of this year.

"Our experiences strategy is around tech, it's around fandom, and it's around new and innovative approaches to content distribution," Levine said. "For us, we're all about these differentiated experiences."

Cosm's 87-foot-diameter, concave screens drop viewers into the middle of the action, whether it's an NFL game or inside the world of "The Matrix." The company's forthcoming Cleveland location will have an even bigger dome, at 100 feet in diameter.

The experience is akin to that of the Sphere in Las Vegas, which has garnered headlines since opening in 2023 for hosting high-profile concerts and events.

"People absolutely love to live out what they love in fandom, just like wearing a sports jersey," Alexis Scalice, head of Cosm Studios, told CNBC. "And I love that I'm seeing that same behavior happen with cinema."

Stadium-style seating is nestled within each Cosm dome, with booths and tables set on each level. The dome's width is such that it fills much of the viewer's periphery, giving the sensation that they are part of the on-screen environment.

The Los Angeles and Dallas locations have capacity for around 350 seats each, while the newer domes in Atlanta and Detroit have up to 500 available seats. Cosm has not shared how many seats the Cleveland location will have, but with its larger screen it is expected to have a larger number of seats.

For live sports events, Cosm has specialty cameras mounted in arenas that broadcast back to the dome, creating the illusion of being on the field of play.

For theatrical features, the film is played in a large rectangle in the center of the dome and the background is transformed to extend the scene.

In Cosm's presentation of "Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone," for example, during the iconic scene where thousands of Hogwarts acceptance letters flood through the fireplace and into a living room, the background is swarmed with animated envelopes.

Cosm has partnered with experiential production company Little Cinema and creative studio MakeMake to create the visuals and environments for each scene of a film released in the dome. From storyboarding to post-production, this process can take up to a year as Cosm, the participating studio and the visual effects artists craft and render each moment.

"We really work hard not to take away from the story, so the film remains the hero in the narrative," Scalice said. "Working very closely with Warner Bros. and our creative partners to make sure that we're not detracting too much from the story, but we're enhancing those moments for more elevated emotional response."

With the exception of "It," which was released in 2017, Cosm's theatrical offerings thus far have been films that were released decades ago and that typically have long-established fan bases.

That helps Cosm leverage what Scalice called "that nostalgia factor" — "parents coming in that ... grew up on this, and they want to share it with their children."

"We can reinvigorate these legendary films," she added.

Cosm has dabbled across genres — horror, fantasy, science fiction — and also demographics — family-friendly and R-rated. "Shrek," set to debut Dec. 16, will be its first foray into animation.

"The thing that's so incredible about it is everybody thought initially it's just going to be working for sports, and when you look at what they've done, a lot of the titles with 'Harry Potter,' with 'Shrek,' and some of the other ones, I mean, the fill rates are actually incredible," Sony's Levine said.

Studios and theatrical exhibitors alike are looking for innovative ways to showcase Hollywood's product to moviegoers, especially as major shifts in viewing habits have disrupted the business.

In the post-pandemic era, the cinema space has been defined by fewer screens and fewer moviegoers, but those declines have been masked by higher movie ticket prices, particularly for PLF experiences.

These elevated offerings — think Imax, Dolby Cinema, ScreenX and 4DX — have bigger screens and better sound or introduce a unique experience, such as moving seats and fog machines. They're particularly popular with Gen Z and millennials, who've yet to be deterred by rising average ticket prices.

While an average standard movie ticket costs around $13, premium tickets average more than $18 each, according to data from EntTelligence.

Prices for Cosm's events fluctuate based on showtime and venue. Sony's Levine said the company's ticket prices and attendance rates are "higher than I think anybody really would have thought."

"They have no problem selling these tickets because people want to experience these things, like an immersive format," said Mike Polydoros, CEO at cinema marketing firm PaperAirplane Media. "That is something they can't get at home."

#347. Poppi founders are going after PepsiCo's Gatorade with new beverage startup Jetty

After disrupting the soda category with Poppi, founders Allison and Stephen Ellsworth are now taking aim at hydration with their new brand, Jetty.

Starting Monday, Jetty drinks and ready-to-mix electrolyte packets will be on shelves at Whole Foods grocery stores and available online at Target and Amazon.

The new, self-funded venture directly pits the Ellsworths against PepsiCo, which bought Poppi for $1.95 billion in 2025. Pepsi owns Gatorade, which dominates the U.S. sports drink category with about 60% market share, according to Numerator data.

Poppi shook up the soda category with prebiotic fiber, low calorie and sugar counts and its fun marketing aimed at Generation Z and millennials. Jetty looks to upend the hydration category through a similar strategy. To do so, it will face off not only against Gatorade but also a swarm of other upstarts looking to get in on the electrolyte-fueled trend.

For decades, Gatorade and other sports drinks have pitched their products to athletes who need electrolytes and fluids after an intense workout, although many consumers drink them every day without even sweating. Jetty is aimed at people who need a hydration boost, but not the same level of electrolytes or sugar as a traditional sports drink.

"No one has really created a product for just everyday hydration — literally everyday hydration for normal people," Allison Ellsworth, Jetty's chief brand officer, told CNBC. "Like I woke up this morning, I didn't run a marathon."

Following Poppi's sale to PepsiCo, Ellsworth stayed on as an advisor to the brand, although she said she is no longer involved in the day-to-day operations.

After 10 years building Poppi, Ellsworth said, she finally had free time. As Ellsworth tells it, she went to the doctor for the first time in a decade. She started using a GLP-1 medication and lost 60 pounds.

Ellsworth's husband, Stephen, now CEO of the new brand, noticed she wasn't hydrating, even when she came back from the gym.

GLP-1 medications such as Ozempic and Mounjaro can make people less thirsty. But because staying hydrated can also help mitigate some of the drugs' side effects, such as nausea and headaches, it can become more important.

"I physically struggle getting liquid into my body," Ellsworth said.

Plain old water is often less appealing to consumers, even dehydrated GLP-1 users, but more flavorful options with added benefits can be tempting. About 44% of GLP-1 users report consuming more functional beverages, according to EY-Parthenon's third annual survey of more than 3,600 current or former GLP-1 users.

Ellsworth sees a broader appeal for Jetty, too.

"Our core audience is going to be millennial moms and Gen Z," she said. "Gen Z just isn't relating to these old school brands, the legacy of the Gatorades and the Powerades. They're just not cool."

Consumers broadly have become more interested in staying hydrated.

Dollar sales of hydration and electrolyte products climbed 20% to $2.6 billion over the 52 weeks ended Oct. 4, based on data from SPINS, which tracks new universal product codes, or UPCs. The category also has a strong repeat buyer rate of 62%.

However, the Ellsworths aren't the only ones to notice the potential in hydration. Big food and beverage companies have been looking to hydration, too.

Kraft Heinz debuted Capri Sun juice pouches and Kool-Aid packets with electrolytes this spring. In April, Pepsi unveiled an overhaul of the Gatorade brand, focused on making it more appealing to a wider swath of consumers, with more potential occasions like a "fresh morning start" or an "afternoon walk." And Electrolit is a top performer for its distributor Keurig Dr Pepper, with 40% retail sales growth in the company's third quarter.

All of that means more competition for shelf space and consumers' "share of throat," an industry term for beverage market share.

To increase Jetty's visibility across the grocery store and to maximize convenience for shoppers, Jetty will be available in both powders and bottles.

Single-use electrolyte mixes are growing at a much faster clip than the ready-to-drink category, Ellsworth said, thanks to Unilever's Liquid I.V. and smaller upstarts like LMNT. On Amazon alone, powders are a $1 billion category, according to Ellsworth.

The ready-to-drink, or RTD, sports beverages are a much larger market, but the category's sales are shrinking as consumers reach for other hydration options instead. Unit sales of sports drinks have fallen 9.1% from 2022 to 2026, according to Numerator data. And Coca-Cola has written down the value of BodyArmor by more than $1.7 billion after buying control of the brand for $5.6 billion in late 2021.

"Me and my husband know how to do RTD," Ellsworth said.

Jetty will launch with five flavors, including watermelon, orange mango, fruit punch, black raspberry and white cherry. All of them contain 100 milligrams of sodium, 310 milligrams of potassium and 100 milligrams of magnesium in a 15.2-ounce bottle or 15-gram packet.

In addition to electrolytes, Jetty products also contain B vitamins, 3 grams of fiber and 6 grams of sugar. It doesn't use artificial sweeteners or dyes, its founders say.

For comparison, a 20-ounce bottle of traditional Gatorade Thirst Quencher has about 270 milligrams of sodium and 80 milligrams of potassium, plus 34 grams of sugar. The sports drink brand is also phasing out artificial colors and promoting lower-sugar versions.

"What's really interesting about this product and this opportunity is we now can capture all those lapsed drinkers — or that generation that didn't even ever drink it in the first place," Ellsworth said.

Poppi's new owner knows about Jetty and the market it is trying to capture, too.

"I don't think [Pepsi] gets it, but what they do get is that the movement is changing, and they think that just by taking dyes out or doing 90% less sugar, they're going to capture this audience," Ellsworth said. "But that is very big corporate thinking."

She added that she believes Pepsi has missed the mark on advertising to Gen Z consumers across its portfolio, such as in its campaigns for Sprite competitor Starry.

Perhaps as an extension of that, Poppi's performance has been underwhelming under PepsiCo's ownership.

The food and beverage giant now estimates the fair value of potential performance-based payments to Poppi's prior owners is $12 million, Pepsi said in a regulatory filing in October. At the time of the deal, Pepsi promised an additional payment of $300 million if Poppi hit certain targets by the third quarter of 2027. The Ellsworths declined to comment on the filing to CNBC.

This time around, with one successful brand under their belts, Jetty's founders say they have learned from past mistakes. Those lessons should help Jetty as it launches at a much larger scale than Poppi did.

Three different claims lawyers have signed off on Jetty's packaging, according to Ellsworth. Poppi paid $8.9 million to settle class-action litigation alleging false advertising over its "gut healthy" claims.

For now, Jetty is entirely self-funded with $2.5 million, although Ellsworth said that the brand will likely raise money in 2027. This time, though, she said investors will probably look less like traditional venture capitalists.

"I want to bring in investors that bring value," Ellsworth said. Instead of a venture capitalist who is willing to invest $15 million, Jetty would rather consider investors who can only write a check for $1 million but have access to celebrities. "So then you're getting access to talent on a cheaper level and people that are invested in the brand."

And Jetty has also learned a few lessons from Gatorade. For example, the brand is already at work on a lemon-lime version — the original flavor of Gatorade.

"We'll eventually do it," Ellsworth said. "But it was really important at the beginning just to stick to flavors that we love, for it to taste good."

#333. Restaurant chains refocus on food quality to bring back diners

Juicier chicken tenders. Revamped sauces. Richer and creamier Frosties.

Across the restaurant industry, chains are turning their focus back to their food in the hopes that better quality will win back diners.

Over the last two years, consumers have dined out less frequently and grown pickier about where they choose to eat, based on data from Black Box Intelligence. In that time, "value" has been the buzzword du jour for restaurant executives, who have explained to analysts and investors that the word encompasses more than menu pricing alone.

Now, industry CEOs are turning to their corporate chefs to try to beat rivals on quality and taste, as they look to expand their definition of value. Discounts and one-off promotions can lift traffic for a time, but the actual product still matters for restaurants' long-term growth, as well as franchisee profitability.

From Wendy's to Chipotle Mexican Grill, chief executives touted new or upcoming improvements to menu items on their quarterly earnings conference calls in recent weeks.

"We think the product's improved and even better," Chipotle CEO Scott Boatwright told analysts on the company's call in late October, referring to the burrito chain's brisket, which returned to menus for a limited time Thursday.

For some chains, better food is a key step in ambitious turnaround plans.

For example, "quality food at real value" is one pillar of Wendy's five-part turnaround strategy that the chain unveiled on Thursday.

"We're also rebuilding our food offerings and have begun testing improvements to quality and craveability of our core items, including thicker, crispier bacon, a richer and creamier Frosty, whole leaf lettuce on sandwiches and improved salads," Wendy's CEO Bob Wright told analysts on the call Thursday.

That same day, the company reported its seventh straight quarter of same-store sales declines and publicly rejected a $1.4 billion takeover bid led by Nelson Peltz's Trian Partners. Wendy's said the proposal did not take into account the potential value that its turnaround plan could unlock.

There is precedent to suggest that better-tasting food can contribute to higher sales.

In 2009, Domino's Pizza changed its recipe, swapping out its sauce, adding garlic to its crust and switching to shredded mozzarella with a hint of provolone. Along with blunt commercials that compared its previous crust to cardboard and old sauce to ketchup, the changes helped to spur U.S. same-store sales growth of 9.9% the following year.

Now, some of the same team that led the Domino's turnaround is copying the playbook at Burger King's U.S. restaurants.

Part of the burger chain's multiyear comeback plan has focused on revamping its core menu items. It started earlier this year with its flagship offering, the Whopper, which received a better bun and a new mayonnaise. And in September, it rolled out an overhaul of its nuggets, switching to 100% white meat, crispier breading and a more flavorful marinade. It also reformulated its sauces.

"We've tried to look at our biggest categories first," Josh Kobza, CEO of Burger King parent Restaurant Brands International, told CNBC. "We've also thought about the ones where the fixes are more straightforward and less operationally involved."

Following the changes, Whopper sales were up by a double-digit percentage in the third quarter, Kobza said on the company's earnings call in late October. And nugget sales climbed more than 35%. Burger King said its U.S. same-store sales jumped 8.4% in the quarter.

Like Domino's, Burger King is highlighting diners' past gripes with its Whoppers and nuggets in its ads, which star Burger King U.S. president and former Domino's executive Tom Curtis.

Better-tasting food isn't just for chains that are in trouble.

Even Chili's, which has seen its same-store sales soar 52% over the past three years, is improving more of its existing menu options. After tackling its baby back ribs, burgers and nachos, it will turn next to salads and steaks, Brinker International CEO Kevin Hochman told CNBC.

"We're going to be doing more dry aging of our beef to make it more tender, versus doing it in a plant," he said. "It's a little bit more expensive, but we think the taste is going to be a whole lot better."

Chili's rival Applebee's launched a juicier chicken tender during its latest quarter, according to John Peyton, CEO of the chain's parent company Dine Brands. In 2027, Applebee's plans to overhaul more of its protein options.

"We think proteins are, number one, our biggest opportunity," Peyton told CNBC. "Number two, they're a big focus of the way people are thinking about eating right now, leaning into proteins and a little bit less with the carbs."

Chicken is also a focus for McDonald's, which is testing hand-breaded wings, strips and a sandwich in a handful of Chicago-area restaurants. It plans to expand the trial to more locations in the U.S. and Ireland in early 2027.

Moreover, McDonald's is trying to improve its coffee, with fresher beans and upgraded equipment, executives said at the company's investor day in September. Both chicken and coffee are fast-growing categories, offering more opportunities to lift sales than McDonald's signature beef burgers.

In addition to menu tests, McDonald's is also leaning into training to improve the quality and consistency of its core menu items. Starting in October, the company launched "Make It Golden," a multiyear training program for corporate employees, restaurant workers and franchisees.

#314. Eli Lilly’s Foundayo obesity pill eases sleep apnea in late-stage trial, paving way for approval

Eli Lilly on Monday said its obesity pill Foundayo significantly reduced the severity of obstructive sleep apnea, the most common sleep-related breathing disorder, in patients with obesity, in a late-stage trial.

The drugmaker plans to file by the end of the year for U.S. approval of the daily pill for moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea, or OSA, in adults with obesity or who are overweight. The results make the pill — which rolled out in the U.S. in April — one of several GLP-1 treatments to show promise in treating the condition.

OSA refers to interrupted breathing during sleep due to narrowed or blocked airways, which can cause drops in oxygen and increases in blood pressure that are associated with serious complications, such as hypertension, heart failure and stroke. The condition affects nearly 1 billion adults globally, including 80 million people ages 20 and over in the U.S., according to the National Council on Aging. Most have never been diagnosed, Lilly said in a release.

Lilly's blockbuster weight loss injection Zepbound became the first medication to win U.S. approval for OSA in December 2024. Patients have limited treatment options outside of that drug and many use cumbersome machines that provide positive airway pressure, or PAP, to allow for normal breathing.

"Foundayo is already making a difference for people living with obesity, and with plans to submit these results to the FDA by the end of the year, we could have the opportunity to help people living with OSA," Dr. Thomas Seck, senior vice president of product development of Lilly Cardiometabolic Health, said in a release.

The trial tested Foundayo in two independent studies, the first of which included 363 patients who were unable or unwilling to use PAP therapy. The second study included 349 people who were on PAP therapy for at least three months at the time of screening and continued that treatment during the trial.

Patients in both studies who took Foundayo started at a low dose and increased it gradually at four-week intervals to different maximum dosages.

Researchers specifically examined how much the pill reduced the so-called apnea-hypopnea index, or AHI, which records the number of times per hour a person's breathing shows a restricted or completely blocked airway. The index is used to evaluate the severity of obstructive sleep apnea and the effectiveness of treatments for the condition.

People who took Foundayo and were not using PAP therapy had an average of 21.1 fewer AHI events per hour at 52 weeks, compared with an average reduction of 4.8 per hour for the placebo, according to the results.

Foundayo also met the trial's second goal in that group, leading to an overall AHI reduction of 43.8% compared with a decrease of 6.3% for the placebo. Lilly said 31.5% of the participants in the Foundayo group met the criteria for remission or improvement to mild OSA.

Meanwhile, patients who were on both Foundayo and PAP therapy had an average of 18.3 fewer AHI events per hour at 52 weeks, compared with an average reduction of 5.5 per hour for the placebo.

Foundayo led to an overall AHI reduction of around 27.5% in that group, compared with a decrease of 3% for the placebo. The company said 19.6% of the participants on Foundayo and PAP therapy also met the criteria for remission or mild OSA.

Foundayo also reduced what's known as the sleep apnea-specific hypoxic burden — a measure of how deeply and for how long oxygen levels fall during sleep. That metric fell by 55.9% in people not using PAP therapy and 45.8% in those using it.

Lilly said the pill also helped patients lose roughly 11% of their weight across groups at 52 weeks and helped to lower a marker of inflammation.

The company said the safety data and how well patients tolerated Foundayo were generally consistent with previous trials on the pill.

Around 12% of people on Foundayo who were not using PAP therapy discontinued due to side effects, compared to 3.8% with the placebo. Meanwhile, 6.8% of those on both Lilly's pill and PAP discontinued the drug due to side effects, compared with 0.6% in the placebo group.

The most common side effects were gastrointestinal, including nausea, diarrhea, constipation and vomiting, and were generally mild to moderate in severity. That's consistent with other GLP-1 medications.
