#485. Wendy's rejects $1.4 billion takeover proposal from Nelson Peltz’s Trian

Wendy's on Thursday said it is rejecting a $1.4 billion takeover offer from a group of investors led by Nelson Peltz's Trian Fund Management.

The group offered $7.35 per share in cash on Oct. 28 to take the struggling burger chain private. In addition to Trian, it included investment firms BlueFive Capital and Sixth Street Partners, plus Flynn Group, one of Wendy's largest franchisees.

In February, Wendy's board formed a committee of independent directors to assess any potential bid from Trian in response to a regulatory filing from the firm. The committee found that the proposal "substantially undervalues" Wendy's and does not reflect potential gains from the chain's ongoing turnaround strategy, the company said in a press release Thursday.

"Our confidence rests firmly in [CEO] Bob Wright's proven track record of seasoned industry leadership, including restaurant business turnarounds," Art Winkleblack, Wendy's board chair and a member of the independent committee, said in a statement.

Trian declined to comment on Wendy's decision.

To stave off a hostile takeover, Wendy's has adopted a shareholder rights plan — or poison pill — with a one-year duration, as of Wednesday, the company said. Wendy's added that the investor group led by Trian rejected an offer from Wendy's for a standstill agreement that would have paused additional share purchases, adding to Wendy's decision to use a poison pill.

The rejection is the latest in the long-running saga between Peltz and the burger chain, which dates back to an activist campaign he led more than two decades ago. A Peltz firm has even owned Wendy's before. In 2008, his company Triarc, which also owned Arby's, bought Wendy's in a $2.3 billion deal. It later sold Arby's, turning Wendy's into a stand-alone company again.

In 2024, Wendy's named Peltz as chairman emeritus after he spent 17 years on the company's board. Trian executive Peter May and Peltz's son, Bradley, still sit on Wendy's board.

Wendy's also reported its third-quarter results on Thursday. Its same-store sales declined for the seventh straight quarter. However, its quarterly earnings and revenue topped Wall Street's expectations.

Shares of Wendy's fell less than 1% in morning trading. The takeover offer had lifted the stock, which has lost about 60% of its value over the last three years.

Wendy's is not the only restaurant chain that has found itself in the crosshairs of activist investors. Applebee's owner Dine Brands recently adopted a poison pill to block a takeover bid from investment firms Arkhouse Management and Cobblers Cove Capital.

#447. Whoop just launched a social network

Whoop, the maker of screen-less fitness bands, is launching a social network on Thursday that lets its members follow friends and family and share their health data.

The network, called Whoop Humans, will be rolled out in the Whoop app to members at every membership tier at no extra cost. Members can follow anyone on Whoop and choose what to share, from sleep and recovery to workouts and achievements. Followers can react and comment; members can also attach photos and commentary to their data. It’s all opt-in, CEO Will Ahmed tells us.

Whoop already has a social feature, Teams, which a little more than half its customers use, according to the company. But Teams is built around groups and leaderboards, and everyone in a group sees everyone else’s information. Humans is meant to be more intimate. Members decide who follows them and who sees what, so a trainer can track a set of clients without the clients seeing one another’s data, for example.

I found myself questioning how much a spouse wants to know about the other’s sleep score when Ahmed mentioned this particular scenario, so I asked him how else beta members used these newer social features. He said the company learned that people were more engaged when someone reported a low recovery score than a nearly perfect one, suggesting that the new feature may be particularly helpful for understanding when someone is struggling and might need attention.

Indeed, the bigger story here is where Whoop continues to be headed, which is into medical wellness. Like its most direct rival, Oura, the company is pushing faster and more aggressively into the wellness opportunity that an aging population presents.

Whoop had already added FDA-cleared ECG and atrial fibrillation detection, blood pressure insights, blood testing, and medical-record uploads. Asked if older customers are gravitating to Whoop, Ahmed said, without offering specifics, that Whoop has seen “huge growth” among people over 60 over the past 18 months. He also said his was among the first companies accepted into a Medicare program that reimburses qualifying wearables that can show they improve health outcomes, and that it now has Medicare members.

He added that he expects families to be big users of Humans. (You can imagine adult children checking in on aging parents.)

Ahmed also said Whoop doesn’t sell member data. “Our product is not you; it’s the membership,” he said, adding that the company doesn’t license data to third parties.

He was candid about dealmaking. Asked about Oura’s announcement this week that it will pay $625 million for Counsel Health, a service offering on-demand physicians, Ahmed said he expects “more consolidation” among AI-focused, clinician-focused, and wearable companies, and that Whoop is always on the prowl. Its deep relationship with members, he said, puts it “in a position to be an acquirer.”

He was more guarded about the company’s own finances, declining to share revenue or say whether Whoop is profitable. He did say that the company now has about 1,200 employees, membership on its software platform has increased more than 70% a year for three years running, and that it expects to have nearly 4 million members by year-end.

As for an IPO (the company raised a very big round earlier this year), he said only that Whoop “will certainly IPO in the future.” He also wouldn’t comment on Oura, which shelved its own IPO of up to $2.2 billion in September citing market uncertainty.

In the meantime, Whoop is widening its reach. The company announced that its devices will be sold through Ulta stores beginning next March (around 500 locations of the beauty retailer to start, and a target to retail in more than a thousand stores eventually). Indeed, Ahmed said Whoop’s female audience has more than doubled from last year.

As for competition, in addition to Google’s new Fitbit Air band, Whoop may soon have to contend with Apple, which is reportedly developing a Whoop-style band. But Ahmed mostly shrugged off those concerns, suggesting that building a product doesn’t guarantee success. Instead, he said, Whoop’s job is to stay true to the “aspiring” people it serves.

We’ll have to wait and see how Nvidia navigates the scandal Huang heaped onto it, but now that the company is finally in its post “founder mode” era, and hunting for a new CEO, it’s doubtful it has the cache to push the industry the way Huang was.

#433. Small retailers brace for 'huge hit' this holiday season as shipping costs spike

When Jenni Shields began her small business eight years ago selling handmade hair accessories, she paid $2.93 to ship an order, or close to $4 in today's dollars when adjusted for inflation.

Last year, Shields said, an average shipping label for her products cost around $4.50. But when she took a typical batch of orders to her local U.S. Postal Service office last month in Kansas City, Kansas, she was shocked to see that it was an average of nearly $6 per package.

Shields is just one of the small- and medium-sized retailers who are struggling to keep up with rising shipping costs as the price of fuel continues to trend upward.

Small business owners such as Shields are facing a difficult holiday season. Customer demand is strong, but some retailers said their profits will be dented — if not eaten up entirely — by higher shipping and other costs.

Shields said she charges her customers $6 in shipping fees, which includes roughly $1.50 to cover her packaging supplies. The dramatic upswing this year means she's no longer breaking even on her orders and instead eating the added costs, she said.

"I am losing money at this point, and I've really taken a huge hit this year," said Shields, the owner of Evie Pearl Handmade, which sells bows, quilted headbands and other accessories. "I feel like it's just been this slow increase up [in shipping costs], and I feel horrible."

The surge in fuel prices since the U.S. and Israel attacked Iran in February has driven up the cost of shipping by sea, air, rail and road.

That means higher costs across the board, including for shipping giant Amazon, which reported an 11% increase in shipping expenses in the third quarter compared with last year, to individual retailers.

Globally, air cargo is going for about $3.37 per kilogram, up 43% from a similar period a year ago, according to transportation marketplace Freightos. Judah Levine, Freightos' head of research, said in addition to higher fuel, demand for fast shipping of hardware used to build data centers is helping to boost air freight rates, even weeks before peak last-minute holiday packages take to the skies.

For businesses, that runup is going to "squeeze their margins in the near-term," said Jason Seidl, managing director and transportation analyst at TD Cowen. "Larger companies tend not to be exposed to the spot market, and they'll have what we call dedicated deals in place."

But that doesn't help smaller companies, which are walking a delicate line of not wanting to alienate customers who have enjoyed free shipping for nearly a generation.

Todd Shelton, founder of the eponymous 23-year-old New Jersey clothing company that makes high-end men's jeans, T-shirts and button-up shirts locally, said he started charging customers for return shipping this week for the first time after absorbing the higher expenses this year. The shipping is deducted from customers' refunds.

Shelton said his costs have been rising for materials, too, such as his imported Japanese denim and shirt fabric from Portugal. The company is still profitable, but the higher costs have eaten into profit margins as they stay elevated, he's considering raising prices or charging for shipping to customers, he said.

Shields said she's considering raising her shipping fees for the first time in six years to keep up with the increases.

The holiday season is one of Shields' busiest. But she said she's seen a slowdown in sales because of shipping fees.

"I can't compete with Amazon on the free shipping. I can't do that. I can't afford that," she said. "I'm seeing so much of my percentage that's supposed to come back to me and my family being put towards the shipping costs."

With no sign of fuel prices declining significantly, Shields, who runs her business out of her spare bedroom at home, said she's not sure that small businesses will be able to emerge on the other side of the holidays.

Shippers including UPS, FedEx and USPS have all instituted holiday surcharges, with prices varying by package type and lasting through the end of the season. Though that's typical during the peak holiday period each year, those charges have continued to rise over the past few years.

But even as costs everywhere climb, consumers are still spending.

According to the National Retail Federation, retail sales through the end of the year are expected to grow 4.2%, translating to total spending of $1.01 trillion. That's compared with last year's holiday sales totaling $972 billion, which was 3.1% growth.

Some of that growth is from inflation, meaning consumers are simply paying more for the same goods.

"Customers all expect to spend more this holiday, but part of why they expect to spend more is they expect things to cost more, not because they'd like to spend more or they were hoping to get more quantity of stuff," said Aaron Cheris, the global retail practice head at Bain & Company. "So what I suspect happens is that non-discretionary categories do fine, because you buy the food you were going to need to buy anyway, but then the amount of cash that's left over for [discretionary] goods is less."

Still, Cheris said it's shaping up to be a record year for shipping costs, which is putting immense pressure on smaller retailers. That means many retailers may find themselves in financial distress in the first quarter of next year as they struggle to make it through the holiday season.

He added that larger retail companies will also have to reckon with rising costs, though they may not feel those effects until later quarters because they have shipping contracts that have already been locked in and have control over their own delivery fleets.

"If anything, it benefits the scale players over the little players, but it's kind of a little bit bad for everybody," Cheris said. "I think that is the challenge."

“Phota is your personal photo assistant that can make great photos for any moment you want to keep before you even ask, which means that it’s a very proactive experience,” Zhang told TechCrunch in an interview. “Think of it almost like a photographer or virtual photographer who looks over your camera roll and tries to understand what’s happening and how they could have taken that photo for you on the spot for that scene.”

The idea is to use AI to transform photos, whether that’s turning a wide-angle group photo into a tighter portrait or transforming a selfie into a regular shot.

While some people turn to ChatGPT and other generative AI tools to enhance or edit images, these tools can often alter a person’s appearance, especially when working with blurry photos because they don’t know what that person actually looks like.

“We’re turning something that’s unusable into something that people want to print,” Zhang said. “People who just love photos but never really got into editing can now experience professional quality that could never have been possible. In terms of funding, the company raised a $5.6 million seed round led by a16z last year.

#428. SoftBank’s billion-dollar loss may have put a cap on AI’s natural gas ambitions

SoftBank might not be the first to invest in a hot trend, but when it does, it goes big.

Witness its $30 billion bet on OpenAI in early 2025 or its announcement in February that its SB Energy subsidiary would spend $33 billion on a 9.2-gigawatt natural gas power plant. If you can’t be first, be biggest, I guess.

That philosophy apparently spilled over into procurement, where orders were also supersized. Starting about two years ago, SoftBank reportedly put down deposits on more than 13 gigawatts of turbines, almost 50% more than it needed.

The decision to over-order undoubtedly helped secure turbines and speed up SoftBank’s projects.

“SB Energy did not have a choice except to overbook turbines in the future if they wanted some turbines right away,” Peter Gardett, CEO of Noreva, told TechCrunch via email.

The energy world was caught off guard by the swiftness and magnitude of AI’s rising power demands. Queues for turbine orders now extend into the early 2030s. As natural gas was shaping up to be a go-to power source for the AI boom, CEO Masayoshi Son bet that by deploying his investment firm’s significant war chest, he could corner a portion of the market. If the AI world wanted power for its data centers, it would have to come to him.

Now that bet appears to have fizzled. SoftBank announced a $7.1 billion quarterly loss on Tuesday, with over half of that stemming from losses related to power-generating equipment. The investor had paid turbine manufacturers nonrefundable deposits to secure orders, and now it considered much of those worthless.

The move was also a costly bit of price discovery, Gardett said. Rather than overpay and potentially still be short of turbines, SoftBank overpaid and over-ordered.

“This charge is just that price discovery finally happening,” Gardett said.

The magnitude of that misplaced bet — over $3 billion — reflects some of the electricity mania that has overtaken the AI world. Faced with years-long waits to connect to the grid, many hyperscalers and developers have turned to natural gas as a sort of energy security blanket, something that is proven and familiar.

As AI data centers spend more time in operation, their operators are getting a better feel for what’s required to keep them operating. Natural gas might seem like a safe bet, but there are still risks, including winter curtailments when supplies dip and demand spikes, Brent Nelson, senior managing director of markets and strategy at Ascend Analytics, told the Equity podcast Wednesday.

In light of that, Nelson said the grid might still be their best bet. Most data centers would be best served by a grid connection, albeit a smaller one than they might normally request. To make up for the shortfall, large batteries are a cost-effective choice, Nelson said. They’d help shave peak loads or ride out requests from the utility to curtail electricity use.

Data center operators might be coming to that same conclusion, leading the natural gas market to develop more slowly than SoftBank expected.

SoftBank’s natural gas-related losses are unlikely to have a domino effect, leading to mass cancellations of gas power plants. AI’s power demand appears to be too large for that. For now, natural gas is riding high, though SoftBank may have found its ceiling.

The next big thing in premium large format theatrical viewing takes inspiration from the stars.

At a time when the theatrical industry is seeing its biggest growth from the sale of premium large format, or PLF, tickets, Cosm seeks to offer a fresh take on an elevated viewing experience. And it's quickly expanding both its footprint and its content library.

Since 2024, Cosm has opened four theaters — near Los Angeles, Dallas, Atlanta and Detroit — with a Cleveland hub set to open in 2027. These experimental venues have garnered the attention of sports fans, screening live matchups in an arena-like environment. Showings often sell out, according to Cosm.

More recently, Cosm has partnered with Hollywood studios like Warner Bros., now a part of Skydance, to bring the likes of "The Matrix," "Willy Wonka & the Chocolate Factory," "Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone" and "It" to its locations. Cosm recently signed a deal with Universal, which will bring "Shrek" to its domes in December.

The agreements mark a lucrative new chapter for the privately held Cosm, expanding its audience reach and viewing opportunities beyond timed, live events.

As a privately held company, Cosm doesn't disclose its financials. Tickets cost anywhere from $18 for a matinee to around $70 for prime seats or in-demand showings. That doesn't include added fees for concessions or items from special, themed menus tied to screenings.

Sony invested $100 million in the company in June, taking a minority stake and placing Sony Pictures Entertainment Chairman and CEO Ravi Ahuja onto its board of directors.

Cosm also counts among its investors Cosm chairman Steve Winn's firm, Mirasol Capital; former Milwaukee Bucks co-owner Marc Lasry's Avenue Sports Fund; Rocket Mortgage co-founder Dan Gilbert's Rock; and David Blitzer, managing partner of the New Jersey Devils and Philadelphia 76ers, who invested through his firm Bolt Ventures.

"Cosm is really early in its growth trajectory at the moment, and we saw an opportunity to partner with the company at, what I would say is at, a very important stage in its development," Jay Levine, chief strategy and business operations officer at Sony, told CNBC. "Our experiences strategy is around tech, it's around fandom, and it's around new and innovative approaches to content distribution," Levine said. "For us, we're all about these differentiated experiences."

Cosm's 87-foot-diameter, concave screens drop viewers into the middle of the action, whether it's an NFL game or inside the world of "The Matrix." The company's forthcoming Cleveland location will have an even bigger dome, at 100 feet in diameter.

The experience is akin to that of the Sphere in Las Vegas, which has garnered headlines since opening in 2023 for hosting high-profile concerts and events.

"People absolutely love to live out what they love in fandom, just like wearing a sports jersey," Alexis Scalice, head of Cosm Studios, told CNBC. "And I love that I'm seeing that same behavior happen with cinema."

Stadium-style seating is nestled within each Cosm dome, with booths and tables set on each level. The dome's width is such that it fills much of the viewer's periphery, giving the sensation that they are part of the on-screen environment.

The Los Angeles and Dallas locations have capacity for around 350 seats each, while the newer domes in Atlanta and Detroit have up to 500 available seats. Cosm has not shared how many seats the Cleveland location will have, but with its larger screen it is expected to have a larger number of seats.

For live sports events, Cosm has specialty cameras mounted in arenas that broadcast back to the dome, creating the illusion of being on the field of play.

For theatrical features, the film is played in a large rectangle in the center of the dome and the background is transformed to extend the scene.

Cosm has partnered with experiential production company Little Cinema and creative studio MakeMake to create the visuals and environments for each scene of a film released in the dome. From storyboarding to post-production, this process can take up to a year as Cosm, the participating studio and the visual effects artists craft and render each moment.

"We really work hard not to take away from the story, so the film remains the hero in the narrative," Scalice said. "Working very closely with Warner Bros. and our creative partners to make sure that we're not detracting too much from the story, but we're enhancing those moments for more elevated emotional response."

With the exception of "It," which was released in 2017, Cosm's theatrical offerings thus far have been films that were released decades ago and that typically have long-established fan bases.

That helps Cosm leverage what Scalice called "that nostalgia factor" — "parents coming in that ... grew up on this, and they want to share it with their children."

"We can reinvigorate these legendary films," she added.

Cosm has dabbled across genres — horror, fantasy, science fiction — and also demographics — family-friendly and R-rated. "Shrek," set to debut Dec. 16, will be its first foray into animation.

"The thing that's so incredible about it is everybody thought initially it's just going to be working for sports, and when you look at what they've done, a lot of the titles with 'Harry Potter,' with 'Shrek,' and some of the other ones, I mean, the fill rates are actually incredible," Sony's Levine said.

Studios and theatrical exhibitors alike are looking for innovative ways to showcase Hollywood's product to moviegoers, especially as major shifts in viewing habits have disrupted the business.

In the post-pandemic era, the cinema space has been defined by fewer screens and fewer moviegoers, but those declines have been masked by higher movie ticket prices, particularly for PLF experiences.

These elevated offerings — think Imax, Dolby Cinema, ScreenX and 4DX — have bigger screens and better sound or introduce a unique experience, such as moving seats and fog machines. They're particularly popular with Gen Z and millennials, who've yet to be deterred by rising average ticket prices.

While an average standard movie ticket costs around $13, premium tickets average more than $18 each, according to data from EntTelligence.

Prices for Cosm's events fluctuate based on showtime and venue. Sony's Levine said the company's ticket prices and attendance rates are "higher than I think anybody really would have thought."

Unlike Cosm's sports offerings, which can also be viewed from outside the dome from its hall and deck viewing areas at a lower price, theatrical presentations are only available in the dome.

"They have no problem selling these tickets because people want to experience these things, like an immersive format," said Mike Polydoros, CEO at cinema marketing firm PaperAirplane Media. "That is something they can't get at home."

“These challengers now have a window to prove a different approach delivers better economics; a leadership change alone won’t win them the business,” Levanon said.

#342. Poppi founders are going after PepsiCo's Gatorade with new beverage startup Jetty

After disrupting the soda category with Poppi, founders Allison and Stephen Ellsworth are now taking aim at hydration with their new brand, Jetty.

Starting Monday, Jetty drinks and ready-to-mix electrolyte packets will be on shelves at Whole Foods grocery stores and available online at Target and Amazon.

The new, self-funded venture directly pits the Ellsworths against PepsiCo, which bought Poppi for $1.95 billion in 2025. Pepsi owns Gatorade, which dominates the U.S. sports drink category with about 60% market share, according to Numerator data.

Poppi shook up the soda category with prebiotic fiber, low calorie and sugar counts and its fun marketing aimed at Generation Z and millennials. Jetty looks to upend the hydration category through a similar strategy. To do so, it will face off not only against Gatorade but also a swarm of other upstarts looking to get in on the electrolyte-fueled trend.

For decades, Gatorade and other sports drinks have pitched their products to athletes who need electrolytes and fluids after an intense workout, although many consumers drink them every day without even sweating. Jetty is aimed at people who need a hydration boost, but not the same level of electrolytes or sugar as a traditional sports drink.

"No one has really created a product for just everyday hydration — literally everyday hydration for normal people," Allison Ellsworth, Jetty's chief brand officer, told CNBC. "Like I woke up this morning, I didn't run a marathon."

Following Poppi's sale to PepsiCo, Ellsworth stayed on as an advisor to the brand, although she said she is no longer involved in the day-to-day operations.

After 10 years building Poppi, Ellsworth said, she finally had free time. As Ellsworth tells it, she went to the doctor for the first time in a decade. She started using a GLP-1 medication and lost 60 pounds.

Ellsworth's husband, Stephen, now CEO of the new brand, noticed she wasn't hydrating, even when she came back from the gym.

GLP-1 medications such as Ozempic and Mounjaro can make people less thirsty. But because staying hydrated can also help mitigate some of the drugs' side effects, such as nausea and headaches, it can become more important.

"I physically struggle getting liquid into my body," Ellsworth said.

Plain old water is often less appealing to consumers, even dehydrated GLP-1 users, but more flavorful options with added benefits can be tempting. About 44% of GLP-1 users report consuming more functional beverages, according to EY-Parthenon's third annual survey of more than 3,600 current or former GLP-1 users.

Ellsworth sees a broader appeal for Jetty, too.

"Our core audience is going to be millennial moms and Gen Z," she said. "Gen Z just isn't relating to these old school brands, the legacy of the Gatorades and the Powerades. They're just not cool."

Consumers broadly have become more interested in staying hydrated.

Dollar sales of hydration and electrolyte products climbed 20% to $2.6 billion over the 52 weeks ended Oct. 4, based on data from SPINS, which tracks new universal product codes, or UPCs. The category also has a strong repeat buyer rate of 62%.

However, the Ellsworths aren't the only ones to notice the potential in hydration. Big food and beverage companies have been looking to hydration, too.

Kraft Heinz debuted Capri Sun juice pouches and Kool-Aid packets with electrolytes this spring. In April, Pepsi unveiled an overhaul of the Gatorade brand, focused on making it more appealing to a wider swath of consumers, with more potential occasions like a "fresh morning start" or an "afternoon walk." And Electrolit is a top performer for its distributor Keurig Dr Pepper, with 40% retail sales growth in the company's third quarter.

All of that means more competition for shelf space and consumers' "share of throat," an industry term for beverage market share.

To increase Jetty's visibility across the grocery store and to maximize convenience for shoppers, Jetty will be available in both powders and bottles.

Single-use electrolyte mixes are growing at a much faster clip than the ready-to-drink category, Ellsworth said, thanks to Unilever's Liquid I.V. and smaller upstarts like LMNT. On Amazon alone, powders are a $1 billion category, according to Ellsworth.

The ready-to-drink, or RTD, sports beverages are a much larger market, but the category's sales are shrinking as consumers reach for other hydration options instead. Unit sales of sports drinks have fallen 9.1% from 2022 to 2026, according to Numerator data. And Coca-Cola has written down the value of BodyArmor by more than $1.7 billion after buying control of the brand for $5.6 billion in late 2021.

"Me and my husband know how to do RTD," Ellsworth said.

Jetty will launch with five flavors, including watermelon, orange mango, fruit punch, black raspberry and white cherry. All of them contain 100 milligrams of sodium, 310 milligrams of potassium and 100 milligrams of magnesium in a 15.2-ounce bottle or 15-gram packet.

In addition to electrolytes, Jetty products also contain B vitamins, 3 grams of fiber and 6 grams of sugar. It doesn't use artificial sweeteners or dyes, its founders say.

For comparison, a 20-ounce bottle of traditional Gatorade Thirst Quencher has about 270 milligrams of sodium and 80 milligrams of potassium, plus 34 grams of sugar. The sports drink brand is also phasing out artificial colors and promoting lower-sugar versions.

"What's really interesting about this product and this opportunity is we now can capture all those lapsed drinkers — or that generation that didn't even ever drink it in the first place," Ellsworth said.

Poppi's new owner knows about Jetty and the market it is trying to capture, too.

"I don't think [Pepsi] gets it, but what they do get is that the movement is changing, and they think that just by taking dyes out or doing 90% less sugar, they're going to capture this audience," Ellsworth said. "But that is very big corporate thinking."

She added that she believes Pepsi has missed the mark on advertising to Gen Z consumers across its portfolio, such as in its campaigns for Sprite competitor Starry.

Perhaps as an extension of that, Poppi's performance has been underwhelming under PepsiCo's ownership.

The food and beverage giant now estimates the fair value of potential performance-based payments to Poppi's prior owners is $12 million, Pepsi said in a regulatory filing in October. At the time of the deal, Pepsi promised an additional payment of $300 million if Poppi hit certain targets by the third quarter of 2027. The Ellsworths declined to comment on the filing to CNBC.

This time around, with one successful brand under their belts, Jetty's founders say they have learned from past mistakes. Those lessons should help Jetty as it launches at a much larger scale than Poppi did.

Three different claims lawyers have signed off on Jetty's packaging, according to Ellsworth. Poppi paid $8.9 million to settle class-action litigation alleging false advertising over its "gut healthy" claims.

For now, Jetty is entirely self-funded with $2.5 million, although Ellsworth said that the brand will likely raise money in 2027. This time, though, she said investors will probably look less like traditional venture capitalists.

"I want to bring in investors that bring value," Ellsworth said. Instead of a venture capitalist who is willing to invest $15 million, Jetty would rather consider investors who can only write a check for $1 million but have access to celebrities. "So then you're getting access to talent on a cheaper level and people that are invested in the brand."

And Jetty has also learned a few lessons from Gatorade. For example, the brand is already at work on a lemon-lime version — the original flavor of Gatorade.

"We'll eventually do it," Ellsworth said. "But it was really important at the beginning just to stick to flavors that we love, for it to taste good."

#333. Restaurant chains refocus on food quality to bring back diners

Juicier chicken tenders. Revamped sauces. Richer and creamier Frosties.

Across the restaurant industry, chains are turning their focus back to their food in the hopes that better quality will win back diners.

Over the last two years, consumers have dined out less frequently and grown pickier about where they choose to eat, based on data from Black Box Intelligence. In that time, "value" has been the buzzword du jour for restaurant executives, who have explained to analysts and investors that the word encompasses more than menu pricing alone.

Now, industry CEOs are turning to their corporate chefs to try to beat rivals on quality and taste, as they look to expand their definition of value. Discounts and one-off promotions can lift traffic for a time, but the actual product still matters for restaurants' long-term growth, as well as franchisee profitability.

From Wendy's to Chipotle Mexican Grill, chief executives touted new or upcoming improvements to menu items on their quarterly earnings conference calls in recent weeks.

"We think the product's improved and even better," Chipotle CEO Scott Boatwright told analysts on the company's call in late October, referring to the burrito chain's brisket, which returned to menus for a limited time Thursday.

For some chains, better food is a key step in ambitious turnaround plans.

For example, "quality food at real value" is one pillar of Wendy's five-part turnaround strategy that the chain unveiled on Thursday.

"We're also rebuilding our food offerings and have begun testing improvements to quality and craveability of our core items, including thicker, crispier bacon, a richer and creamier Frosty, whole leaf lettuce on sandwiches and improved salads," Wendy's CEO Bob Wright told analysts on the call Thursday.

That same day, the company reported its seventh straight quarter of same-store sales declines and publicly rejected a $1.4 billion takeover bid led by Nelson Peltz's Trian Partners. Wendy's said the proposal did not take into account the potential value that its turnaround plan could unlock.

There is precedent to suggest that better-tasting food can contribute to higher sales.

In 2009, Domino's Pizza changed its recipe, swapping out its sauce, adding garlic to its crust and switching to shredded mozzarella with a hint of provolone. Along with blunt commercials that compared its previous crust to cardboard and old sauce to ketchup, the changes helped to spur U.S. same-store sales growth of 9.9% the following year.

Now, some of the same team that led the Domino's turnaround is copying the playbook at Burger King's U.S. restaurants.

Part of the burger chain's multiyear comeback plan has focused on revamping its core menu items. It started earlier this year with its flagship offering, the Whopper, which received a better bun and a new mayonnaise. And in September, it rolled out an overhaul of its nuggets, switching to 100% white meat, crispier breading and a more flavorful marinade. It also reformulated its sauces.

"We've tried to look at our biggest categories first," Josh Kobza, CEO of Burger King parent Restaurant Brands International, told CNBC. "We've also thought about the ones where the fixes are more straightforward and less operationally involved."

Following the changes, Whopper sales were up by a double-digit percentage in the third quarter, Kobza said on the company's earnings call in late October. And nugget sales climbed more than 35%. Burger King said its U.S. same-store sales jumped 8.4% in the quarter.

Like Domino's, Burger King is highlighting diners' past gripes with its Whoppers and nuggets in its ads, which star Burger King U.S. president and former Domino's executive Tom Curtis.

Better-tasting food isn't just for chains that are in trouble.

Even Chili's, which has seen its same-store sales soar 52% over the past three years, is improving more of its existing menu options. After tackling its baby back ribs, burgers and nachos, it will turn next to salads and steaks, Brinker International CEO Kevin Hochman told CNBC.

"We're going to be doing more dry aging of our beef to make it more tender, versus doing it in a plant," he said. "It's a little bit more expensive, but we think the taste is going to be a whole lot better."

Chili's rival Applebee's launched a juicier chicken tender during its latest quarter, according to John Peyton, CEO of the chain's parent company Dine Brands. In 2027, Applebee's plans to overhaul more of its protein options.

"We think proteins are, number one, our biggest opportunity," Peyton told CNBC. "Number two, they're a big focus of the way people are thinking about eating right now, leaning into proteins and a little bit less with the carbs."

Chicken is also a focus for McDonald's, which is testing hand-breaded wings, strips and a sandwich in a handful of Chicago-area restaurants. It plans to expand the trial to more locations in the U.S. and Ireland in early 2027.

Moreover, McDonald's is trying to improve its coffee, with fresher beans and upgraded equipment, executives said at the company's investor day in September. Both chicken and coffee are fast-growing categories, offering more opportunities to lift sales than McDonald's signature beef burgers.

While the burger giant isn't in full turnaround mode like rivals Wendy's and Burger King, its U.S. business has struggled in recent quarters after its value strategy didn't gain the traction the company had hoped. In the third quarter, its U.S. same-store sales fell 0.2%.

In addition to menu tests, McDonald's is also leaning into training to improve the quality and consistency of its core menu items. Starting in October, the company launched "Make It Golden," a multiyear training program for corporate employees, restaurant workers and franchisees.

Ultimately, improved menu items mean little if the recipe changes aren't well executed.

"Foundayo is already making a difference for people living with obesity, and with plans to submit these results to the FDA by the end of the year, we could have the opportunity to help people living with OSA," Dr. Thomas Seck, senior vice president of product development of Lilly Cardiometabolic Health, said in a release.

The trial tested Foundayo in two independent studies, the first of which included 363 patients who were unable or unwilling to use PAP therapy. The second study included 349 people who were on PAP therapy for at least three months at the time of screening and continued that treatment during the trial.

Patients in both studies who took Foundayo started at a low dose and increased it gradually at four-week intervals to different maximum dosages.

Researchers specifically examined how much the pill reduced the so-called apnea-hypopnea index, or AHI, which records the number of times per hour a person's breathing shows a restricted or completely blocked airway. The index is used to evaluate the severity of obstructive sleep apnea and the effectiveness of treatments for the condition.

When I step out of Southampton Central railway station, it takes me just seconds to spot an e-scooter. In this city, these devices have become a normal part of the transport ecosystem, along with e-bikes.

"It's just the most convenient way to commute from home to the station and back," says Keith Reed, who I speak to as he arrives at the station to catch a train to London. Keith says uses rental e-scooters twice a week. His alternative option would be his bike, but he'd then have to worry about where to leave it.

Around the city, there are parking areas for bright orange rental devices everywhere - but they're not necessarily a permanent feature. Southampton hosts one of the numerous government-backed trials around England. This one has been going on since 2021.

A study suggests around half of users of e-scooter do so in conjunction with another mode of transport - like Keith, who rides to the railway station

It also doesn't take long to find e-scooters which aren't part of the trial, but instead are owned by individuals. I see a man riding one along the pavement outside a parade of shops. He doesn't want to stop and talk.

These privately owned e-scooters are illegal to ride on public roads across the UK, but they've proliferated regardless. One expert told me the situation was a "Wild West".

Among people I spoke to in Southampton, the same concerns came up time and time again: "They're a blinking nuisance," says elderly pedestrian Fred. Another shopper, Martin, says he's "very concerned" whenever he sees them racing through precincts: "An accident's almost inevitable."

The endgame of the trials is to reach a decision on whether to make permanent hired e-scooter schemes such as the one Keith uses - and whether to bring in legislation that would legalise and regulate privately owned e-scooters too.

But six years since the trials started around England, there's still no conclusion - and as the UK government has responsibility for road traffic legislation across Great Britain, officials in Scotland and Wales say they are waiting on Westminster to act.

So why is it taking so long for the government to decide what to do, and what are the consequences of delay? What does the evidence tell us about how safe these things are?

And why are people using them in the first place - are they here to stay as a useful part of the transport offer, or will they end up being viewed as a pest on our streets?

You'd recognise an e-scooter if you saw one. The Department for Transport's (DfT's) definition of an e-scooter includes no pedals, two wheels and a maximum continuous power rating of 500W.

And so far, the trials around England appear to have proved popular. According to DfT research, fom January 2022 to May 2024, 36.9 million trips were made across 30 trial areas outside of London. On average, nearly 19,500 e-scooters were available to hire every day.

Dr Yasir Ali, a reader in Transport Modelling at Loughborough University, says e-scooters are "complementary" to other modes of transport, rather than replacing them. Due to factors such as range and being exposed to the elements, they are less suitable for longer trips, rural areas with less infrastructure or in bad weather. They work best as "a first- and last-mile solution" in urban areas, to reduce reliance on cars for short trips.

Shared mobility charity CoMoUK's 2025 report suggests that for more than half of the people using rental e-scooters, the most common use was travelling to work or school. Its analysis of DfT data suggests the majority of people using hired e-scooters are male and under 35.

"Sometimes it's a bit difficult to take the bus from dot to dot", says Yan Zhang, who works at Southampton university and doesn't drive, "so for shorter distances, I'll take the scooter."

Like Yan Zhang, e-scooter users are less likely to have a car, CoMo's report says - this is partly explained by official trials' concentration in urban areas

Around half of respondents to CoMoUK's online survey used it in conjunction with another mode of transport - such as riding to a railway station, like Keith.

Earlier evidence from a national evaluation of the trials published in 2022 found that although some users would have driven instead had they not had access to an e-scooter, the biggest "modal shift" - meaning switch in ways of getting around - to e-scooters was from walking. Given walking is an active mode of travel, this has raised questions over how significant the public health impact of e-scooter use really is.

And the usage of privately owned e-scooters isn't reflected in this research. The DfT has said between 760,000 and 1.2 million people in England aged 16 and over own one - despite the fact that it remains illegal to ride them in public.

In the eyes of the law, e-scooters are classed as motor vehicles. Outside of the trials, they can't be insured, and thus can only legally be ridden on private land, with the owner's permission.

Parking bays for e-scooters and e-bikes are available across Southampton

The trials themselves are bound by strict rules. The devices' top speed is limited. Businesses who operate the pilots must insure them. Users must have a full or provisional driving licence, and they must not ride on the pavement. Helmets are recommended but not mandatory.

Richard Dilks, CEO of CoMoUK says "mass-scale use of something that is inherently low-carbon, low-impact, low-cost" is a "good thing" but "we also don't have rules of engagement about how they should be ridden".

And as a result of the delays in introducing regulation, Loughborogh University's Yasir Ali says "we are increasingly in a situation where the law and reality have diverged", with "the disadvantages of e-scooter use without securing the full benefits of regulation".

Back in Southampton, I meet a lady pushing a black e-scooter, who doesn't want to give her name. She says nobody told her when she bought it that it was illegal to ride on the streets.

The Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency has a unit that carries out surveillance of retailers to check they're complying with the law. But earlier this month, an investigation by the Press Association news agency discovered some retailers were advertising them for commuting purposes.

Police tell me they regularly see children illegally riding private e-scooters. Margaret Winchcomb, deputy executive director at the Parliamentary Advisory Council for Transport Safety (PACTS), says some are being specifically marketed at kids.

Police say they will seize privately owned e-scooters that are ridden illegally

So what can police do? Forces regularly launch crackdowns where they seize illegally used devices, and often crush them. Each year, awareness campaigns run urging parents not to buy their kids an e-scooter for Christmas. But enforcement is a challenge.

Insp Matt Boiles from Avon and Somerset's road policing team says problems arise even with the legal trial devices - for example, people riding them after drinking alcohol, which counts as drink-driving. Riding with another person on the back is another common issue.

But officers see private e-scooters being illegally ridden in public places "all the time, every single day" and they can be really hard to catch - it isn't always safe for police in a car to pursue someone on an e-scooter, he says.

Insp Boiles despairs over parents buying them for children: "We do have parents attend a police station quite a lot, getting very, very upset that their child's Â£500 e-scooter has been seized." But "the legislation is really, really clear that you can't have it back".

There's a potential risk of fire if unregulated devices with lithium batteries are left to charge unattended. Most train operators have banned e-scooters on board for this reason.

But there's also the fact that riders are unprotected in the case of falling off or being hit by motorised vehicles. And - along with e-bikes - they can end up involved in collisions where pedestrians are hurt. Many trial e-scooters in the UK are restricted to 12.5mph, but privately owned scooters' speeds aren't.

Some coroners have even raised concerns. In 2022, a coroner in London called for action after the death of 14-year-old Fatima Abukah on a privately owned scooter.

According to the DfT, in 2024 - the most recent year for which figures are available - 1,390 casualties were recorded in collisions that involved e-scooter in England, Scotland and Wales, three more than in the previous 12 months. The vast majority, 1,096, were e-scooter users themselves.

Young boys and men appear to be over-represented in the stats. Of the casualties, 302 - the most of any age and sex category - were males aged 10-19.

Six deaths were recorded in 2024, unchanged from the previous year. Five of these were riders and one a pedestrian.

Although the numbers aren't conclusive, it is thought more accidents are happening on privately owned e-scooters. Winchcomb says police statistics "aren't reflective of the number of injuries".

Nonetheless, campaigners believe there is enough evidence to show that regulation is urgently needed.

Carly Calland's 14-year-old son Jacob died in March 2025 of a catastrophic head injury. He was a passenger on an e-scooter that was involved with a collision with a car.

If privately owned e-scooters are legalised for public use, Carly believes, there should be mandatory helmets, a ban on carrying passengers and penalties for parents that allow children to ride illegally.

"If Jacob was wearing a helmet that day, he would still be here," Carly, from Wythenshawe in Greater Manchester, says.

Carly is not against e-scooters. They "are really good for people to get to work, and they are eco-friendly", she tells me, but "they just need to be used in the correct way."

What happens if the current situation goes on? Her answer is emphatic: "More deaths."

Meanwhile a number of major European cities have ended their shared e-scooter schemes. Paris abolished them after a referendum in 2023. Brussels is the most recent, announcing theirs would end in 2027, following a rise in accidents.

The last evaluation of the trials in England was in 2022. New findings are expected by early next year.

In recent years, successive British transport secretaries have supported the idea of a new law to legalise and regulate the e-scooters available for sale. E-scooters were mentioned in the 2022 Queen's speech, but nothing materialised.

Winchcomb says some of the "dithering" may be because of the potential knock-on effect of any legislation for users of mobility scooters.

There is some frustration from devolved administrations around the UK about the delays in Whitehall. The Scottish Government "will continue to urge the UK government, which holds powers on registration and licensing over off-road vehicles, to take action in this area", a spokesperson says.

The Welsh Government says it will "monitor developments on legalisation plans and consider the implications for Wales".

Northern Ireland's Department for Infrastructure says it is also monitoring the trials in England, as well as the impact of recent law changes in the Republic of Ireland.

The DfT says it says the trials are "building the real-world evidence needed to get regulation right" and it will consult on longer-term rules, including registration and licensing requirements, within the next year.

"E-scooters have real potential to improve local connectivity and cut emissions, and we want to see that potential realised safely and responsibly across the UK," a spokesperson says.

But Ali says: "The current halfway house becomes harder to justify as usage grows."

Even if legislation and associated technical standards were introduced, Richard Dilks acknowledges enforcing them would remain difficult.

But, he adds: "That problem only gets bigger if you leave it."

MARYSVILLE, Ohio — Honda Motor plans to invest $3 billion to build a new vehicle assembly plant in Ohio, the automaker announced Monday.

Honda said the new plant, which will be near its other facilities in Marysville, Ohio, is expected to begin production in 2029, have a capacity for 240,000 vehicles annually and create more than 2,000 jobs.

The facility is expected to be capable of producing traditional vehicles with internal combustion engines, all-electric vehicles and hybrid models, which have been growing in popularity in the U.S.

Honda declined to disclose what vehicles the company plans to produce at the new facility.

The plant is expected to increase Honda's light-duty vehicle capacity to 1.3 million units annually in the U.S. It currently has 12 manufacturing plants in United States, eight of which produce engines or light-duty vehicles for the auto industry.

"This is not simply an investment in another plant. It is an investment in the future of Honda manufacturing in America," Bob Nelson, executive vice president of American Honda Motor, said during the announcement at the company's U.S. heritage center in Ohio.

The auto industry has been trying to localize more production amid President Donald Trump's tariffs and inflated logistics costs that have cut into profits.

Nelson told CNBC after the event that tariffs are affecting the company's business but the new plant is more about Honda's ambitions to grow in the U.S.

"Tariffs are an impact to us, even with the high localization rate that we have," he said. "But the real impact is that we have customers out there wanting Honda product and we can't give it to them. That's the real area."

Nelson said Honda did not work with the Trump administration ahead of Monday's announcement.

Honda's plans for a new plant come roughly two weeks after Volkswagen-owned luxury brand Audi announced it would invest more than $4 billion in a new plant at a rally with President Donald Trump, which Audi CEO Gernot Dollner faced criticism for attending.

Honda's new plant is the company's first new mass production U.S. vehicle assembly plant since it opened a facility in Indiana in 2008. It also opened a $70 million performance vehicle facility in 2016 and a multibillion-dollar joint venture battery plant earlier this year, both in Ohio.

The announcement comes after the automaker in May indefinitely suspended an $11 billion investment plan to produce all-electric vehicles and batteries in Canada. It also canceled three planned EVs for the U.S. market.

American Honda Motor Co. executive Jennifer Thomas recently emphasized the Japanese automaker's commitment to the U.S. and need for additional capacity.

"In order to be competitive in this market, you're going to need to build products here," Thomas, senior vice president of corporate affairs, said Oct. 27 in Detroit. "We are focused on trying to maximize the existing capacity that we have, but we are at capacity, and so we're exploring all options."

Thomas also warned the automotive industry faces a "catastrophic" scenario if the U.S. and Canada cannot reach a new trade deal by the end of this year, when Trump has threatened to double tariffs on Canadian vehicles and parts from 25% to 50% on Jan. 1 if the two countries don't reach a deal.

Honda said it has built more than 33 million vehicles and more than 39 million engines in the U.S. since it started auto production in the U.S. in 1982.

The Japanese automaker currently has more than 30,000 employees in the U.S.

#294. Aviation faces hotter, stormier skies – and passengers might have to accept more disruption

Aviation faces hotter, stormier skies â and passengers might have to accept more disruption

As the country sweltered through one of the many heatwaves of a baking summer in late June, towering thunderclouds were gathering over the south of England and across much of northern Europe. It was a Saturday, and with the holiday season getting under way, thousands of passengers were heading to London's Heathrow and Gatwick airports to catch flights abroad. It was always going to be a very busy day.

What followed, however, was chaos for travellers. As air traffic controllers struggled to route aircraft around the storms, airspace became heavily congested and many planes due to fly were simply forced to wait on the ground. In the end, some 900 flights to and from both airports were delayed, some by up to 11 hours. Dozens more were cancelled. At airports across Europe, passengers expecting to fly into London also faced long waits and uncertainty.

The weather in the UK may now have cooled, and passengers may soon forget their experiences. But some within the industry believe what happened in June should be seen as a warning.

The timing in this case was clearly unlucky. But there are concerns that such incidents are only going to become more common as the effects of climate change mount up around the world and weather systems are likely to become increasingly unstable, making disruption more likely and adding to costs for airlines, airports and travellers.

Tim Atkinson, an airline risk consultant and former pilot, says the very crowded skies over the UK and northern Europe mean the region is particularly exposed.

He thinks there is a real risk, for example, that all of London's airports could be affected by storms at the same time, potentially with major consequences in the air and on the ground.

"What we're seeing now is very rapid climate change," he says.

"We're going from the kind of weather activity which takes out an airport, and which the system kind of copes withâ¦ to a situation where, with very little predictability, it would be possible to lose all of the London airports from real-time operation quite swiftly and with little notice".

Such a scenario, he thinks, would make the disruption for passengers caused by the incident in June seem relatively minor â and could even put lives at risk. But how likely is it?

For controllers at National Air Traffic Services's (NATS) main control centre in Swanwick, Hampshire, thunderstorms are a major preoccupation. When I visit, it is a hot and humid day, there are ominous dark clouds in the sky, and heavy, thundery downpours are hitting the surrounding region.

In Terminal Control, the part of the building responsible for flights heading to or from airports in London and southern England, those same storm cells are being monitored continuously. Their progress is tracked on large radar screens, while at the same time weather forecasts are carefully scrutinised.

Air traffic tends to come in waves during the day. Right now, at lunchtime, it is relatively quiet and the storms have yet to affect major routes. But with activity expected to intensify in the afternoon, staff are already preparing for what could be a tricky few hours.

Stormy weather can shrink the available airspace, meaning traffic has to be restricted and rerouted. If planes are unable to land at one airport, for example due to high winds, they may need diverting to another. But that can create its own problems; if too many unscheduled aircraft land at an already busy airport, it might not have sufficient infrastructure to accommodate them all.

Heavy rain and storms can make flying harder

Planes need fuel and somewhere to park. Passengers need food and facilities. Ultimately, if there isn't space, it can simply refuse to accept all but emergency traffic.

This is where the scenario set out by Atkinson comes in. He warns of a situation where traffic is diverted from Heathrow, Gatwick, Luton and Stansted at the same time, leaving aircraft coming in across the Atlantic and a large number of European flights all looking for somewhere to land at the same time, and some with dwindling fuel reserves.

This would at the very least create serious disruption for passengers, who might end up hundreds of miles from their destinations. But it could also, he thinks, create "a very serious risk event, such as an aircraft running out of fuel in the sky".

Aircraft are legally required to carry enough fuel to take them to an alternative airport if necessary, and to allow them to fly for an extra 30 minutes on top of that.

Not everyone shares Atkinson's concern. Former airline executive Andrew Charlton, who manages the Swiss-based consultancy Aviation Advocacy, thinks the risk is low.

"You've always got to imagine the worst-case scenario", he says. "But I also think we need to keep that worst case in context.

"Yes, it's possible to imagine it. But I'm also fairly comfortable with the ability of this industry to react well and make good decisionsâ¦ of all the things that wake me up at 3amâ¦this is not the top of that list."

Thunderstorms are caused by what is known as convective activity, where moist air close to the ground warms rapidly and rises quickly. As it rises, it expands and cools, allowing the moisture it carries to condense, forming large clouds. More heat means more convective energy, potentially creating instability. In addition, for every degree the Earth warms, the atmosphere can hold about 7% more moisture, increasing the probability of heavy rainfall.

It is not clear whether this means we will actually get more thunderstorms in the UK and across Europe, as so many variables are involved beyond the simple matter of energy and moisture. Some parts of the world may even get fewer. However, studies have shown that higher latitudes, particularly in the northern hemisphere, will see the biggest increases in favourable conditions for storms to form, and those that do may be more violent. According to the UK's Met Office, "while we may not see more thunderstorms overall, the ones we do experience could be more impactful".

Where possible, pilots will avoid flying through convective weather. Even though aircraft are designed to cope with severe conditions, storms can produce intense turbulence, capable of damaging the structural frame of the plane, as well as throwing people around the cabin if they are not strapped in. Hail can also be a big problem, potentially cracking windscreens or causing engine problems. So, however much inconvenience it may cause passengers, crews will invariably put safety first.

New technology helps pilots navigate tricky flying conditions

Onboard weather radars can help aircrew to see up to 320 nautical miles ahead. They also have access to live weather forecasting resources online. But thunderstorms can move quickly and, according to Prof Guy Gratton, an expert in aeronautics at Cranfield University, these systems are essentially "tactical rather than strategic". In other words, although they enable pilots to see storm cells on their screens from a relatively short distance away, and request a diversion, they do not allow for detailed long-distance planning.

The result is that unplanned diversions often happen late in flight, which is likely to mean burning more fuel. If storm delays do become more common, says Andrew Charlton, then aircraft will have to load more fuel as a matter of course, in case there is trouble brewing ahead. "Aircraft are going to have to fly further, and they're going to have to factor in the assumption they're going to fly further," he says. For an airline, fuel is one of its major costs â and if the fuel bill goes up, he says, it is inevitable passengers will pay more.

More intense storms would be likely to make air traffic management in busy regions harder and potentially lead to more delays and cancellations. However, it is possible some of this could be mitigated using new technologies.

Both Heathrow and Gatwick, for example, have moved away from mandating minimum distances between incoming aircraft in favour of a system which continually calculates spacing times based on wind speeds and aircraft types. In practice, this is meant to allow controllers to reduce the distance between landing aircraft, particularly in high winds, and get them on the ground more quickly. According to NATS, it has successfully improved landing rates and reduced delays by a significant margin.

However, there could be other effects from more severe storms as well. Heavier rainfall, for instance, increases the likelihood of flooding. In a worst case, this can paralyse airport operations, as happened in April 2024 when torrential downpours hit Dubai International Airport. It briefly had to close its runways, while disruption lasted for two days. More than 1,200 flights were cancelled, affecting tens of thousands of passengers.

Passengers were delayed during the floods in Dubai

But even if runways and taxiways themselves are clear, and aircraft are able to fly without any difficulty, passengers may struggle to catch their flights if roads, railways or car parks are flooded.

Protecting against such risks can be expensive. Gatwick Airport, which experienced severe flooding over the Christmas period in 2013, has spent tens of millions of pounds on mitigation strategies in recent years â although those figures are dwarfed by the more than Â£2bn it is expecting to invest in its current expansion plans.

The problem of clear-air turbulence is also a real one. It cannot be seen, and is becoming increasingly common as the climate warms.

This is when air moves violently at high altitudes with no clouds or other visual cues to help a pilot avoid it. For passengers, it usually only means a little bit of bumpiness, but occasionally it can be a lot more serious. On 1 March 2023, for example, a Lufthansa flight from Austin, Texas to Frankfurt encountered what the airline described as "brief but severe" turbulence.

Those aboard described how passengers and flight crew were thrown around the cabin as the plane suddenly lost altitude. After it made an emergency landing in Washington DC, seven people were taken to hospital.

While still rare, this kind of turbulence is already much more prevalent than it used to be and is likely to become even more so.

"You know the projections are pretty dire: doubling, trebling or worse in the amount of severe turbulence on many busy flight routes around the world," says Paul Williams, a professor of atmospheric science at the University of Reading. He says incidents over the North Atlantic have increased by 55% since 1979.

Nevertheless, he remains confident solutions can be found, including improvements to forecasting and making use of laser-based technology.

There is another climate-related threat to aviation, which may prove challenging to overcome: rising sea levels. When Hurricane Sandy arrived off the US East Coast in October 2012, it provoked a storm surge that flooded the city. The two runways at New York's LaGuardia airport were left under water for several days, preventing it from reopening even after the winds had subsided.

Significant flooding that would have affected the New York City area once in every 500 years in pre-industrial times is now experienced every 25 years or so â and could occur every five years by the middle of the century, according to one major study. That makes LaGuardia, built on reclaimed land next to Flushing Bay, appear highly vulnerable.

LaGuardia could be at greater risk of flooding and future closures

It is not alone. With some 40% of the world's population living within 100km of coastlines, many airports have been built on low-lying areas near the sea. According to research by Newcastle University, 269 airports around the world are already at risk of flooding due to rising sea levels, and that total is expected to rise. Among those potentially most exposed are the likes of Amsterdam Schiphol, London City, Bahrain International and Newark Liberty International.

Many airports do already have some degree of flood protection, especially larger ones. But stopping the flood risks from rising over the next century means expensive investment in flood defences, raising runways, or even moving airports. It could cost up to $57bn (Â£42bn) according to the Newcastle report. Such costs are ultimately likely to be met by taxpayers or by travellers.

The aviation industry is often criticised for its own contribution to climate change emissions. It faces a forbidding challenge in attempting to meet a target of being carbon neutral by 2050, while also accommodating growth that is expected to see the global aircraft fleet double in size over the next two decades.

"I think the best we can do is mitigate", says Andrew Charlton.

"We need to make sure we have enough space at air terminals for people whose flights are being disrupted and delayed, that we have enough hotel rooms and parking space and so on."

But ultimately, he says, "disagreeable moments are going to happen more".

Passengers, he thinks, will have to accept it as a fact of flying life.

#291. Europe's car makers are in crisis. Will the threat of war rescue them?

Europe's car makers are in crisis. Will the threat of war rescue them?

It's green, bulging and intimidating. Ford's latest vehicle looks like a pick-up truck that has been through an Incredible Hulk transformation. Based on its popular Ranger series, this beefy number parked outside the front door of Ford's Dagenham plant can carry a load of two tonnes, tow up to four tonnes and is also shouldering the hopes of the 2,000-strong workforce that still makes engines here.

Inside the factory, the three-litre diesel engines that will power this camo-painted beast drift down a production line that has seen output cut from 90,000 engines a year to about half that over the last decade.

Ford is hoping that a pivot towards military vehicles can help fill the void created by what the company's UK boss calls the most challenging environment since the invention of the motor car. Once mighty, the European car industry is starting to look puny and is hoping surging defence budgets as Europe re-arms can help it flex its industrial muscles once more.

Ford is part of a bid for the British Army's Light Mobility Vehicle (LMV) programme

Ford is part of a joint venture with defence specialists General Dynamics and Ricardo bidding for a Ministry of Defence (MoD) contract to supply 9,000 vehicles over the next five to seven years to replace the Army's ageing Land Rover-based fleet.

Lisa Brankin, chair of Ford UK, says it is a chance to showcase their abilities to respond quickly to defence needs: "As a manufacturer you look at every opportunity that comes at you and this is a great opportunity that we would love to take advantage of."

This is not the first time Ford has busied itself with defence work.

Ahead of World War Two, Ford's factory in Dagenham, east London, was the largest car plant in Europe.

When war broke out, civilian car assembly stopped completely, and the factory was converted entirely to military production. Between 1939 and 1945, the Dagenham plant built 360,000 vehicles for the Allied war effort.

Ford workers in Manchester manufactured 34,000 Rolls-Royce-designed Merlin engines, which powered Spitfires and Hurricane fighter planes.

Eighty years later, the UK and European car industries are hoping that the engines of war can help defend against what one supplier told the BBC was a "terminal decline".

As Europe feels compelled to commit hundreds of billions to ramp up defence spending in the face of the menace from Russia and US reluctance to be Europe's protector, car makers that are under commercial attack from Chinese rivals have taken notice.

So can the rearmament of Europe save an auto industry and a supply chain facing crisis?

Ford is far from the only car company that sees defence as a growth industry - and one that can utilise the growing overcapacity at car plants across Europe.

French car maker Renault has signed a strategic agreement with defence giant Thales to produce military drones - targeting an output of up to 1,000 units per month. The French military and Directorate General for Armament want to tap into Renault's mass-production capabilities to bypass traditional, slower defence supply chains.

Meanwhile, Volkswagen has agreed to sell an under-used factory in Osnabruck, western Germany, which will become a military manufacturing hub in a joint venture with an Israeli-based defence investor.

Jaguar Land Rover (JLR), which makes the Land Rover, is also bidding for the same contract as Ford - as the Army retires its existing Land Rover-based fleet by 2030. JLR has also established a new dedicated business unit to support its global military ambitions.

It makes sense for under-utilised auto manufacturing capacity to switch to defence, says Mike Hawes of the Society for Motor Manufacturers and Traders (SMMT) trade association.

The UK car industry and its suppliers are heavily reliant on a few big manufacturers â Nissan in Sunderland, Toyota in Derbyshire, BMW in Oxfordshire and the biggest of them all by value of output, JLR at multiple sites in the Midlands and Merseyside.

And Hawes says the supply chain is very vulnerable.

"UK automotive output has been in decline over the last eight or nine years," he says. "We're probably half of what we were 10 years ago. Now, that's obviously going to hit the supply chain because they're not making the same number of parts. So they've got capacity. They may be quite dependent on one particular manufacturer as well."

So they will welcome "the opportunity to broaden their customer base and potentially move into defence", he says.

Just two weeks ago, JLR announced it was cutting 4,000 jobs from its 30,000-strong UK workforce in order to reduce costs to stay competitive with international â particularly Chinese â rivals. Dave Roberts of the firm Evtec, which supplies cooling systems components to JLR, is worried about the implications of this.

"JLR is the critical mass in the UK automotive manufacturing space," he says. "It is the glue that holds the whole of the sector together. Because remember, when you're making volumes for JLR, they're significantly higher than any other car maker in the UK.

"If they suffer, the ripples run deeper through the supply chain."

Earlier this week, major JLR suppliers urged the government to help automotive manufacturers move into aerospace and defence, warning that large-scale car production in the UK faces long-term decline.

Executives have warned of a "visible crack" in the UK automotive supply chain

In an open letter to the prime minister, chancellor and West Midlands Mayor Richard Parker, industry leaders argued Britain's automotive supply chain was "not in decline" but "in the wrong market".

The letter was signed by executives representing businesses with more than 8,600 direct employees, alongside the Confederation of British Metalforming, which represents about 75,000 workers.

Signatories to the letter said the redundancies were "the first visible crack" in a UK automotive supply chain supporting about 183,000 manufacturing jobs.

Across Europe, the industry is facing what Sigrid de Vries, director general of the ACEA auto industry manufacturers' association called "a perfect storm". Car makers are spending billions to shift to electric vehicles and insist that government sales targets for EVs are outpacing consumer demand.

And the EVs people are buying are increasingly from the biggest new threat to the industry â China.

In the early 2000s, Western firms saw China as a land of opportunity. The country's rapidly growing middle class had plenty of money and a seemingly insatiable appetite for cars, including the most profitable upmarket models. It was a lucrative business â the Chinese market once accounted for half of Volkswagen's profits.

However, it didn't last. China wanted a home-grown car industry and lavish state funding was ploughed into making the country a leader in high-tech businesses, including electric cars.

Today, the Chinese market is overloaded with brands, both foreign and domestic, and is characterised by cut-throat competition. Chinese car makers have focused their attention on foreign markets, and have used the transition to electric vehicles as a pathway to gaining market share. The likes of BYD, Chery and Geely are moving aggressively into Europe.

For European brands, all of this could not have happened at a worse time. The loss of the steady stream of profits from China, and the emergence of Chinese rivals on their home turf, has come after they invested heavily in producing electric vehicles (EVs). But EV sales have not increased as quickly as expected. Executives admit they have struggled to match the low production costs and development speed of the Chinese insurgents.

The result is that European manufacturers are now rushing to cut costs, while wondering what to do with expensive factories capable of producing millions more cars than they are able to sell.

Volkswagen has already announced plans to cut 100,000 jobs over the next few years. Whereas once closing plants in Germany would have been unthinkable, the company has now shuttered one in Dresden and may shut down four more. That includes a site in Zwickau, where VW spent over â¬1bn (Â£857m) converting production lines to build electric vehicles - a process that was completed just four years ago.

Industry estimates indicate that western European car plants have roughly 2.5 million vehicles worth of annual spare capacity.

Little wonder that car makers are jealously eyeing soaring defence budgets across Europe.

Sigrid de Vries says car makers are well placed to help Europe rearm.

"Many of the capabilities that defence needs are needed for and also delivered by the automotive sector," she says. "So automotive manufacturers and suppliers possess industrial assets, they possess manufacturing expertise, logistics capabilities, also advanced technologies. They have vast and also very integrated supply chains that may be relevant for Europe's broader defence preparedness objective."

But it is not that simple â security protocols, political and economic rivalries within Europe plus the fact that unless you're actually at war, the volumes will not replace the mass consumer market, all of which present challenges, she says.

"These are two very different worlds," she says. Governments want to invest in their defence capabilities and that is why it's now, more than ever, interesting for manufacturers and suppliers to see what's possible, but it will not be enough to address the underutilisation of manufacturing capacity we currently see."

If defence can't plug the yawning gaps in UK and European car manufacturing production â is it time for the henhouse to rent a room to the fox?

In other words, let Chinese companies move into European production facilities?

To offset the immense fixed costs of running idle assembly lines, legacy European and UK automakers are opening their doors to Chinese rivals.

Stellantis â owners of the Vauxhall, Fiat, Peugeot and Citroen marques â has taken a 20% stake in Chinese EV maker Leapmotor and production of the Chinese brand started in Poland two years ago. Nissan and Chery International UK have signed a non-binding Memorandum of Understanding for the study of contract manufacturing for Nissan to manufacture Chery vehicles at its Sunderland plant.

VW CEO Oliver Blume said in April of this year that Volkswagen was considering sharing spare European factory capacity with Chinese joint-venture partners.

In one sense it could be a win-win. European factories would have extra work while Chinese manufacturers could avoid high tariffs when selling into the US and the EU by making and exporting the cars in and from those markets.

But building cars in Europe does not automatically mean creating or saving European supply chains.

Car making is still seen as central to countries' sovereign manufacturing capability â in the old days the mantra was: "What's good for General Motors is good for America." You could say the same for Volkswagen, Mercedes and BMW for Germany and JLR for the UK.

Car plants are often the major employer in their area and support local supply chains, so job losses can hit particularly hard. That's why countries go to great lengths to protect and preserve them.

Australia is a case in point. When its final locally made car left the production line in 2017, it didn't just lose a car industry, it lost an engineering mindset and key skills, says Dave Roberts of Evtec.

"Over the next decade... ripple effects crept into all manufacturing," he says. "So they lost infrastructure capability. They lost advanced manufacturing capability.

"They're vulnerable. They're not resilient in those sectors anymore."

"It is a drop in the ocean but every single opportunity is worth having, isn't it?"

A spokesperson for the Ministry of Defence says it wants UK industry to play a "central role" in delivering thousands of modern light mobility vehicles. The spokesperson adds: "We are backing British businesses and supporting our defence industrial base with 85% of our defence spending currently staying in the UK, driving reindustrialisation and making defence an engine for growth."
