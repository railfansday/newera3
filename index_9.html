#459. Stellantis stock rises 13% as automaker's turnaround plan, cost cuts gain traction

Shares of Stellantis were up Wednesday as the European car giant's third-quarter results showed it was making progress with its 60 billion euro turnaround plan.

Third-quarter revenues rose 1% to 37.6 billion euros from a year ago, the Chrysler and Jeep owner said on Wednesday, while adjusted operating income rose 184% to 256 million euros from 90 million euros a year earlier.

The automaker's industrial free cash flow improved to negative 4 billion euros, up from negative 4.3 billion the year prior.

Stellantis on Wednesday also reconfirmed its 2026 guidance and target to achieve positive free cash flow next year, which is a main focus for investors.

"Q3 was soft, but consensus expectations were far lower. Key here is that '26 Guidance was maintained while other auto OEMs and suppliers are cutting on elevated costs," RBC Capital Markets analyst Tom Narayan said Wednesday in an investor note.

Stellantis stock on the New York Stock Exchange rose 13% on Wednesday to close at $4.69 a share. Shares in Milan closed at 4.28 euros, up 15.4%. Shares in both markets remain sharply down since the beginning of the year.

Stellantis CEO Antonio Filosa told investors Wednesday that the company is ahead of schedule on its turnaround plan, which was announced in May. He said Stellantis expects to exceed its previously announced cost-cutting efforts of 6 billion euros under the plan by 2028, including 2.4 billion euros to start 2027.

"We are very confident in reaffirming our '26 guidance. Those include '27 free cash flow positive," Filosa said Wednesday in response to concerns about the carmaker's ability to achieve its free cash flow target.

Stellantis' 2026 guidance includes a mid-single-digit percentage increase in net revenue and a low-single-digit adjusted operating margin.

Stellantis' turnaround plan includes investing 36 billion euros in its portfolio of automotive brands, with 60% of the cash to go towards its struggling North America sector. It also plans to introduce new vehicles, refresh models, and incorporate new technologies in its products.

Stellantis is targeting roughly 23% revenue growth, from 154 billion euros last year to 190 billion euros by 2030, with a 7% adjusted operating margin by then.

European carmakers, including Stellantis, have been struggling in China as domestic brands like Xiaomi, BYD and Geely gain ground and intensify competition.

Just this week, German luxury carmaker Porsche said it would reduce its exposure to China as car sales decline in the region. Meanwhile, Mercedes-Benz said its third-quarter car sales slumped due to a "challenging market environment," in the country.

Earlier this month, Filosa told CNBC that European regulators need to do more to incentivize carmakers to manufacture vehicles in the region to fend off Chinese rivals.

Filosa called for regulation that "incentivizes, rewards, and protects the ones that are able to demonstrate that they design cars, engineer cars."

The decision means that schools choosing to restrict AI altogether will no longer be able to access Google Search, as AI features are now integrated into the search engine.

#447. Rivian spinout Also was working on an e-moto project in Kenya — until it walked away

Also, the micromobility startup spun out of Rivian in 2025, quietly started and then walked away from an electric two-wheeler pilot project in Kenya earlier this year, as it sought to focus its resources on its consumer e-bike and autonomous vehicle ambitions, TechCrunch has learned.

The project received grant funding from the Shell Foundation, an independent charity established by the British oil and gas giant Shell plc. A section of the Shell Foundation’s website that has since been removed said the project’s goal was to “enable and de-risk global OEM entry into Africa’s EV market by piloting a locally integrated electric two-wheeler platform in Kenya, with potential to scale across East Africa.”

According to the deleted section, Also was expected to work on modular battery designs and distributed EV charging, and planned on “developing a purpose-built electric motorcycle for African riders.”

The opportunity for electric motorcycles seems ripe on the continent, which has a massive two-wheeler market. African mobility company Spiro recently said it has more than 130,000 electric motorcycles operating across seven countries, along with 2,500 battery-swapping stations. This boom has drawn interest from Chinese mobility companies and investors, which are pouring resources into the space.

In a September email, Also told TechCrunch that it had “explored this project” with an unnamed partner, but ultimately “decided to focus on its core roadmap” after completing only “some early-stage work.” Also said the grant funding it received from the Shell Foundation “will follow the project to support its ongoing development,” but declined to offer specifics.

The Shell Foundation told TechCrunch in an email that the project was “intended to generate evidence on driver income uplift potential, operational performance and commercial viability to deepen the company’s understanding of the market opportunity.” But when TechCrunch asked for more specifics, the Shell Foundation declined, citing a nondisclosure agreement with Also. Days later, the project disappeared from the Shell Foundation’s website.

During an interview last month at TechCrunch Disrupt, Also co-founder and president Chris Yu said the Kenya pilot project initially made sense given Also’s mission to build small, purpose-built vehicles that are “first and foremost cost-optimized to do the job that needs to be done in a particular market.”

But other projects took precedence, Yu said, especially the development of small autonomous vehicles — which Also is working on as part of a partnership with DoorDash.

“We piloted and ultimately decided, largely because of just urgency and pace around autonomy, frankly, that our attention would be best, in the near term, served fully focused” on that, he said, adding that he thinks there’s a “high chance that autonomy will show up first, and at a broader scale in these types of purpose-built smaller vehicles than at scale in passenger vehicles.”

Also walked away from the Africa project during a somewhat rocky first year of independence.

Supply chain problems forced the company to repeatedly delay deliveries of its first product, a premium e-bike called the TM-B, frustrating customers who had reserved one. What made matters worse was Also’s relative silence. The company said little for much of 2026 about when the bikes would arrive — a communication failure its leadership now acknowledges was a mistake.

“I think we did a pretty poor job communicating to our reservation holders through the summer when they were all waiting, and we got called out for that,” Yu said in the interview. “But I think it’s actually great. It’s really hard to earn the opportunity for people to care, and to tell us what they think, and to help us improve.”

Getting its first product launch back on track is crucial for Also, which has ambitions far beyond e-bikes. The company plans to sell commercial cargo bikes to companies like Amazon, including four-wheeled models, and has the partnership with DoorDash. These products are next in the company’s pipeline, Yu said, and represent bigger revenue opportunities.

But the TM-B comes first. It’s Also’s most visible product to consumers, and its striking design has drawn plenty of curiosity.

Yu said no single problem was responsible for delaying the bikes, which finally started shipping to customers in early October. Instead, Also spent much of the year playing supply chain Whac-A-Mole, a particularly difficult challenge given the TM-B’s complexity. Yu described the bike as “kind of a computer on wheels.”

At one point, Also found itself competing for the same raw materials used to make printed circuit boards for AI data centers. At other times, the company struggled to navigate certification requirements with different government agencies.

Yu said the scope of these problems, and the pace at which new ones popped up — almost weekly — made it difficult to give customers reliable updates about why their bikes were delayed.

“The entire team is like running at a million miles an hour to get these things produced and shipped to customers, and then it’s also really dynamic as well,” he said. “I think what we could have done better is just [say], straight up: ‘We’re chasing down these things, we’ll have an update shortly.’”

That didn’t happen at first, but Also has steadily improved its communication, earning some goodwill from customers on Reddit. The company will need it as it works through the inevitable kinks that come with a first product. Early customers have already reported problems, including delivery damage and noticeable battery drain.

#444. Anthropic is still grappling with the economics of compute

Buy One Founder Summit Ticket, Get a Second for Free. Register now.

Founder Summit begins Nov. 4, 9:00 a.m. ET. Get your pass savings & deals before it starts. REGISTER HERE.

There’s a lot of mystery around the finances of major AI labs, and as Anthropic inches towards it IPO, we’re starting to get some answers. In particular, the company is finally disclosing more about the economics of compute, and how much of Anthropic’s skyrocketing revenue is being lost to compute costs.

In a new filing on Monday, Anthropic released data that showed those margins on compute actually getting worse. The filing was an update to the S-1 released last week — but where that filing’s financial disclosures only covered through June 30 of this year, the new data extended to disclose results through an extra quarter, to September 30. Broadly, Anthropic had a good quarter: revenue reached $17 billion, up from $11.5 billion in the previous quarter, suggesting durable growth. But the operating loss grew at the same time, thanks in large part to growing compute costs.

Looking at the compute margins makes the picture even worse. In the new quarter, compute took up 58% of the company’s revenue, up from 48% the quarter before. At a moment when the company could win over IPO investors by showing margins improving, they’re getting worse.

A chart in the filing shows this even more clearly, tracking the company’s revenue for every dollar spent on compute. Since the start of 2025, it’s been steadily growing, peaking at just over 2x this past quarter, then falling back down to 1.72x.

Many indications imply that this shift in compute margins is more of a blip than a long-term problem, but it still tells us important things about the business of running a major lab. Trying to keep up with demand, Anthropic’s compute bill shot up to $10 billion for the quarter, including, as the filing notes, “$5.0 billion of compute under short-term commitments, which currently costs significantly more than comparable compute procured under our multi-year commitments.” Basically, demand was growing so fast that Anthropic had to scramble for compute, which meant paying higher rates.

Some of those deals have already played out in public. Anthropic has been paying SpaceX $1.25 billion a month for compute, alongside multibillion-dollar deals with Nscale, Akamai, and Volta. In the parlance of the industry, these are neoclouds rather than hyperscalers — smaller outfits that offer more flexible compute deals at higher rates.

Anthropic’s margins got worse in Q3 because of deals like these, and there’s no sign they’ll be able to do without them anytime soon. As the new filing puts it, “We expect compute and infrastructure expenses to increase in absolute dollars and remain our largest operating expense for the foreseeable future as additional committed compute becomes available to us under multi-year arrangements with CSPs, and as we continue to invest in additional compute.”

Anthropic is still trying to lower its compute costs. In particular, the company talks quite a bit about its efforts to build its own compute base under a project it calls “Anthropic Direct.” But with demand growing so fast, those efforts aren’t going to be able to keep pace, and the neoclouds will continue to be the best option they have.

In a sense, these are champagne problems. As long as revenue is growing at nearly 50% a quarter, few investors will worry about high compute costs — and the moment demand slows down, investors may be comforted by a compute picture that has gotten a lot sunnier. But it’s a reminder of how much AI labs still depend on the trillion-dollar infrastructure buildout — and how vulnerable they are to hiccups in that process. It’s easy to imagine the data center buildout slowing down over the next year if bond markets get skittish or local opposition gets more intense. There’s not much Anthropic can do to address either of those risks, and either one would make a messy compute picture a whole lot messier.

In the meantime, the future of Anthropic and other labs will be at the mercy of compute prices.

Cybersecurity giant CrowdStrike is buying two AI security companies that expand its products to more broadly monitor, test, and cover AI models and agents, as companies across industries start rolling out AI agents and models wholesale.

CrowdStrike said it will buy Fiddler AI, which makes observability software for monitoring AI agents inside enterprises, and Dreadnode, which helps companies automate security testing specifically aimed at detecting flaws in AI models, apps, and agents before they are set live.

The company is paying around $400 million for Fiddler AI, and about $95 million for Dreadnode, according to The Wall Street Journal.

The deals are happening at a time of significant upheaval in the cybersecurity space, especially as companies’ policies and processes lag behind AI adoption within their walls. Teams and individual employees alike are experimenting with AI tools, automating workflows, vibe-coding custom apps, and deploying armies of AI agents to do their bidding, which is understandably causing headaches for security teams.

CrowdStrike, whose products are used widely across enterprises and governments, is clearly trying to leverage its already significant heft and presence to capture a share of this quickly burgeoning market. It’s not alone, of course, as we’ve already seen several startups and cybersecurity vendors raising dozens of millions in funding to solve issues for companies, though most of these approaches are essentially based on the conviction that existing security software isn’t enough to monitor and control AI tools, connectors, or usage.

The company has itself been building detection and response controls, dubbed Falcon Guardian, to help companies see and understand what agents are doing on their devices, and these acquisitions seem to further flesh out its offerings.

Fiddler AI is going to be part of CrowdStrike’s Falcon platform as Falcon Observe, where it’ll ostensibly help the company’s clients evaluate agents before they’re deployed, understand their actions, and monitor what they do, and feed that information as telemetry into the broader platform. Dreadnode, meanwhile, will be integrated into the Falcon Guardian product, to which the startup’s software will feed its testing results as security policy recommendations.

Founded in 2018, Fiddler AI has raised a total of about $99 million to date, according to PitchBook. Dreadnode, founded in 2023, has raised $14 million so far.

In the lead-up to a presidential election that would irrefutably transform American culture, Hillary Clinton took the stage at a rally in Virginia as she campaigned to become the first female president.

The only thing about that political moment that hasn’t changed is that “Pokémon Go to the polls” memes still crop up on every Election Day.

“It was very bold and confident, and also just sounded so terribly out of touch and corny,” Cesar Ruiz de Castilla, an elected ward committee member in Philadelphia, told TechCrunch. “I don’t know if the mainstream media cared that much about it, but everyone was making a meme about it and talking about it online.”

Since then, digital culture has become increasingly inextricable from the fate of American politics. Donald Trump tweeted his way through his first term, then later started his own social media platform; presidential campaigns hire meme managers; the White House invites podcasters and content creators to press briefings; Elon Musk even spun up his own advisory group under Trump that named itself after the cutesy doge meme and then slashed international aid programs.

“The 2016 election was such a monumental moment in internet history, and we were all there for it — our politics have become more, not just divided, but segmented online,” Organizer Memes, a political influencer who teaches Gen Z activists how to reach their peers online via memes, told TechCrunch. “It’s a relic of an optimistic, bygone political era.”

Now, people who weren’t even old enough to vote in 2016 reflect on the year as a cultural turning point, even calling it “the last good year.” It’s a sentiment that isn’t so much political as it is rooted in nostalgia for an era of internet culture before AI slop, when we used social media to connect with friends, rather than doomscrolling through an algorithmic feed of people we don’t even follow.

At the start of the new year, this yearning for the old internet became a viral sentiment as millions of people shared throwback photos from 2016 on Instagram.

Spotify reported that user-generated playlists featuring 2016 in their titles spiked 790% in the first three weeks of the year, and the company changed its Instagram bio at the time to say it’s “romanticizing 2016 again.”

Never mind that, at the time, the internet perceived 2016 as a cursed year, even before the election — months earlier, one Slate columnist wondered how 2016 compared to 1348, the year the Black Death took hold in Europe.

“[Pokémon Go to the polls] is in that era of the singular internet, when Facebook and Twitter and Tumblr were all kind of bouncing content back-and-forth and not just this complete anarchic mess of 25 platforms that we now have and don’t use,” Organizer Memes said.

Organizer Memes also notes that some cultural touchstones that felt more significant then — like the obsession with Harambe the gorilla — haven’t had as much staying power as “Pokémon Go to the polls.”

Perhaps this is because we’re bound to remember “Pokémon Go to the polls” every few years on Election Day, and because it appeals to both sides of the aisle. On the right, it’s a way to make fun of Democrats, and on the left, it’s a shared groan of embarrassment.

“It’s like if all the kids in a class are making a joke, and then the teacher gets in on the joke, and it stops being funny,” Mack Finkel, who canvassed at the polls on Tuesday on behalf of a healthcare workers’ union, told TechCrunch. “I think there’s a key essence of millennial cringe happening here, and it’s the same reason that all my friends are rewatching [HBO’s] ‘Girls.’”

Gen Z rallied around Harris by creating flashy, absurdist “fan cam”-style edits of the candidate’s best quips in the aesthetic of Charli XCX’s album “Brat,” which soundtracked the summer of 2024.

“Previously the views I saw about Harris were making fun of her being a cop, like her history as attorney general in California with truancy laws,” Jess Rauchberg, an assistant professor of communications at the University of Leeds, told TechCrunch at the time. “Now we see that same style of memeification, but it’s an endorsement.”

It’s hard to say why “Pokémon Go to the polls” jokes still make us laugh — whether we’re mocking Clinton or expressing solidarity with others who keep showing up to vote, even as each passing year leaves us feeling more cynical.

“Pokémon Go to the polls” is a way of urging others to make the time to vote and take Election Day seriously, while still acknowledging disappointment with aging lawmakers who don’t know what it’s like to come of age in the social media era. It’s shrouded in enough irony to project a sense of jaded detachment, yet remains a desperate, earnest plea to our friends: Please, just go vote.

A satellite built by the German startup Orbital Matter 3D-printed a half-meter long beam out of a custom plastic material, in what executives called a first for a free-flying spacecraft in orbit, and a step toward constructing large structures in space.

The company’s small satellite, Replicator 2, was launched on a SpaceX rideshare mission this summer, with four of the company’s 3D printers onboard. Each printer can create a rigid, cylindrical beam by extruding coils of resin that are cured by an ultraviolet light, which the company analogizes to cement and water.

This technology matters because future spacecraft will need to be larger in order to generate more electrical power and radiate a more heat into the vacuum of space, particularly if they’re going to serve as orbital data centers. But they’ll still need to fit into the tight confines of the fairing on top of the rocket that carries them into orbit. If spacecraft are able to construct their own structures in orbit, the power and heat-dissipation challenges may get a lot easier to solve.

“Replicator 2 is the first proof that we can simply build the array in space, at whatever size the mission demands,” said Jakub Stojek, CEO and co-founder of Orbital Matter. The company is partnering with the European Space Agency and Thales Alenia, a leading European satellite builder, with which it collaborated on Replicator 2.

Today’s large structures in space are origami projects — large solar arrays or telescope mirrors are folded up and then deployed on orbit. That’s a tricky engineering project, and so the idea of making and assembling those structures in space is appealing. NASA has funded some efforts at the idea, notably a mission with the company Redwire that 3D-printed structural beams in a vacuum chamber in 2023 but never got off the ground.

Anthropic is working on an open source system to verify which AI model produced a given output, according to a blog post published Tuesday. The system, called FlashProve, is built to provide what researchers call “provable inference” — a cryptographic proof, essentially a mathematical guarantee, that a given output came from a specific model. It’s meant to let users confirm that the answer they received really came from the model they think they’re using.

Similar to cryptographic email signatures that let recipients confirm a message really came from its claimed sender, FlashProve would be robust against impostors attempting to disguise one model as another, even when the impostor is the lab that built and runs the model. In particular, the researchers note that the system could be used to prevent an AI company from secretly swapping in a cheaper model to cut costs, or a more expensive model to inflate its performance results.

FlashProve could also be used to verify claims by third parties that a specific task was done by an advanced model. “A developer might claim that their software library was scanned for vulnerabilities by a frontier cybersecurity model, or a company might claim that its business plan was given the go-ahead by an appropriate model,” the researchers write. “How can a user verify these claims?”

To work, the system requires AI labs to publish a “fingerprint” of each model they release that can later be matched to specific outputs and verified cryptographically. Crucially, the fingerprints don’t reveal a model’s weights, the internal numbers that define how it behaves and which closed-model makers like Anthropic and OpenAI keep private. That means labs could publish the fingerprints without giving up their most valuable asset, enabling the system to work with commercial models, too. The system is also designed to be lightweight, so verification could be performed on a local device.

As part of the announcement, researchers released a detailed technical specification and working code for various elements of the system, which is publicly available under an open source license on GitHub. Research in the system is ongoing, and there are no plans to deploy it at Anthropic or any other lab.

“This research post is meant to share intermediate progress, and not a completed research agenda,” researchers explain in the announcement post. “We are sharing this explicitly with the goal that others look at what we have done, take it, and improve it.”
