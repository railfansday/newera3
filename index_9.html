#483. The tech products we enthusiastically recommend this holiday season

We’ve been reviewing a bunch of new gadgets and hardware at TechCrunch this year: dumb phones, bird feeders, health trackers, slushy machines, AI mattress pads, you name it. Sometimes we think they’re unnecessary and expensive, but other times, we try out devices that we love so much that we go out and buy them ourselves.

If these products can impress a group of (sometimes) curmudgeonly tech reporters, then they’re sure to please whoever you’re shopping for this holiday season.

We’ve reviewed a lot of e-readers this year. What can we say? We’re nerds. We work for a tech website. But after trying devices from the usual suspects as well as some wildcards, we have recommendations. Evidently, 2026 was the year we fell in love with the pocket-sized e-reader.

It’s an e-reader so small that it can fit in your wallet, or even magnetize to the back of your phone. Do you dream of a world where instead of scrolling Instagram when you’re waiting in line for coffee, you actually opt to read books? There’s really something special about the X3, and it’s only $55 during Xteink’s Black Friday sale. Fair warning, it takes some fiddling with to set up, and it has no integration with Kindle, Libby, or any other apps, so only buy this for someone willing to do a bit of Reddit browsing and tinkering to get it up and running.

If you aren’t planning to magnetize the XTeink to your phone, you’ve got to go with the X4 Pro. It’s slightly bigger, but it has a backlight, which makes it way more versatile. After using both the X3 and the X4 Pro, I find that the backlight really is a game changer, since I do a lot of reading in bed — plus, I like my Pop Socket wallet a bit too much to displace for the X3, and I’d rather just carry an extra credit card-sized device than risk forgetting my actual credit and ID cards, which I have grown too used to having on my phone.

If I’m being honest, reading my colleague Aisha Malik’s review of the Boox Palma Pro 2 is making me want to buy one. I love using the Xteink e-readers, but they don’t connect to any outside apps. As a hardcore Libby power user (a phrase I am saying in earnest sincerity), it’s a bit frustrating that I can’t get my library books on Xteink e-readers. The same goes for apps like Kobo and Kindle, where any e-book you already own cannot be transferred out of the Amazon ecosystem unless the publisher agrees, which most do not.

If you’re looking for an e-reader with a screen that’s actually book-sized, my favorite that I’ve used is the Kobo Libra Colour, which is $259. The Amazon Paperwhite line is great, but c’mon, Amazon. Branch out!

Hey, we’ve all got to eat. And if you want, you can make that eating process overly techy. Why not?

The Ember Mug 3 is the latest iteration in Ember’s line of coffee/tea cups that actually stay warm. Our reviewer Lauren Forristal especially appreciated that you no longer need to use your phone app to change the mug’s temperature, which seems like it would be really annoying!

This mug is perfect for gifting to someone, or to put on your own wishlist — you might not want to spend $99 on a mug for yourself, but these sorts of luxury, nice-to-haves are what holidays are made for!

Ah, Ninja, the premiere company in the business of turning every appliance into a two-in-one air fryer. I jest, but at least Ninja knows it’s onto something. Despite the high price point, Lauren says that this new product made “a pretty convincing case for ditching two appliances and making room for one.” You can’t buy more counter space (without moving), but you can buy more versatile appliances.

Sure, there are cheaper ice makers on the market. But Aisha clarifies that this is the ice maker for “those who crave what’s called ‘the good ice.’” If you feel so passionately about the ice in your beverages that you know what she means, then maybe this is for you.

What they really want is a Mac mini or Claude subscription, but if that’s out of budget, we have some other ideas that might impress your AI-pilled, vibe-coder loved ones…

When I asked my colleague Lucas Ropek what about his favorite product he reviewed this year, he mentioned the Codex Micro. Some hard-core coders — who the product is aimed toward — have found this thing less than pleasing, but Lucas seems to have had fun figuring out this first attempt by OpenAI at hardware.

Best case, it’s a productivity game-changer for your friend who has a hardworking team of AI agents within the OpenAI ecosystem. Worst case, this will be a great piece of tech history in a few years.

As people have become more conscious about how much time they spend online, new tech has emerged that, ironically, is designed to keep you away from tech. Some of these products can really make a difference in your life.

For about six months, I’ve consistently used the Brick to prevent me from using social media as the last thing I do before bed, or the first thing in the morning. Some people use it to help them focus during the work day, but evidently, I was not the only one who needed Brick’s help with sleep. The company recently launched its second device, Brick Night, which “Bricks” your phone when you are in the same room. You can customize the times that the Brick Night is active, so it’s not as though you can never, ever use social media in your room. Both Brick and Brick Night are very customizable, so you can create the perfect set-up tailored to your needs.

It’s embarrassing how whole-heartedly I recommend the Brick, which is basically just a hunk of plastic with an NFC chip in it and a companion app. But my sleep has improved significantly — I even do 8 AM Pilates now. Who am I?

I’ve tried out far pricier smart phone alternatives, but the Dumb Phone was both the most affordable, and the most usable for me. In the humble shell of a $20 TCL flip phone, Dumb Co loads its own software so that users can access apps like WhatsApp, Spotify, Apple Music, and Uber. You can even access iMessage through a third-party app, though Apple could probably get that shut down… no one tell Apple, please!

The Dumb Phone is particularly usable because it’s not intended to be a complete replacement for your smartphone — it’s more like a companion device that you can take out with you when you want to feel more present in the world but still may need to call an Uber home.

Wearable health trackers are pretty common nowadays, but after reviewing several different rings and wristbands, Aisha has named a winner.

There are a lot of ugly health trackers. And then there’s the Oura Ring 5, the leading smart ring maker’s first product that’s thin enough to wear without loudly broadcasting to the world that you’re monitoring your heart rate. (For what it’s worth, I tried the RingConn 3 — which is about the same size as Oura Ring 5 — but returned it, unsatisfied. Womp womp.) Aisha notes in her review that the newest Oura ring is more durable, has a better battery life, and looks more premium than its predecessors.

#480. TechCrunch Mobility: The Nvidia backup plan for China’s self-driving industry

Welcome back to TechCrunch Mobility, your hub for the future of transportation and now, more than ever, the role AI is playing in it. To get this in your inbox, sign up here for free — just click TechCrunch Mobility!

I am traveling through Portugal now that Web Summit has wrapped up, so please enjoy this shortened edition.

Web Summit leaned heavily into Chinese founders and companies this year. The opening remarks from Web Summit co-founder Paddy Cosgrave drove the point home when he said the superstars of the future are coming from China, not California. He also gave a strong endorsement of China and open source technology when he noted that Chinese companies are building more of our shared future than anyone else.

These remarks carried a different kind of weight just a few days later as the news spread throughout the conference that Nvidia founder and CEO Jensen Huang had resigned from his leadership post after admitting to an affair with an alleged spy.

The former CEO, after all, has been one of the strongest proponents for open source in the United States. And anyone who has followed the emergence of autonomous vehicle tech knows how central Nvidia is to that ecosystem.

Nvidia is a major supplier of AI computing technology to the industry — although the degree of reliance varies. Some companies are using Nvidia’s automotive processors to run automated driving software, while others are using Nvidia GPUs to train autonomous driving models. And then there are the full-stack partnerships, which includes Mercedes-Benz, Zoox, and Wayve.

I spoke to Leo Wang, the CFO of Chinese autonomous vehicle company Pony.ai, this week. I asked him about the challenges China’s self-driving industry faces and what he thinks will happen if the fallout from Huang’s resignation results in even stricter export controls on Nvidia chips shipped to China.

Demand is already far greater than supply, Wang said, noting that it’s one of the pain points Pony.ai struggles with. The company’s strategy is twofold. Engineers are directed to be as efficient as possible when deploying its training models. That might mean using midtier compute resources instead of high-end ones, he said.

Pony.ai is also turning more to domestically produced chips to help close the gap.

“If you look at the whole ecosystem, especially in recent years, definitely domestic chips in China also get lots of attention and development,” he said, noting that these chips provide another option for AI training. “I think that gives us some flexibility.”

When asked what he thinks will happen if export restrictions on Nvidia chips tighten, Wang said Chinese companies have already been preparing.

“If you look at the whole situation, this has been developing for already a couple of years,” he said. “Everyone, I would say, is also practicing kind of like a Plan B for a few years, so at least if you think about that, I would say there are alternatives. Although cost-effectiveness-wise, probably it’s not the best choice yet. But still, it’s an alternative way that already exists and which is more mature than a few years ago.”

May Mobility plans to use the Volkswagen all-electric ID. Buzz for its global ride-hailing platform. The company said it is targeting the deployment of more than 10,000 autonomous vehicles through 2031. This does not mean that VW’s MOIA group is ditching Mobileye, I have been told. Instead, VW told me, the company is offering an automated driving-ready base vehicle to other companies that develop their own self-driving systems. Newmo, a Japanese robotaxi startup, raised a ¥20.8 billion (roughly $135 million) round led by KDDI.

ParkBee, a Dutch parking technology company, acquired Mobian’s city and parking operations.

Port, an urban logistics company, raised €14.5 million in a round led by the European Bank for Reconstruction and Development (EBRD) and Balnord.

Lucid reported Q3 revenue of $350 million, below Wall Street expectations, and it is still losing money. Although its Q3 net loss did narrow to $830.9 million. Notably, the EV maker increased its delayed draw term loan from its largest shareholder, Saudi Arabia’s Public Investment Fund, to $1.75 billion.

The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration is investigating a Waymo software recall from a year ago that was issued after numerous reports that its robotaxis were illegally passing school buses. The agency said that a majority of these reported incidents occurred after the company deployed a software update intended to fix the problem. Sources familiar with Waymo tell me that there have not been school bus-related incidents since March. I have not been able to independently verify that with the Austin Independent School District.

The Tesla Cybertruck will live on through next summer, but production will ultimately end, according to Elon Musk, who defended the polarizing steel-clad truck.

Tesla is expanding its robotaxi service to San Antonio, Texas, as it seeks to compete with Waymo and position itself as more than just an automaker.

Uber is launching a new service that lets customers reserve and pay for parking in major U.S. cities, about nine months after the ride-hailing company acquired parking app SpotHero. #476. Flaming Hydra’s new Kickstarter campaign rethinks e-book ownership and digital lending

Here’s a big promise: A new Kickstarter campaign aims to usher in the “return of the human internet.”

That’s the tagline of a crowdfunding campaign for The Phoenix Project, an initiative from the online culture and commentary magazine Flaming Hydra. If you back The Phoenix Project, you’re supporting three things — a one-off print magazine designed to celebrate “the values and fun of the early internet,” a new e-book service for libraries, and a newsletter subscription/archiving program that’s also designed for libraries.

To Flaming Hydra’s lead editor Maria Bustillos, these disparate projects are “basically all one thing,” tied together by the goal of “protecting freedom of information” and fulfilling the “promise of the early internet, where you’re making information available to as many people as possible, while still protecting the right of artists and authors to make a living.”

Bustillos told me that these issues have been highlighted on Flaming Hydra itself, for example when Tal Levin wrote about finding countless AI-generated clones of his book on Amazon. By “creating an ecosystem that is based in people working together, who are offering their stuff in a context that is controlled and doesn’t allow” others to create and sell AI-generated clones, Bustillos said Flaming Hydra is “part of the human project.”

“AI is not this unstoppable force,” she added. “If people just set up publishing pipelines and networks and organizations that are dedicated to the human internet, people who want to read stuff can go to them, and it’s actually completely isolated from mechanistic interference.”

The Phoenix Project fits into this vision by making the work of human writers more accessible to libraries.

Back in 2020, Bustillos wrote about the e-book systems dominating libraries and schools. In one example, instead of just buying a book, a school had to pay $27 per student per year for continued access to copies of “The Diary of Anne Frank.”

This is symptomatic of a broader system that treats true ownership of e-books and other digital media as a thing of the past. Instead, we “license” these e-books from their publishers, and they can be altered or removed at any time.

“E-books are books,” Bustillos said. “They’re not some other thing. They are books, and people should be able to own them, and libraries should be able to own them, and media should be able to be owned. All digital media should be able to be owned.”

In her view, ownership boils down to a simple question: “If you are at the mercy of somebody who can shut the book off, you do not own it.”

So even beyond the question of ongoing payments, there have also been examples of e-books being altered or removed from readers’ devices long after their purchase. Bustillos acknowledged that for individual pieces of media, this distinction may not seem like a big deal, “but if it’s all the textbooks for everybody, if it’s all the books,” then it’s a real problem.

Flaming Hydra and The Phoenix Project seek to solve this by letting libraries truly purchase and own e-books. They can then lend those e-books to their patrons through a new, open source “library in a box” system called Lenny for Libraries.

The Phoenix Project is starting by offering five new e-books from Flaming Hydra writers, including collections of writing from The Awl, and of essays by Tom Scocca and Bustillos herself. That might sound like a relatively small start, but Bustillos said she’s talking to library consortiums and author groups, and the goal here is to “change the conversation” around e-books and digital archiving and lending. So The Phoenix Project could be paving the way for other initiatives that follow a similar model.

Similarly, The Phoenix Foundry Archive is designed to make paywalled newsletter content available to libraries. Bustillos noted that “so much of the best journalism, criticism, essays” is now being published in paywalled newsletters that are inaccessible to libraries (as well as the Internet Archive).

Flaming Hydra has been working on all this even before launching the Kickstarter, and Bustillos said she was grateful for the financial support from backers like Anand Raganathan, Brewster Kahle, and Paul Ford. But by turning to crowdfunding, Bustillos said Flaming Hydra can better protect the work, ensuring that it remains sustainable and independent.

“We don’t have to be at the mercy of anybody, and it also protects editorial independence,” she said. “Those things go hand in hand.”

Flaming Hydra seeks to raise $30,000 for The Phoenix Project, with the campaign ending on November 27.

#470. Is bad news for Jensen Huang also bad news for the AI industry?

We didn’t see this coming. Or at least, I didn’t — a week ago, when I wrote about Nvidia’s board opening an investigation into then-CEO Jensen Huang, I assumed his job was safe.

On the latest episode of TechCrunch’s Equity podcast, Rebecca Bellan, Tim De Chant, and I discussed how Huang’s ouster went down and what it means for the larger AI economy. While Tim said he saw it coming (albeit not this quickly), Rebecca said, “I didn’t think it was going to happen at all.”

“Jensen is Nvidia, right?” she added. “And so much of this economy is riding on Nvidia.”

Similarly, I marveled at how quickly Huang’s support seemed to crumble, with more scandals seeming to emerge every day. (At the same time, there are many who’d still like to see him return.) And we also took a moment to wonder what will happen to Nvidia’s investments in the larger AI ecosystem.

Tim De Chant: I figured this was coming. I didn’t think it would happen this quickly. I thought there would be at least a few days.

Rebecca Bellan: I didn’t think it was going to happen at all. I mean, Jensen is Nvidia, right? And so much of this economy is riding on Nvidia. And we’ve got nothing but the last few years of data to show us that billionaires can do whatever the hell they want. So it was surprising to me  — it almost restored my faith in humanity, that he resigned.

#467. Indian chip startup Bharat Semi lands $24M seed round as it eyes global AI sensor market

Bharat Semi, an Indian semiconductor startup, has raised $24 million in seed funding to develop AI-enabled image sensors, as it seeks to win customers in the United States and elsewhere.

The investment comes as the nearly five-year-old, pre-revenue startup says it has secured design wins with U.S. defense technology company General Atomics and India’s Kaynes Semicon. Its image sensors, however, remain under development, with the startup targeting its initial commercial production in 2027.

India has committed billions of dollars to build up a domestic semiconductor industry, with a hope to become an alternative manufacturing hub as U.S. companies look to diversify supply chains beyond China. The country, however, has yet to establish itself as a major chip manufacturing hub. Meanwhile, the AI boom is also giving Indian startups such as Bharat Semi an opportunity to develop specialized chips for global markets.

The New Delhi-based startup is developing programmable image sensors that combine image capture, processing, and AI capabilities on a single chip, reducing the need to send visual data to separate processors. The move could cut system costs by at least 70% compared with conventional setups that use a separate image sensor, image signal processor, and a system-on-chip, co-founder Vrinda Kapoor (pictured above, right) said in an interview.

“Our aim here is to move from sensors to sense-making solutions,” Kapoor told TechCrunch.

Kapoor, a South Park Commons fellow who earlier worked in healthcare technology, co-founded Bharat Semi in late 2021 with Vinayak Dalmia (pictured above, left), the startup’s managing director, who previously worked at McKinsey and in private equity. Its chief technology officer, Boyd Fowler, previously held the same role at image-sensor maker OmniVision.

Bharat Semi is targeting smart homes, industrial automation, surveillance, and aerospace. Without disclosing specifics, Kapoor pointed to air conditioners that could detect when a room is empty and adjust automatically and drones that could process visual information — even with the absence of more expensive computing hardware elsewhere in the system.

The startup’s first-generation sensors are already in prototyping, while its second-generation sensors, which will incorporate more advanced AI capabilities, are expected to reach the prototype stage by mid-2027, Kapoor said. Moreover, the startup plans to get those newer sensors into customers’ hands by the end of 2027.

The all-equity seed round was led by Info Edge Group, South Park Commons, and Indus Bridge Ventures, with participation from Valour Capital, Micromax, Gaingels, and individual investors. Bharat Semi, Kapoor said, plans to use the funding to expand its engineering team, complete product development, and bring its sensors to market.

Bharat Semi has about 75 employees across India and the U.S., including 10 to 12 in its American operations, which span engineering and business development in Santa Clara. The startup plans to increase its U.S. headcount to around 20 by mid-2027, Kapoor said. It has also begun hiring engineers in Europe.

“It’s a story of can we build a global technology company out of India,” Kapoor said.

In 2024, Bharat Semi announced plans for a semiconductor fabrication project in India involving a technology partnership with the U.S. Space Force. The initiative, Kapoor said, remains in development and the startup is working to identify domestic partners that would build and operate the facility.

The project is separate from Bharat Semi’s image-sensor business, Kapoor added. The startup plans to remain fabless, outsourcing chip manufacturing while focusing on semiconductor design and software. The new funding will not be used to finance the proposed fabrication facility.

#465. Cosm rides premium moviegoing wave with more immersive screenings and a wider library

The next big thing in premium large format theatrical viewing takes inspiration from the stars.

Cosm, an immersive entertainment technology company, got its start programming planetariums — its name a combination of "cosmos" and "colosseum." The company's massive, domed screens first offered guests a view of the universe, now they mimic courtside seats at sports venues around the world and drop film fans into some of Hollywood's most iconic sets.

At a time when the theatrical industry is seeing its biggest growth from the sale of premium large format, or PLF, tickets, Cosm seeks to offer a fresh take on an elevated viewing experience. And it's quickly expanding both its footprint and its content library.

Since 2024, Cosm has opened four theaters — near Los Angeles, Dallas, Atlanta and Detroit — with a Cleveland hub set to open in 2027. These experimental venues have garnered the attention of sports fans, screening live matchups in an arena-like environment. Showings often sell out, according to Cosm.

More recently, Cosm has partnered with Hollywood studios like Warner Bros., now a part of Skydance, to bring the likes of "The Matrix," "Willy Wonka & the Chocolate Factory," "Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone" and "It" to its locations. Cosm recently signed a deal with Universal, which will bring "Shrek" to its domes in December.

The agreements mark a lucrative new chapter for the privately held Cosm, expanding its audience reach and viewing opportunities beyond timed, live events.

As a privately held company, Cosm doesn't disclose its financials. Tickets cost anywhere from $18 for a matinee to around $70 for prime seats or in-demand showings. That doesn't include added fees for concessions or items from special, themed menus tied to screenings.

Sony invested $100 million in the company in June, taking a minority stake and placing Sony Pictures Entertainment Chairman and CEO Ravi Ahuja onto its board of directors.

Cosm also counts among its investors Cosm chairman Steve Winn's firm, Mirasol Capital; former Milwaukee Bucks co-owner Marc Lasry's Avenue Sports Fund; Rocket Mortgage co-founder Dan Gilbert's Rock; and David Blitzer, managing partner of the New Jersey Devils and Philadelphia 76ers, who invested through his firm Bolt Ventures.

"Cosm is really early in its growth trajectory at the moment, and we saw an opportunity to partner with the company at, what I would say is at, a very important stage in its development," Jay Levine, chief strategy and business operations officer at Sony, told CNBC. "They have about five locations with the goal of a pretty significant rollout. And the real reason why we did it was it gave us a lot of exposure to a growing distribution and what we deem an experiential platform that we think has long-term potential."

Sony has made several plays in the theatrical space in recent years. It bought Alamo Drafthouse in 2024 and entered a long-term lease to reopen and restore the iconic Cinerama Dome in Los Angeles in July of this year.

"Our experiences strategy is around tech, it's around fandom, and it's around new and innovative approaches to content distribution," Levine said. "For us, we're all about these differentiated experiences."

Cosm's 87-foot-diameter, concave screens drop viewers into the middle of the action, whether it's an NFL game or inside the world of "The Matrix." The company's forthcoming Cleveland location will have an even bigger dome, at 100 feet in diameter.

The experience is akin to that of the Sphere in Las Vegas, which has garnered headlines since opening in 2023 for hosting high-profile concerts and events.

"People absolutely love to live out what they love in fandom, just like wearing a sports jersey," Alexis Scalice, head of Cosm Studios, told CNBC. "And I love that I'm seeing that same behavior happen with cinema."

Stadium-style seating is nestled within each Cosm dome, with booths and tables set on each level. The dome's width is such that it fills much of the viewer's periphery, giving the sensation that they are part of the on-screen environment.

The Los Angeles and Dallas locations have capacity for around 350 seats each, while the newer domes in Atlanta and Detroit have up to 500 available seats. Cosm has not shared how many seats the Cleveland location will have, but with its larger screen it is expected to have a larger number of seats.

For live sports events, Cosm has specialty cameras mounted in arenas that broadcast back to the dome, creating the illusion of being on the field of play.

For theatrical features, the film is played in a large rectangle in the center of the dome and the background is transformed to extend the scene.

In Cosm's presentation of "Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone," for example, during the iconic scene where thousands of Hogwarts acceptance letters flood through the fireplace and into a living room, the background is swarmed with animated envelopes.

Cosm has partnered with experiential production company Little Cinema and creative studio MakeMake to create the visuals and environments for each scene of a film released in the dome. From storyboarding to post-production, this process can take up to a year as Cosm, the participating studio and the visual effects artists craft and render each moment.

"We really work hard not to take away from the story, so the film remains the hero in the narrative," Scalice said. "Working very closely with Warner Bros. and our creative partners to make sure that we're not detracting too much from the story, but we're enhancing those moments for more elevated emotional response."

With the exception of "It," which was released in 2017, Cosm's theatrical offerings thus far have been films that were released decades ago and that typically have long-established fan bases.

That helps Cosm leverage what Scalice called "that nostalgia factor" — "parents coming in that ... grew up on this, and they want to share it with their children."

"We can reinvigorate these legendary films," she added.

Cosm has dabbled across genres — horror, fantasy, science fiction — and also demographics — family-friendly and R-rated. "Shrek," set to debut Dec. 16, will be its first foray into animation.

"The thing that's so incredible about it is everybody thought initially it's just going to be working for sports, and when you look at what they've done, a lot of the titles with 'Harry Potter,' with 'Shrek,' and some of the other ones, I mean, the fill rates are actually incredible," Sony's Levine said.

Studios and theatrical exhibitors alike are looking for innovative ways to showcase Hollywood's product to moviegoers, especially as major shifts in viewing habits have disrupted the business.

In the post-pandemic era, the cinema space has been defined by fewer screens and fewer moviegoers, but those declines have been masked by higher movie ticket prices, particularly for PLF experiences.

These elevated offerings — think Imax, Dolby Cinema, ScreenX and 4DX — have bigger screens and better sound or introduce a unique experience, such as moving seats and fog machines. They're particularly popular with Gen Z and millennials, who've yet to be deterred by rising average ticket prices.

While an average standard movie ticket costs around $13, premium tickets average more than $18 each, according to data from EntTelligence.

Prices for Cosm's events fluctuate based on showtime and venue. Sony's Levine said the company's ticket prices and attendance rates are "higher than I think anybody really would have thought."

Unlike Cosm's sports offerings, which can also be viewed from outside the dome from its hall and deck viewing areas at a lower price, theatrical presentations are only available in the dome.

"They have no problem selling these tickets because people want to experience these things, like an immersive format," said Mike Polydoros, CEO at cinema marketing firm PaperAirplane Media. "That is something they can't get at home."

#462. Apple launches new app to study how people use AI for health

Big AI labs like OpenAI and Anthropic have made their bets on offering chatbots that can answer health-related queries with specialized features. Apple is debuting a new study to understand how people use AI for health. But it isn’t quite following other AI startups just yet; it wants to study how clinically grounded answers can help people.

The iPhone maker is partnering with healthcare-focused AI company OpenEvidence to launch a new study app. Apple is inviting users in the U.S. over 21, using iPhone 15 or later, and running iOS 26 or later, with Apple Intelligence enabled. The company is aiming to run this study with up to 200,000 participants for more than 12 months.

The new app will allow users to ask health-related questions, as they do with ChatGPT or Claude, with answers grounded in clinical data, from sources including The New England Journal of Medicine, Journal of the American Medical Association, and Nature. Apple said that the app will try to give simplified answers to everyday medical questions. Users can rate those answers and provide feedback about the responses; they’ll also be asked to participate in monthly surveys.

Participants can share data from HealthKit such as resting heart rate, activity, sleep, or lab results to get more personalized and pointed suggestions from the app. The company said the app can also help prepare participants better for a doctor’s visit, and it will guide users to specific providers for answers related to concerning medical issues.

Apple specified that the data will be anonymized before sending it to a secure third-party cloud provider, and it won’t be used to train any kind of AI models. The company said that OpenEvidence deletes the query and response data after 24 hours.

“At Apple, we believe responsible innovation starts with scientific rigor and clinical grounding. Health is deeply personal, and conducting a dedicated research study is the most thoughtful way to understand how people engage with, comprehend, and evaluate health AI in everyday life,” Sumbul Desai, M.D., Apple’s vice president of Health and Fitness, said in a statement, adding, “Insights from this study will help advance the broader scientific conversation and ensure that as technology evolves, we continue building trusted tools that genuinely support people wherever they are on their health journey.”

The app is available to download starting today for U.S. users. It’s not clear if Apple wants to release AI-powered answering to its Health app or Siri yet. The potential is huge — earlier this year, OpenAI said users were asking over 300 million health-related queries each week.

#417. Poppi founders are going after PepsiCo's Gatorade with new beverage startup Jetty

After disrupting the soda category with Poppi, founders Allison and Stephen Ellsworth are now taking aim at hydration with their new brand, Jetty.

Starting Monday, Jetty drinks and ready-to-mix electrolyte packets will be on shelves at Whole Foods grocery stores and available online at Target and Amazon.

The new, self-funded venture directly pits the Ellsworths against PepsiCo, which bought Poppi for $1.95 billion in 2025. Pepsi owns Gatorade, which dominates the U.S. sports drink category with about 60% market share, according to Numerator data.

Poppi shook up the soda category with prebiotic fiber, low calorie and sugar counts and its fun marketing aimed at Generation Z and millennials. Jetty looks to upend the hydration category through a similar strategy. To do so, it will face off not only against Gatorade but also a swarm of other upstarts looking to get in on the electrolyte-fueled trend.

For decades, Gatorade and other sports drinks have pitched their products to athletes who need electrolytes and fluids after an intense workout, although many consumers drink them every day without even sweating. Jetty is aimed at people who need a hydration boost, but not the same level of electrolytes or sugar as a traditional sports drink.

"No one has really created a product for just everyday hydration — literally everyday hydration for normal people," Allison Ellsworth, Jetty's chief brand officer, told CNBC. "Like I woke up this morning, I didn't run a marathon."

Following Poppi's sale to PepsiCo, Ellsworth stayed on as an advisor to the brand, although she said she is no longer involved in the day-to-day operations.

After 10 years building Poppi, Ellsworth said, she finally had free time. As Ellsworth tells it, she went to the doctor for the first time in a decade. She started using a GLP-1 medication and lost 60 pounds.

Ellsworth's husband, Stephen, now CEO of the new brand, noticed she wasn't hydrating, even when she came back from the gym.

GLP-1 medications such as Ozempic and Mounjaro can make people less thirsty. But because staying hydrated can also help mitigate some of the drugs' side effects, such as nausea and headaches, it can become more important.

"I physically struggle getting liquid into my body," Ellsworth said.

Plain old water is often less appealing to consumers, even dehydrated GLP-1 users, but more flavorful options with added benefits can be tempting. About 44% of GLP-1 users report consuming more functional beverages, according to EY-Parthenon's third annual survey of more than 3,600 current or former GLP-1 users.

Ellsworth sees a broader appeal for Jetty, too.

"Our core audience is going to be millennial moms and Gen Z," she said. "Gen Z just isn't relating to these old school brands, the legacy of the Gatorades and the Powerades. They're just not cool."

Consumers broadly have become more interested in staying hydrated.

Dollar sales of hydration and electrolyte products climbed 20% to $2.6 billion over the 52 weeks ended Oct. 4, based on data from SPINS, which tracks new universal product codes, or UPCs. The category also has a strong repeat buyer rate of 62%.

However, the Ellsworths aren't the only ones to notice the potential in hydration. Big food and beverage companies have been looking to hydration, too.

Kraft Heinz debuted Capri Sun juice pouches and Kool-Aid packets with electrolytes this spring. In April, Pepsi unveiled an overhaul of the Gatorade brand, focused on making it more appealing to a wider swath of consumers, with more potential occasions like a "fresh morning start" or an "afternoon walk." And Electrolit is a top performer for its distributor Keurig Dr Pepper, with 40% retail sales growth in the company's third quarter.

All of that means more competition for shelf space and consumers' "share of throat," an industry term for beverage market share.

To increase Jetty's visibility across the grocery store and to maximize convenience for shoppers, Jetty will be available in both powders and bottles.

Single-use electrolyte mixes are growing at a much faster clip than the ready-to-drink category, Ellsworth said, thanks to Unilever's Liquid I.V. and smaller upstarts like LMNT. On Amazon alone, powders are a $1 billion category, according to Ellsworth.

The ready-to-drink, or RTD, sports beverages are a much larger market, but the category's sales are shrinking as consumers reach for other hydration options instead. Unit sales of sports drinks have fallen 9.1% from 2022 to 2026, according to Numerator data. And Coca-Cola has written down the value of BodyArmor by more than $1.7 billion after buying control of the brand for $5.6 billion in late 2021.

"Me and my husband know how to do RTD," Ellsworth said.

Jetty will launch with five flavors, including watermelon, orange mango, fruit punch, black raspberry and white cherry. All of them contain 100 milligrams of sodium, 310 milligrams of potassium and 100 milligrams of magnesium in a 15.2-ounce bottle or 15-gram packet.

In addition to electrolytes, Jetty products also contain B vitamins, 3 grams of fiber and 6 grams of sugar. It doesn't use artificial sweeteners or dyes, its founders say.

For comparison, a 20-ounce bottle of traditional Gatorade Thirst Quencher has about 270 milligrams of sodium and 80 milligrams of potassium, plus 34 grams of sugar. The sports drink brand is also phasing out artificial colors and promoting lower-sugar versions.

"What's really interesting about this product and this opportunity is we now can capture all those lapsed drinkers — or that generation that didn't even ever drink it in the first place," Ellsworth said.

Poppi's new owner knows about Jetty and the market it is trying to capture, too.

"I don't think [Pepsi] gets it, but what they do get is that the movement is changing, and they think that just by taking dyes out or doing 90% less sugar, they're going to capture this audience," Ellsworth said. "But that is very big corporate thinking."

She added that she believes Pepsi has missed the mark on advertising to Gen Z consumers across its portfolio, such as in its campaigns for Sprite competitor Starry.

Perhaps as an extension of that, Poppi's performance has been underwhelming under PepsiCo's ownership.

The food and beverage giant now estimates the fair value of potential performance-based payments to Poppi's prior owners is $12 million, Pepsi said in a regulatory filing in October. At the time of the deal, Pepsi promised an additional payment of $300 million if Poppi hit certain targets by the third quarter of 2027. The Ellsworths declined to comment on the filing to CNBC.

This time around, with one successful brand under their belts, Jetty's founders say they have learned from past mistakes. Those lessons should help Jetty as it launches at a much larger scale than Poppi did.

Three different claims lawyers have signed off on Jetty's packaging, according to Ellsworth. Poppi paid $8.9 million to settle class-action litigation alleging false advertising over its "gut healthy" claims.

For now, Jetty is entirely self-funded with $2.5 million, although Ellsworth said that the brand will likely raise money in 2027. This time, though, she said investors will probably look less like traditional venture capitalists.

"I want to bring in investors that bring value," Ellsworth said. Instead of a venture capitalist who is willing to invest $15 million, Jetty would rather consider investors who can only write a check for $1 million but have access to celebrities. "So then you're getting access to talent on a cheaper level and people that are invested in the brand."

And Jetty has also learned a few lessons from Gatorade. For example, the brand is already at work on a lemon-lime version — the original flavor of Gatorade.

"We'll eventually do it," Ellsworth said. "But it was really important at the beginning just to stick to flavors that we love, for it to taste good."

#398. Restaurant chains refocus on food quality to bring back diners

Juicier chicken tenders. Revamped sauces. Richer and creamier Frosties.

Across the restaurant industry, chains are turning their focus back to their food in the hopes that better quality will win back diners.

Over the last two years, consumers have dined out less frequently and grown pickier about where they choose to eat, based on data from Black Box Intelligence. In that time, "value" has been the buzzword du jour for restaurant executives, who have explained to analysts and investors that the word encompasses more than menu pricing alone.

Now, industry CEOs are turning to their corporate chefs to try to beat rivals on quality and taste, as they look to expand their definition of value. Discounts and one-off promotions can lift traffic for a time, but the actual product still matters for restaurants' long-term growth, as well as franchisee profitability.

From Wendy's to Chipotle Mexican Grill, chief executives touted new or upcoming improvements to menu items on their quarterly earnings conference calls in recent weeks.

"We think the product's improved and even better," Chipotle CEO Scott Boatwright told analysts on the company's call in late October, referring to the burrito chain's brisket, which returned to menus for a limited time Thursday.

For some chains, better food is a key step in ambitious turnaround plans.

For example, "quality food at real value" is one pillar of Wendy's five-part turnaround strategy that the chain unveiled on Thursday.

"We're also rebuilding our food offerings and have begun testing improvements to quality and craveability of our core items, including thicker, crispier bacon, a richer and creamier Frosty, whole leaf lettuce on sandwiches and improved salads," Wendy's CEO Bob Wright told analysts on the call Thursday.

That same day, the company reported its seventh straight quarter of same-store sales declines and publicly rejected a $1.4 billion takeover bid led by Nelson Peltz's Trian Partners. Wendy's said the proposal did not take into account the potential value that its turnaround plan could unlock.

There is precedent to suggest that better-tasting food can contribute to higher sales.

In 2009, Domino's Pizza changed its recipe, swapping out its sauce, adding garlic to its crust and switching to shredded mozzarella with a hint of provolone. Along with blunt commercials that compared its previous crust to cardboard and old sauce to ketchup, the changes helped to spur U.S. same-store sales growth of 9.9% the following year.

Now, some of the same team that led the Domino's turnaround is copying the playbook at Burger King's U.S. restaurants.

Part of the burger chain's multiyear comeback plan has focused on revamping its core menu items. It started earlier this year with its flagship offering, the Whopper, which received a better bun and a new mayonnaise. And in September, it rolled out an overhaul of its nuggets, switching to 100% white meat, crispier breading and a more flavorful marinade. It also reformulated its sauces.

"We've tried to look at our biggest categories first," Josh Kobza, CEO of Burger King parent Restaurant Brands International, told CNBC. "We've also thought about the ones where the fixes are more straightforward and less operationally involved."

Following the changes, Whopper sales were up by a double-digit percentage in the third quarter, Kobza said on the company's earnings call in late October. And nugget sales climbed more than 35%. Burger King said its U.S. same-store sales jumped 8.4% in the quarter.

Like Domino's, Burger King is highlighting diners' past gripes with its Whoppers and nuggets in its ads, which star Burger King U.S. president and former Domino's executive Tom Curtis.

Better-tasting food isn't just for chains that are in trouble.

Even Chili's, which has seen its same-store sales soar 52% over the past three years, is improving more of its existing menu options. After tackling its baby back ribs, burgers and nachos, it will turn next to salads and steaks, Brinker International CEO Kevin Hochman told CNBC.

"We're going to be doing more dry aging of our beef to make it more tender, versus doing it in a plant," he said. "It's a little bit more expensive, but we think the taste is going to be a whole lot better."

Chili's rival Applebee's launched a juicier chicken tender during its latest quarter, according to John Peyton, CEO of the chain's parent company Dine Brands. In 2027, Applebee's plans to overhaul more of its protein options.

"We think proteins are, number one, our biggest opportunity," Peyton told CNBC. "Number two, they're a big focus of the way people are thinking about eating right now, leaning into proteins and a little bit less with the carbs."

Chicken is also a focus for McDonald's, which is testing hand-breaded wings, strips and a sandwich in a handful of Chicago-area restaurants. It plans to expand the trial to more locations in the U.S. and Ireland in early 2027.

Moreover, McDonald's is trying to improve its coffee, with fresher beans and upgraded equipment, executives said at the company's investor day in September. Both chicken and coffee are fast-growing categories, offering more opportunities to lift sales than McDonald's signature beef burgers.

In addition to menu tests, McDonald's is also leaning into training to improve the quality and consistency of its core menu items. Starting in October, the company launched "Make It Golden," a multiyear training program for corporate employees, restaurant workers and franchisees.

Ultimately, improved menu items mean little if the recipe changes aren't well executed.

The condition affects nearly 1 billion adults globally, including 80 million people ages 20 and over in the U.S., according to the National Council on Aging. Most have never been diagnosed, Lilly said in a release.

Lilly's blockbuster weight loss injection Zepbound became the first medication to win U.S. approval for OSA in December 2024. Patients have limited treatment options outside of that drug and many use cumbersome machines that provide positive airway pressure, or PAP, to allow for normal breathing.

"Foundayo is already making a difference for people living with obesity, and with plans to submit these results to the FDA by the end of the year, we could have the opportunity to help people living with OSA," Dr. Thomas Seck, senior vice president of product development of Lilly Cardiometabolic Health, said in a release.

The trial tested Foundayo in two independent studies, the first of which included 363 patients who were unable or unwilling to use PAP therapy. The second study included 349 people who were on PAP therapy for at least three months at the time of screening and continued that treatment during the trial.

Patients in both studies who took Foundayo started at a low dose and increased it gradually at four-week intervals to different maximum dosages.

Researchers specifically examined how much the pill reduced the so-called apnea-hypopnea index, or AHI, which records the number of times per hour a person's breathing shows a restricted or completely blocked airway. The index is used to evaluate the severity of obstructive sleep apnea and the effectiveness of treatments for the condition.

People who took Foundayo and were not using PAP therapy had an average of 21.1 fewer AHI events per hour at 52 weeks, compared with an average reduction of 4.8 per hour for the placebo, according to the results.

Meanwhile, patients who were on both Foundayo and PAP therapy had an average of 18.3 fewer AHI events per hour at 52 weeks, compared with an average reduction of 5.5 per hour for the placebo.

Foundayo led to an overall AHI reduction of around 27.5% in that group, compared with a decrease of 3% for the placebo. The company said 19.6% of the participants on Foundayo and PAP therapy also met the criteria for remission or mild OSA.

Foundayo also reduced what's known as the sleep apnea-specific hypoxic burden — a measure of how deeply and for how long oxygen levels fall during sleep. That metric fell by 55.9% in people not using PAP therapy and 45.8% in those using it.

#376. Why the UK is dithering over what to do about e-scooters

Why the UK is dithering over what to do about e-scooters

When I step out of Southampton Central railway station, it takes me just seconds to spot an e-scooter. In this city, these devices have become a normal part of the transport ecosystem, along with e-bikes.

"It's just the most convenient way to commute from home to the station and back," says Keith Reed, who I speak to as he arrives at the station to catch a train to London. Keith says uses rental e-scooters twice a week. His alternative option would be his bike, but he'd then have to worry about where to leave it.

Around the city, there are parking areas for bright orange rental devices everywhere - but they're not necessarily a permanent feature. Southampton hosts one of the numerous government-backed trials around England. This one has been going on since 2021.

A study suggests around half of users of e-scooter do so in conjunction with another mode of transport - like Keith, who rides to the railway station

It also doesn't take long to find e-scooters which aren't part of the trial, but instead are owned by individuals. I see a man riding one along the pavement outside a parade of shops. He doesn't want to stop and talk.

These privately owned e-scooters are illegal to ride on public roads across the UK, but they've proliferated regardless. One expert told me the situation was a "Wild West".

Among people I spoke to in Southampton, the same concerns came up time and time again: "They're a blinking nuisance," says elderly pedestrian Fred. Another shopper, Martin, says he's "very concerned" whenever he sees them racing through precincts: "An accident's almost inevitable."

The endgame of the trials is to reach a decision on whether to make permanent hired e-scooter schemes such as the one Keith uses - and whether to bring in legislation that would legalise and regulate privately owned e-scooters too.

But six years since the trials started around England, there's still no conclusion - and as the UK government has responsibility for road traffic legislation across Great Britain, officials in Scotland and Wales say they are waiting on Westminster to act.

So why is it taking so long for the government to decide what to do, and what are the consequences of delay? What does the evidence tell us about how safe these things are?

And why are people using them in the first place - are they here to stay as a useful part of the transport offer, or will they end up being viewed as a pest on our streets?

You'd recognise an e-scooter if you saw one. The Department for Transport's (DfT's) definition of an e-scooter includes no pedals, two wheels and a maximum continuous power rating of 500W.

And so far, the trials around England appear to have proved popular. According to DfT research, fom January 2022 to May 2024, 36.9 million trips were made across 30 trial areas outside of London. On average, nearly 19,500 e-scooters were available to hire every day.

Dr Yasir Ali, a reader in Transport Modelling at Loughborough University, says e-scooters are "complementary" to other modes of transport, rather than replacing them. Due to factors such as range and being exposed to the elements, they are less suitable for longer trips, rural areas with less infrastructure or in bad weather. They work best as "a first- and last-mile solution" in urban areas, to reduce reliance on cars for short trips.

Shared mobility charity CoMoUK's 2025 report suggests that for more than half of the people using rental e-scooters, the most common use was travelling to work or school. Its analysis of DfT data suggests the majority of people using hired e-scooters are male and under 35.

"Sometimes it's a bit difficult to take the bus from dot to dot", says Yan Zhang, who works at Southampton university and doesn't drive, "so for shorter distances, I'll take the scooter."

Like Yan Zhang, e-scooter users are less likely to have a car, CoMo's report says - this is partly explained by official trials' concentration in urban areas

Around half of respondents to CoMoUK's online survey used it in conjunction with another mode of transport - such as riding to a railway station, like Keith.

Earlier evidence from a national evaluation of the trials published in 2022 found that although some users would have driven instead had they not had access to an e-scooter, the biggest "modal shift" - meaning switch in ways of getting around - to e-scooters was from walking. Given walking is an active mode of travel, this has raised questions over how significant the public health impact of e-scooter use really is.

And the usage of privately owned e-scooters isn't reflected in this research. The DfT has said between 760,000 and 1.2 million people in England aged 16 and over own one - despite the fact that it remains illegal to ride them in public.

In the eyes of the law, e-scooters are classed as motor vehicles. Outside of the trials, they can't be insured, and thus can only legally be ridden on private land, with the owner's permission.

Parking bays for e-scooters and e-bikes are available across Southampton

The trials themselves are bound by strict rules. The devices' top speed is limited. Businesses who operate the pilots must insure them. Users must have a full or provisional driving licence, and they must not ride on the pavement. Helmets are recommended but not mandatory.

Richard Dilks, CEO of CoMoUK says "mass-scale use of something that is inherently low-carbon, low-impact, low-cost" is a "good thing" but "we also don't have rules of engagement about how they should be ridden".

And as a result of the delays in introducing regulation, Loughborogh University's Yasir Ali says "we are increasingly in a situation where the law and reality have diverged", with "the disadvantages of e-scooter use without securing the full benefits of regulation".

Back in Southampton, I meet a lady pushing a black e-scooter, who doesn't want to give her name. She says nobody told her when she bought it that it was illegal to ride on the streets.

The Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency has a unit that carries out surveillance of retailers to check they're complying with the law. But earlier this month, an investigation by the Press Association news agency discovered some retailers were advertising them for commuting purposes.

Police tell me they regularly see children illegally riding private e-scooters. Margaret Winchcomb, deputy executive director at the Parliamentary Advisory Council for Transport Safety (PACTS), says some are being specifically marketed at kids.

So what can police do? Forces regularly launch crackdowns where they seize illegally used devices, and often crush them. Each year, awareness campaigns run urging parents not to buy their kids an e-scooter for Christmas. But enforcement is a challenge.

Insp Matt Boiles from Avon and Somerset's road policing team says problems arise even with the legal trial devices - for example, people riding them after drinking alcohol, which counts as drink-driving. Riding with another person on the back is another common issue.

But officers see private e-scooters being illegally ridden in public places "all the time, every single day" and they can be really hard to catch - it isn't always safe for police in a car to pursue someone on an e-scooter, he says.

Insp Boiles despairs over parents buying them for children: "We do have parents attend a police station quite a lot, getting very, very upset that their child's Â£500 e-scooter has been seized." But "the legislation is really, really clear that you can't have it back".

There's a potential risk of fire if unregulated devices with lithium batteries are left to charge unattended. Most train operators have banned e-scooters on board for this reason.

But there's also the fact that riders are unprotected in the case of falling off or being hit by motorised vehicles. And - along with e-bikes - they can end up involved in collisions where pedestrians are hurt. Many trial e-scooters in the UK are restricted to 12.5mph, but privately owned scooters' speeds aren't.

Some coroners have even raised concerns. In 2022, a coroner in London called for action after the death of 14-year-old Fatima Abukah on a privately owned scooter.

According to the DfT, in 2024 - the most recent year for which figures are available - 1,390 casualties were recorded in collisions that involved e-scooter in England, Scotland and Wales, three more than in the previous 12 months. The vast majority, 1,096, were e-scooter users themselves.

Young boys and men appear to be over-represented in the stats. Of the casualties, 302 - the most of any age and sex category - were males aged 10-19.

Six deaths were recorded in 2024, unchanged from the previous year. Five of these were riders and one a pedestrian.

"If Jacob was wearing a helmet that day, he would still be here," Carly, from Wythenshawe in Greater Manchester, says.

Carly is not against e-scooters. They "are really good for people to get to work, and they are eco-friendly", she tells me, but "they just need to be used in the correct way."

#372. Aviation faces hotter, stormier skies – and passengers might have to accept more disruption

Aviation faces hotter, stormier skies â and passengers might have to accept more disruption

As the country sweltered through one of the many heatwaves of a baking summer in late June, towering thunderclouds were gathering over the south of England and across much of northern Europe. It was a Saturday, and with the holiday season getting under way, thousands of passengers were heading to London's Heathrow and Gatwick airports to catch flights abroad. It was always going to be a very busy day.

What followed, however, was chaos for travellers. As air traffic controllers struggled to route aircraft around the storms, airspace became heavily congested and many planes due to fly were simply forced to wait on the ground. In the end, some 900 flights to and from both airports were delayed, some by up to 11 hours. Dozens more were cancelled. At airports across Europe, passengers expecting to fly into London also faced long waits and uncertainty.

The weather in the UK may now have cooled, and passengers may soon forget their experiences. But some within the industry believe what happened in June should be seen as a warning.

The timing in this case was clearly unlucky. But there are concerns that such incidents are only going to become more common as the effects of climate change mount up around the world and weather systems are likely to become increasingly unstable, making disruption more likely and adding to costs for airlines, airports and travellers.

Tim Atkinson, an airline risk consultant and former pilot, says the very crowded skies over the UK and northern Europe mean the region is particularly exposed.

He thinks there is a real risk, for example, that all of London's airports could be affected by storms at the same time, potentially with major consequences in the air and on the ground.

"What we're seeing now is very rapid climate change," he says.

"We're going from the kind of weather activity which takes out an airport, and which the system kind of copes withâ¦ to a situation where, with very little predictability, it would be possible to lose all of the London airports from real-time operation quite swiftly and with little notice".

Such a scenario, he thinks, would make the disruption for passengers caused by the incident in June seem relatively minor â and could even put lives at risk. But how likely is it?

For controllers at National Air Traffic Services's (NATS) main control centre in Swanwick, Hampshire, thunderstorms are a major preoccupation. When I visit, it is a hot and humid day, there are ominous dark clouds in the sky, and heavy, thundery downpours are hitting the surrounding region.

In Terminal Control, the part of the building responsible for flights heading to or from airports in London and southern England, those same storm cells are being monitored continuously. Their progress is tracked on large radar screens, while at the same time weather forecasts are carefully scrutinised.

Air traffic tends to come in waves during the day. Right now, at lunchtime, it is relatively quiet and the storms have yet to affect major routes. But with activity expected to intensify in the afternoon, staff are already preparing for what could be a tricky few hours.

Stormy weather can shrink the available airspace, meaning traffic has to be restricted and rerouted. If planes are unable to land at one airport, for example due to high winds, they may need diverting to another. But that can create its own problems; if too many unscheduled aircraft land at an already busy airport, it might not have sufficient infrastructure to accommodate them all.

Heavy rain and storms can make flying harder

Planes need fuel and somewhere to park. Passengers need food and facilities. Ultimately, if there isn't space, it can simply refuse to accept all but emergency traffic.

This is where the scenario set out by Atkinson comes in. He warns of a situation where traffic is diverted from Heathrow, Gatwick, Luton and Stansted at the same time, leaving aircraft coming in across the Atlantic and a large number of European flights all looking for somewhere to land at the same time, and some with dwindling fuel reserves.

This would at the very least create serious disruption for passengers, who might end up hundreds of miles from their destinations. But it could also, he thinks, create "a very serious risk event, such as an aircraft running out of fuel in the sky".

Aircraft are legally required to carry enough fuel to take them to an alternative airport if necessary, and to allow them to fly for an extra 30 minutes on top of that.

Not everyone shares Atkinson's concern. Former airline executive Andrew Charlton, who manages the Swiss-based consultancy Aviation Advocacy, thinks the risk is low.

"You've always got to imagine the worst-case scenario", he says. "But I also think we need to keep that worst case in context.

"Yes, it's possible to imagine it. But I'm also fairly comfortable with the ability of this industry to react well and make good decisionsâ¦ of all the things that wake me up at 3amâ¦this is not the top of that list."

Thunderstorms are caused by what is known as convective activity, where moist air close to the ground warms rapidly and rises quickly. As it rises, it expands and cools, allowing the moisture it carries to condense, forming large clouds. More heat means more convective energy, potentially creating instability. In addition, for every degree the Earth warms, the atmosphere can hold about 7% more moisture, increasing the probability of heavy rainfall.

It is not clear whether this means we will actually get more thunderstorms in the UK and across Europe, as so many variables are involved beyond the simple matter of energy and moisture. Some parts of the world may even get fewer. However, studies have shown that higher latitudes, particularly in the northern hemisphere, will see the biggest increases in favourable conditions for storms to form, and those that do may be more violent. According to the UK's Met Office, "while we may not see more thunderstorms overall, the ones we do experience could be more impactful".

Where possible, pilots will avoid flying through convective weather. Even though aircraft are designed to cope with severe conditions, storms can produce intense turbulence, capable of damaging the structural frame of the plane, as well as throwing people around the cabin if they are not strapped in. Hail can also be a big problem, potentially cracking windscreens or causing engine problems. So, however much inconvenience it may cause passengers, crews will invariably put safety first.

New technology helps pilots navigate tricky flying conditions

Onboard weather radars can help aircrew to see up to 320 nautical miles ahead. They also have access to live weather forecasting resources online. But thunderstorms can move quickly and, according to Prof Guy Gratton, an expert in aeronautics at Cranfield University, these systems are essentially "tactical rather than strategic". In other words, although they enable pilots to see storm cells on their screens from a relatively short distance away, and request a diversion, they do not allow for detailed long-distance planning.

The result is that unplanned diversions often happen late in flight, which is likely to mean burning more fuel. If storm delays do become more common, says Andrew Charlton, then aircraft will have to load more fuel as a matter of course, in case there is trouble brewing ahead. "Aircraft are going to have to fly further, and they're going to have to factor in the assumption they're going to fly further," he says. For an airline, fuel is one of its major costs â and if the fuel bill goes up, he says, it is inevitable passengers will pay more.

More intense storms would be likely to make air traffic management in busy regions harder and potentially lead to more delays and cancellations. However, it is possible some of this could be mitigated using new technologies.

Both Heathrow and Gatwick, for example, have moved away from mandating minimum distances between incoming aircraft in favour of a system which continually calculates spacing times based on wind speeds and aircraft types. In practice, this is meant to allow controllers to reduce the distance between landing aircraft, particularly in high winds, and get them on the ground more quickly. According to NATS, it has successfully improved landing rates and reduced delays by a significant margin.

However, there could be other effects from more severe storms as well. Heavier rainfall, for instance, increases the likelihood of flooding. In a worst case, this can paralyse airport operations, as happened in April 2024 when torrential downpours hit Dubai International Airport. It briefly had to close its runways, while disruption lasted for two days. More than 1,200 flights were cancelled, affecting tens of thousands of passengers.

Passengers were delayed during the floods in Dubai

But even if runways and taxiways themselves are clear, and aircraft are able to fly without any difficulty, passengers may struggle to catch their flights if roads, railways or car parks are flooded.

Protecting against such risks can be expensive. Gatwick Airport, which experienced severe flooding over the Christmas period in 2013, has spent tens of millions of pounds on mitigation strategies in recent years â although those figures are dwarfed by the more than Â£2bn it is expecting to invest in its current expansion plans.

The problem of clear-air turbulence is also a real one. It cannot be seen, and is becoming increasingly common as the climate warms.

This is when air moves violently at high altitudes with no clouds or other visual cues to help a pilot avoid it. For passengers, it usually only means a little bit of bumpiness, but occasionally it can be a lot more serious. On 1 March 2023, for example, a Lufthansa flight from Austin, Texas to Frankfurt encountered what the airline described as "brief but severe" turbulence.

Those aboard described how passengers and flight crew were thrown around the cabin as the plane suddenly lost altitude. After it made an emergency landing in Washington DC, seven people were taken to hospital.

While still rare, this kind of turbulence is already much more prevalent than it used to be and is likely to become even more so.

There is another climate-related threat to aviation, which may prove challenging to overcome: rising sea levels. #344. My hometown shows that high streets have to change or die

My hometown shows that high streets have to change or die

The empty building that used to be the home of Marks & Spencer in Aberdeen is one of the first things you see when you arrive off the train and come into the city centre. The sign has gone, though the shape of the lettering can still be made out; the windows are blacked out and the doors boarded up.

This is my hometown. As a teenager, I spent hours on Union Street, its main thoroughfare, nicknamed the Granite Mile after the local silver-grey stone. It was the place to try on all the latest fashions from the likes of Clockhouse and Chelsea Girl.

The street used to be packed with shoppers from across the north-east of Scotland.

"I come to Union Street to take a bus somewhere else... there's nothing to shop for nowâ¦ it's horrendous," complains Marie Sim, a lifelong Aberdeen resident.

It's a similar story across the country: High Streets, once bursting with shops, hit first by the rise of out-of-town retail centres and then by online commerce.

But Aberdeen is in the thick of an ambitious turnaround plan. The port city has essentially declared war on its empty shop problem, taking an unusually hands-on approach to filling vacant units and bringing long-neglected upper floors of buildings into use.

The Marks & Spencer flagship store in Aberdeen closed in August, 80 years after it opened there

On top of that, the council is pouring tens of millions into trying to make the city centre a destination again. Like many other places, it's leaning into the "experience economy", with a new indoor food and drink market and a major redevelopment of the seafront.

High Streets are integral to how we feel about where we live. The sight of vacant shops and graffiti can quickly become a vicious circle, deterring shoppers, discouraging investment and leading to anti-social behaviour.

Politicians of all stripes have been trying for years to breathe new life into town centres - and Andy Burnham, who has made a focus on "place" part of his political identity, is the latest. He argues they should become "symbols of Britain's renaissance".

But can it be achieved? And if they're to survive, do town centres need a fundamental rethink?

One in eight properties on British High Streets are currently vacant, according to Green Street, the property and retail analytics company, a number that's stayed stubbornly high for years.

"If there was a silver bullet, it would have been fired years ago," says Jackie Sadek, a former government adviser on urban regeneration, who has spent nearly four decades working in town centre renewal.

One of the biggest obstacles is fractured ownership. Shops and buildings are often owned by dozens of different landlords, sometimes through offshore companies or complex structures, each with their own interests and priorities. That can slow decision-making and make large-scale regeneration difficult.

But even with empty units that are available, you need to find someone willing to take them on.

One in eight properties on British High Streets are currently vacant

The cost of running a High Street business has also risen; employment costs are higher, and there's the burden of business rates.

Firms need customers. Footfall on many High Streets has been in long-term decline as shopping and services move online, and this enormous structural shift has yet to play out fully.

Two-thirds of town centre experts think there is still 20% to 40% too much retail space, according to a survey by retail body Revo and real estate consultancy Lambert Smith Hampton.

In Aberdeen's boom years of North Sea oil, from the late 1970s through to the early 2010s, it didn't have to work hard to fill its hotels and restaurants and get the cash tills ringing because of the wealth the energy industry brought to the city.

I remember weekends with friends as a teenager, spending whatever cash we had in the fashion chains and shoe shops. Later came the pubs and bar-hopping, and trips to the art deco Capitol Theatre (now office space), where I saw Duran Duran.

But by the end of 2022, Union Street had hit rock bottom. Aberdonians had long deserted the High Street for a shopping centre, which opened in 2009 next to the railway station.

"They'd fallen out of love with their city," says Bob Keiller, head of Our Union Street coalition, a community-led group formed after an emergency summit to reverse the decline.

The first thing it did was listen to what residents wanted, receiving thousands of submissions. Restoring a bit of civic pride and filling empty units became the focus.

It's been a co-ordinated effort, involving letting agents, the council, landlords, businesses and a big team of volunteers, led by Keiller, a former FTSE 100 CEO, who isn't getting paid. He has a map on his office wall, detailing the status of every property, unit by unit, with colour-coded Post-it notes.

They audited the street, tracking down absent landlords, and logging what was empty and why. Volunteers offered to give the empty properties a clean and a lick of paint, to make them more attractive to let. They also spruced up the streets, picking litter, weeding, and painting bins. A small team still goes out once a fortnight.

Bob Keiller heads a community-led group set up to help revive Aberdeen's Union Street

There's a website where all the available properties are collated; crucially, all of the financial incentives are also listed. These can include council fit-out grants (where a new business gets money to decorate premises), and a two-year discount from business rates.

In 2023, 57 of the 198 units on Union Street were empty. As of today, 33 have been filled, with a further 14 in the process of being let.

But it can feel like a game of whack-a-mole because since the initiative started, another 20 units have become vacant. "The key thing is, we're filling quicker than emptying," Keiller insists.

Keiller's operations director, David McKeith, describes it as putting an arm round the shoulder of those they can help and being a "wasp in the ear" of people who just say no.

At one end of the office, a small, young team produces social media content, linking visitors and residents to a new Our Aberdeen app, showing what the city can offer.

For Keiller, the "small wins" - like tackling graffiti - are needed to help change public perception.

"We've realised the psychology is not to do it once but be prepared to do it two or three times before people that do these things eventually say, 'there's no point in putting it there because somebody's going to paint over it.'"

Attention is also turning to the often-forgotten upper floors of buildings along the once-iconic Union Street. In a separate initiative run by the business group Aberdeen Inspired, experts are getting inside those vacant high-up spaces to identify the barriers to bringing them back into use.

Aberdeen sought the help of a High Street troubleshooter, Iain Nicholson, who's worked with dozens of towns and cities through his Vacant Shops Academy.

Out of more than 1,000 empty units he helped audit nationally, on average more than half didn't have a letting agent actively marketing the property. He says some councils don't even know who the landlords are.

Empty shops, he says, won't all be filled by the market alone. Towns and cities need to encourage a broader range of occupiers, from arts and culture to leisure, health and well-being.

He says what's striking about Aberdeen is its joined-up approach, and the fact people "rolled up their sleeves" and overcame differences. "That's not something you can yet say about everywhere."

Every town and city faces its own mix of challenges.

Nicholson also helped Crewe in Cheshire. As a smaller place than Aberdeen, it is focusing on arts, culture, and community, with a new women's centre opened this month.

And English councils are being encouraged to take a more interventionist role.

They can now force an empty commercial unit that's been vacant for at least a year to be offered to tenants through a High Street Rental Auction, even if the landlord doesn't want to let it.

A noodle bar (left) is one of the new businesses in the area. Parts of the city centre have been undergoing construction work (right)

It's early days but the mere prospect of losing control of a property is delivering some results. Bassetlaw Council in Nottinghamshire, an early adopter of the scheme, says more than 30 shops across the district have been let or are currently under offer as a result of the policy.

But filling empty units is only part of the challenge. The bigger question is what town and city centres are actually for.

Changing habits mean we won't need so many shops. Instead, it's argued, town centres will need more people living and working in them, with new homes and office space alongside services that can't be done online. With an ageing population, for example, bringing NHS health centres into the heart of a town could be a big opportunity.

In Aberdeen, the building that used to house BHS - a department store shuttered in 2016 - is being converted into an entrance plaza for the new food and drink market.

The physical appearance of Union Street is changing too. Like many Victorian shopping thoroughfares, it was built with a wide road (probably to give space to horses and carriages). Now parts of the pavements are being widened to make it a more pleasant place to spend time, though many Aberdonians are weary of the disruption, and of new traffic restrictions.

Money is a big barrier. Aberdeen's new indoor food and drink market is costing Â£40m, funded by the council and the UK Levelling Up Fund. In other places, regeneration schemes can struggle because the cost of redevelopment outweighs the likely financial return, making support from the public sector essential. And the economics are becoming more challenging.

"Higher interest rates, rising costs of construction and other issues are impacting the viability and delivery of new housing and big redevelopment projects," says Steve Norris, a director at Lambert Smith Hampton. He says that big projects usually require cash from both the public and private sectors.

He wants to see "more creative ways of funding".

Part of Burnham's plan is to give local leaders more freedom to invest in town centres and drive regeneration, building on the approach he has championed in Greater Manchester. But bigger projects take years.

Volunteers have played a role in the redevelopment of Aberdeen's Union Street

Making it easier and cheaper to open and run a High Street business is one of the more immediate solutions being called for, with business rates a particular concern.

Burnham has promised further rates reform. All eyes are on this month's Budget to see how far he will go. All eyes are on this month's Budget to see how far he will go in England. Business rates are devolved in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The chief executive of Next, Simon Wolfson, argues that the bigger challenge is not business rates, but planning reform.

"The most important thing that the government can do for high streets is let them develop," says Wolfson, who's also a Conservative peer.

Back in Aberdeen, there are some signs of traction.

Lauren Reid and her mother Rose moved their fashion boutique to Union Street in 2024. A council grant helped with the refurbishment after they decided to make the leap.

Can they make the sums add up? "We're getting there," she says.

She feels the same about the future of her street. "I always say Rome wasn't built in a day."

Lauren Reid and her mother Rose moved their fashion boutique to Union Street with the help of a council grant

Right now the area feels like a construction site - but another gaping hole will soon be filled. Robert Keane is a local property developer who in 2023 bought the Trinity shopping centre, once home to Debenhams. The space is now set to be re-occupied by a national leisure brand. A notoriously tiring stairway leading to the Trinity from the train station is also being replaced by an escalator.

"We saw a real good opportunity, because it was dying," he says.

He reckons he can make a go of it, cutting the cost of parking and overheads, resetting rents and being more nimble than previous far-flung owners.

It was a bold move given Lambert Smith Hampton suggests that more than one in 10 UK shopping centres is ripe for demolition.

But he's betting the dynamics of Aberdeen are finally changing.

"I think by 2027 it should look and feel like a different city."

That is certainly the hope. Yet walking along Union Street today, past the boarded-up shopfronts and roadworks, it can still feel like a city caught between its past and whatever comes next.

#317. Why are more roofs not made of solar tiles?

From installer to instructor Kate Collardson (left) has a long history in solar systems

Some people in the US "still hate the look of solar", Kate Collardson acknowledges.

She would know. Collardson handles operations for the Solar Service of Colorado, a solar maintenance and inspection company. She's also held just about every other job in the industry, from installer to instructor.

For those that hate the look of solar panels, an alternative is a solar roof, where the tiles (or shingles) house solar cells, but look much the same as regular tiles.

This is especially important for historic buildings, where heritage rules limit changes to their appearance.

For many people, the first time they'd heard of this product category was in August, when Tesla's website stopped offering solar roofs, external. This followed disappointing sales and a lawsuit over sudden price hikes, external.

Other companies have also pulled out, but Tesla's decision in particular sent shockwaves through the solar roof industry.

Some see it as a death knell for solar tiles; others, as an opportunity.

Tesla abruptly stopped marketing its solar tiles in August

For instance, the small Swiss company Freesuns is considering expanding to the US.

Deborah Learoyd, Freesuns' CEO and managing director, estimates that the company's sales have grown 20â30% since it was established in 2019.

In general, solar roof components continue to be manufactured in smaller batches. This limits the economies of scale and standardisation, though flexible manufacturing could help somewhat.

Learoyd says that while the unit costs for Freesuns' products are slowly decreasing, "a lot of the cost is actually the fact that every roof is a little bit different. And so it has to be engineered a little bit differently, installed a little bit differently".

Solar roofs typically require collaboration among roofers, solar installers and electricians. And building regulations tend to be more complex than for solar panels.

The additional time and paperwork can take a toll on a roofing business, Collardson explains. "You try to put a roofer into the equation and they're going, 'This is taking forever. I'm losing money. I could have sold 10 roofing jobs in the time that it takes me to sell this one'."

Collardson has to charge more for repair of solar shingles, compared to a standard photovoltaic (PV) setup.

She explains, "With traditional solar, we generally have electronics on the panel and a map that shows us exactly how much each panel is producing." So when something goes wrong, it's easy to spot where.

"With solar shingles, we don't really have the benefit of that mapping; there's no room underneath the shingle for the electronics." Thermal cameras can identify the trouble spots, however.

But then there's the matter of accessing them. "You can remove the one in the middle of the array, but you have to walk on the others, and you really shouldn't walk on them because you're going to create micro-fractures and that's going to cause issues down the road."

Before repair costs are factored in, a solar roof might cost twice as much as a system of solar panels mounted on a roof.

Freesuns solar tiles on the LycÃ©e Jean-Piaget in NeuchÃ¢tel, Switzerland

Solar roofs are also less efficient - one reason is that the tiles are just smaller.

Another factor is the energy lost as heat. Since the tiles sit close to the roof, there's less airflow than with standard solar panels mounted on a roof. Heat impairs solar performance.

Coloured tiles are even less efficient, comments Ben Graves, co-founder of UK solar company Sunsave. "The reason why all solar cells are black, and increasingly the whole panel is now black, is because black is the best absorbing colour."

Overall, much more space would need to be devoted to solar roof tiles to produce the same amount of energy.

Sunsave does not offer solar roofs. "Most people in the UK just don't have enough money to pay for it," Graves believes.

While appearance was a bigger factor five years ago, now "the vast majority of consumers are most interested in how much they can save".

But supporters of solar roofs argue that they should not be pitted against standard solar modules on roofs.

"Let's not compare PV roof tiles to a standard PV system, but look at PV roof tiles as generating additional electricity from our roofs instead of just having the roof sit there," urges Martin Heinrich who leads a research group at the Fraunhofer Institute for Solar Energy Systems in Germany.

A solar roof can cost twice as much as installing solar panels

While standard solar panels have gone from strength to strength, solar roofs have not advanced to the same extent.

One advancement that could especially help solar roofs is matrix shingling technology.

This is a different way of connecting solar cells. The solar cells are cut into narrow strips, layered like overlapping shingles, and connected to each other using electrically conductive adhesive, rather than metal wires.

This means that the electrical current can flow around shaded areas, unlike with standard modules. "If you get like a bird pooping on a part of the cell, the current can go around it," Learoyd explains.

In addition to increasing energy output, this improves the appearance as the silver wires aren't visible.

Another advantage, Heinrich says, is that "since we cut the cells into smaller areas, we can fill out special sizes more easily. You don't have to adhere to the regular pattern size."

Freesuns is an early adopter of matrix shingling technology in building-integrated PV. "We see it as a big opportunity for the future in terms of how we would make our tiles," Learoyd says.

What would also help the company is better software for automated design. This would speed up the design process, since each roofing job is unique.

Learoyd believes this would lower costs and give "the whole construction industry much more confidence in using the product".

Ten years after her own solar roof was installed, and despite the sector's setbacks, Learoyd remains optimistic.

She believes that for people with the means, it will eventually become standard to install a new roof with some sort of embedded solar. "It's just a no-brainer."

#309. How long will humans be needed for translation?

Google's translation service handles one trillion words per month

"Il pleut des cordes," say the French when it's raining hard.

"It's raining ropes," is the direct translation. It's the kind of idiom which can trip up an AI translator.

"You can imagine the dilemma this throws AI into," says FranÃ§ois Yvon, a research director specialised in automatic translation with the French National Centre for Scientific Research CNRS.

He says a well-trained system will know that ropes are not literally falling from the sky and use the English phrase "it's raining cats and dogs".

"But is it going to find the equivalent when it's translating into Indonesian?" asks Yvon.

A comment on the weather is pretty low-risk, but more and more translation is being done by machines, and on topics with higher stakes than the weather.

In April, Google said people are now translating, external one trillion words a month with Google Translate.

That doesn't count the others: DeepL, Microsoft, Claude, ChatGPT and Gemini, not to mention made-to-measure corporate translation systems.

Direct sales of machine translation, external (i.e. outside general AI subscriptions) were worth an estimated $1.4bn (Â£1bn) last year.

AI translation is the "right call" in many cases says Jonathan Zimmermann

"For plenty of use cases, raw AI translation is now the right call: multiplying your costs by 100 to catch the last few percent of errors isn't rational if you're translating internal documents or product listings," says Jonathan Zimmermann, founder of OKA (Open Knowledge Association), a Swiss non-profit that translates Wikipedia articles into other languages.

"For an encyclopedia it's different. Wikipedia runs on trust. A wrong fact can sit there for years and get cited by journalists and AI systems," he says.

With more than 10,000 published pieces, mostly into Spanish, Portuguese and English, OKA is, Zimmerman says, the single largest contributor of translated articles to the collaborative online encyclopedia.

Which is something it absolutely could not have become without AI.

"Translating 10,000 articles the traditional way would cost millions. In our process, the AI produces the draft, then a human translator checks every sentence," he explains.

But that human input is required less and less.

"The machine output is now good enough that skipping the human entirely has become a serious option, and a real temptation across the industry. Two or three years ago, with tools like DeepL, our translators still rewrote a good chunk of every draft.

"Today the prose often doesn't need fixing at all. So, the question is no longer whether AI translation is good, it clearly is, but whether it's good enough to remove the human," says Zimmermann.

She also makes the point that, as AI translators have got better, catching errors has got more difficult.

"Whereas in the past, with things like Google Translate, the sentence would jump out at you because there'd be a grammatical error or it would sound clunky, today it sounds perfectly legitimate. It sounds fluid. It sounds natural. So the errors are a lot harder to spot."

Even sentences that seem quite clear to a human can trip up an AI service, he says.

"When you have words with different meanings, it's usually very clear for a human which one applies because they understand the context, but when a machine translator comes across the sentence 'translate into Polish', for example, it is quite capable of deciding it must be all about shoe cream, says Yvon.

Context is often crucial. If an AI translator hasn't grasped that we're talking about a football match it is liable to mis-translate "taking a corner".

Last year OKA ran a study, funded by Wikimedia. It studied 119 articles, 10 language pairs and more than 1,000 hours of translator work.

"27% of the AI-generated text still got corrected before publication. Three quarters of those corrections were errors the AI introduced, and 5 to 6% of errors changed the meaning of the original text," says Zimmerman.

"As things stand, I think the fundamental way these systems function makes it quite hard to imagine how they could become 100% reliable," says FranÃ§ois Yvon.

He thinks a new generation of tech will be an improvement.

"We will see translation systems that are increasingly capable of evaluating what they do and correcting themselves using different AI tools simultaneously: one to do the translation, a second to criticize it and a third to produce a new version, for example."

But perhaps the big moment will be when AI can show doubt.

"The real breakthrough won't come until these systems are not merely capable of translating but also of telling us when they're not sure," Yvon says.
