#455. Automakers like Rivian and Ford are ‘democratizing 3D printing’ to reduce costs and increase speed

DETROIT — Automakers including Ford Motor and Rivian Automotive are leaning into a new type of manufacturing line: 3D printing.

Manufacturers say it's helping them reduce costs and increase speed. The technology has been around for decades, but it's becoming more commoditized in the automotive industry, with 3D printers used to create everything from vehicle parts and accessories to tools for assembly plants and testing units for research and development labs.

"At this point, we don't have a choice but to scale up and begin using it through all factions of Rivian," Jonathan Dankenbring, Rivian senior manager of prototype manufacturing, told CNBC during a tour of the automaker's expansive 3D-printing operations in suburban Detroit. "We're going to be democratizing 3D printing for the entire company."

Also known as additive manufacturing, 3D printing can create three-dimensional objects from digital designs by dispensing material layer by layer, much like a traditional printer does with ink on paper. Instead of ink, the machines use a wide variety of materials such as plastics, composites, ceramics and even metals.

Printers can range from hundreds of dollars for hobby-like devices to hundreds of thousands of dollars for specialty equipment, depending on how big the product being produced is and the material it needs. They can produce individual pieces or hundreds of smaller parts in a batch using powders and resins.

Rivian said it has spent more than $5 million on 3D-printing machines, with the cost of materials for the operations growing to about $1.7 million annually.

Dankenbring said despite the costs, the machines typically pay for themselves within a year. He said the technology helps lower supplier tooling costs, streamline logistics expenses and cut production times for parts from weeks to days, if not hours.

"It's well in the millions [for savings], but the main thing is speed for Rivian," Dankenbring said.

The all-electric vehicle company's latest expansion of 3D printing includes adding new machines that can build flexible silicone parts as well as installing a bay of machines at its plant in Normal, Illinois, for employees to print parts on demand.

"3D prints should be readily available for every engineer so that they can make a part and get it in their hands as soon as possible," Dankenbring said.

Rivian has also used the technology for electric-motor models for fluid testing, creating screw guides for tooling and stamping press inserts and some production parts, including trim pieces to fill gaps in some early builds.

There's a similar push underway at larger, more-established automakers like Ford and its crosstown rival General Motors, which have used 3D printing for many years, including in their massive assembly plants.

Instead of having a bay of 3D-printing machines like at Rivian, Ford has departments for additive manufacturing at many of its plants to produce and distribute needed parts. That includes a new vending machine for 3D-printed parts for production of F-150 pickup trucks near its Dearborn, Michigan, headquarters.

"By putting the vending machine up, all the team leaders have access," said Gregory Foresi, a Ford mechanical engineer who ran Ford's 3D-printing lab at the plant from 2024 until last month. "We're able to keep that thing stocked rather than have a bunch of parts laying around in the lab. So that's been pretty huge."

Foresi used roughly two dozen machines to print off thousands of tools, some of which have been designed by employees, such as torque bit sockets, hood jackers and hand tools for smoothing adhesive.

Employees can use an app to request that specific auto parts or tools be printed. Ford's 3D-printing teams help develop them or determine what to produce next, as the machining time to build different parts can range from hours to days, if not weeks.

Foresi said creating the tools with 3D printing has allowed the automaker to uniquely design them when needed, get them faster and, in many cases, receive them at a lower cost.

Ford also has used 3D-printed parts to help keep production running. For example, Foresi said a large machining wheel was needed to maintain assembly line production and the company was able to 3D print the part until a permanent replacement from a supplier was available.

"They didn't perform as well, and it's not really a replacement for the ones that we were purchasing, but they kept the plant running," Foresi said.

Ford said it expects to have 3D labs deployed at more than 23 plants by the end of this year. Its current 3D-printing program has more than 200 machines, and there are additional printers outside the official program, the company said.

GM has used 3D-printed parts for some of its low-volume products in addition to research and development and inside plants, including recently expanding its use of industrial Stratasys printers that can cost hundreds of thousands of dollars based on use case, training and certification.

The Detroit automaker uses 135 components that are 3D-printed for its bespoke Cadillac Celestiq as well as other components for its performance Cadillac Blackwing sedans, including a unique medallion on the manual shifter knob.

Fadi Abro, senior global director of automotive and mobility at 3D-printing company Stratasys, said GM has been a great example of how companies can "standardize and then scale" 3D printing.

"The material properties improved so significantly that the use cases improved," he said, noting the company works with virtually every major automaker. "From a product perspective, we have to get the technology to become faster and more accessible, in an economic sense."

Right before the midterm elections, OpenAI hired Jessica Schumer, daughter of Democratic Sen. Chuck Schumer. The company also said it would start calling its data centers “compute campuses.”

For the past two years, many of Silicon Valley’s biggest names have built closer ties with President Donald Trump and his administration. Several attended White House events, some companies scaled back diversity, equity and inclusion programs, and some executives donated millions to super PACs supporting his campaign and allied candidates. Administration policies also benefited the industry, including tax breaks, the pausing of some federal investigations, and looser AI regulation. Meanwhile, Meta faced lawsuits and billion-dollar fines, and OpenAI faced suits alleging its technology harmed users.

Now, with the midterms shifting the balance of power in Washington, those same companies are working to rebuild ties with Democrats, who have pledged to tighten oversight of AI. How far that gets them may depend less on image-polishing than on where the real fights are happening: in state capitols, in communities deciding whether to host data centers, and, as we’ve seen this past week, in the markets watching the AI boom.

J.B. Branch, director of federal AI governance and technology policy at Public Citizen, a nonprofit advocacy group that describes itself as nonpartisan, told me that the tech industry “realizes they have an optics problem.” He characterized moves like the Schumer hire and the rebranding as “PR washing.” He expects more of it, too, pointing to OpenAI’s partnership with the American Federation of Teachers, announced last year, as well as Anthropic’s newly launched Claude Corps and Meta’s America’s Workforce Academy. “I would imagine more of that sort of investment in these corporate, private-public partnerships,” he said.

These initiatives didn’t spring up overnight. Meta and other tech companies reportedly began looking for ways to strengthen their ties to Democrats as Democratic gains in the midterms looked more likely. Reached for comment about the midterm elections’ impact on Meta, a company spokesperson pointed TechCrunch to remarks that Meta President Dina Powell McCormick made this week at WSJ Tech. “You have to work with the government that the American people elect,” McCormick had said. “I have seen it across the board that you have to be bipartisan, and you want to be, because you want buy-in on the things that you’re trying to do.”

Not everyone expects them to work. Alice Marwick, director of research at the tech policy nonprofit Data & Society, told me she expects Big Tech to try to pivot back toward the middle, “if not the left. But it’s a little too late,” she said. “Tech companies should be asking themselves how their products can make people’s lives better, because right now, it doesn’t seem like they are.”

If Democrats follow through on pledges to tighten oversight of AI and Big Tech, the industry could face a tougher environment. Data center moratoriums are gaining traction, and a wave of AI regulation bills awaits drafting. At the same event, Powell McCormick said Meta wants to show that communities welcoming its investments will see “an enormous amount of benefit,” while those that don’t want them “will of course have the right to stop them.”

Still, the next two years could bring political gridlock in Washington. Aryan Ranjan, founder of Baraza, a political campaign management software company, told me most action will likely happen at the state level, since states have already sued tech companies and passed regulations. “A coalition built on newer, working-class voters is likely to push Democrats to frame AI around electricity bills, data-center siting and jobs rather than abstract AI safety,” he said of what to expect at the federal level.

Last December, the Trump administration signed an executive order creating the AI Litigation Task Force, a federal group meant to challenge state AI laws. The administration has argued that U.S. AI companies “must be free to innovate without cumbersome regulations.” Many states have nonetheless passed their own laws, including measures on chatbots and deepfakes.

It also remains to be seen how President Trump will respond, including whether he’ll sign more AI executive orders or veto bills that could be seen as anti-AI. Clearly, voters want stronger guardrails around the technology.

In a statement to TechCrunch, a White House official said: “American leadership in super intelligence is essential for our national security, economic prosperity, and the quality of life of the American people. President Trump is committed to ensuring America continues to lead the world in super intelligence while protecting American consumers. As the President has said, he’s willing to work with anyone to continue delivering victories for hardworking Americans.”

Branch argued that Trump’s political fortunes are now tied to the AI industry. On Wednesday, the day after the midterms, a trillion-dollar market sell-off spooked investors, some are whom are reportedly now reconsidering Anthropic’s reported trillion-dollar IPO. “There’s a very strong likelihood that there will be an AI bubble pop, and it’ll happen on Trump’s hands,” Branch said, predicting a possible market collapse. “Does he want to sink or swim along with the AI industry?”

For now, the rules companies face may vary by location. “For the next two years, a company’s AI rules may depend more on where it operates rather than on anything Washington passes,” Ranjan said.

#446. Sequoia shutting down its Scouts program marks the end of an era

Sequoia this week announced a broad overhaul in a memo posted to X, a move that includes closing its London office and shutting down its scout program. Observers warn that it would be a mistake to read too much into the London closure, since the British capital has a thriving AI scene. On the other hand, the decision to end scouts says more about where venture investing is headed.

That’s partly because 17 years ago, Sequoia pioneered the scouts model, in which select founders and operators write checks on the firm’s behalf. Amid increased competition for deal flow, many VC firms followed suit. But a consensus now seems to be emerging that the model has reached its limits in its current form.

Not everyone was sold on the model in the first place. “They pioneered the scout program but [to be honest] it never made sense to me!” VC Sheel Mohnot wrote on X this week. “The scouts themselves don’t really care that much about the investments, you can still miss (like they did with Uber), not sure what it gets you. I suspect other firms will shutter theirs too,” he added.

To be clear, Sequoia didn’t make a case against scouts in the memo. Instead, it framed its decision as part of a broader need for more focus. But its goal of “putting more energy behind fewer people and things” is likely to resonate with others who’ve questioned the ‘spray and pray’ approach to scouting.

Rival firm Andreessen Horowitz (a16z), for instance, rolled out a global scout network aimed at spotting opportunities in the U.S. and also in smaller European hubs. But a16z partner Gabriel Vasquez told TechCrunch earlier this year that the firm had revamped its program to double down “on the best people.”

Under this new approach, Vasquez explained, scouts get more resources but are also expected to be more active, such as by co-hosting dinners with the firm. “They get more budget, but we have reduced the number,” he said.

This also seems to indicate that it is VC firms that have been driving an overhaul of the model. For founders, being a scout is attractive: it brings them closer to firms they may want checks from someday, or even a partner role with, and being seen as someone who can write a check can raise their standing among other founders.

Some scouts will undoubtedly mourn the end of Sequoia’s program. Among them is Station F boss Roxanne Varza, who wrote on LinkedIn that she was “sad” to hear that the program was getting cut off, and “hope[d] that other funds [w]on’t follow suit.”

Varza, a former Sequoia scout from 2020 to 2025, told TechCrunch that she had no information beyond what has been shared publicly, but observed that becoming a scout had often been a stepping stone for aspiring investment managers.

“I cannot name all of the incredible scouts that have gone on to join or build funds, but there are quite a few. And the scout programs may have helped them get there — allowed them to build an investment track record, allowed them to build relationships with various funds directly, and more,” she wrote to TechCrunch.

According to Varza, the program’s impact went beyond the individual level, instead helping the entire ecosystem become more mature. “I believe they have helped to foster the investment culture and early-stage investment landscape that we now see in Europe, for example,” Varza said.

While others may agree with Varza about the role these programs have played, they may still have run their course for two reasons. First, seasoned operators may get more financial upside as angels. When it’s their own money, they get to keep the returns, too. Second, leading VC firms are now big and well-known enough that the best founders tend to come to them, so they worry less about missing deals, except at the very earliest stages or in niches and geographies where they have less reach.

Still, the pull of those early and niche deals is why scouts programs are much more likely to get revamped than to disappear entirely. “While Sequoia may be cutting the program, venture partner-style deals are likely to continue,” said Varza in her email to TechCrunch. “Scouts was just a way to scale the format. I don’t imagine Sequoia would end all forms of venture partners, but they are full of surprises.”

In October, Amazon announced its refreshed Kindle lineup, its first significant design overhaul in years. The highlights included a new edge-to-edge design that eliminated traditional raised bezels, thinner and lighter Paperwhite and Colorsoft models with better displays, and page-turning accessories like a Bluetooth remote and a magnetic cover with page-turn buttons.

However, given the impact of the global memory-chip shortage driven by the AI boom, the new Kindles also debuted with pricing that had increased by $40 to $50 across the lineup. That makes it more difficult for the average consumer to consider buying one of the more premium devices.

For that reason, the Kindle I most wanted to get my hands on was the 6-inch entry-level device, which starts at $169.99 for the ad-free version. With the price hikes, this is the Kindle that will remain the most affordable to new e-reader buyers.

The question I weighed was whether or not the basic Kindle is even worth buying at this price when there are arguably better Kindles on the market. The answer, after a couple of weeks of testing, is yes — if you can live without the bells and whistles and prefer a smaller, lighter device.

On the 6-inch Kindle, there’s no warm light to reduce eye strain, no color display, no auto-adjusting brightness, no option to use the page-turn cover, and it’s not waterproof. Obviously, because of its size, the screen is smaller, as is the battery. Battery life is 6 weeks compared with the Paperwhite’s 12 weeks or Colorsoft’s 8 weeks.

Still, even this basic Kindle has seen improvements of its own, mostly from a physical design perspective.

The plastic 2026 Kindle is 11% lighter and 14% thinner than the 2024 model, weighing 140g versus 158g and measuring 6.85mm thick versus 8mm. While this seems like a small change, the 18-gram difference is noticeable and particularly welcome for a device you’re likely to carry in your purse or bag. Plus, it makes it easier to hold for hours on end when you’re really binging your way through a book.

(The aluminum version, meanwhile, is slightly heavier at 162g, or 4 grams more than the 2024 Kindle, though it still benefits from the slimmer design.)

And despite being the “starter” Kindle, the 6-inch basic device still received the new flush-front screen update. This makes it more comfortable to hold in your hand, especially for those long reading sessions. Amazon explained that you don’t have to worry about no longer having a bezel to rest your thumb upon, either, as the new Kindles have been updated to detect a dead zone in the capacitive touch that will readjust to account for the fact that your finger is there.

In practice, that doesn’t mean you can just press your thumb down anywhere on the Kindle’s screen as you hold it — doing so would register this as an attempt to highlight or look up a word. Instead, it means you can rest your thumb or fingers on the outer edge of the Kindle’s screen (the colored border) without triggering an unintentional tap, even though some of your finger will be touching part of the Kindle’s capacitive screen.

In terms of the software, the new basic Kindle is a bit snappier than its 2024 predecessor. This is most noticeable when opening books, which Amazon claims to be 30% faster.

If you’re coming from the 2019 Kindle Basic, the 2026 version is an even bigger upgrade, as you gain USB-C, more storage (from 4GB to 8GB to now 16GB or 32GB), the new flush screen, and the screen resolution jumps from 167 ppi to 300 ppi. It’s faster, thinner, and lighter, too, and the battery lasts weeks longer.

Another perk about the basic Kindle beyond its small size (this thing fits in your jeans’ back pocket!), is that you can now pay a little more for an upgraded version with an aluminum backing instead of plastic and more storage. The new $189.99 aluminum option comes in Seagrass and Alpine Sky hues, and ships with 32GB of storage. Meanwhile, the $169.99 plastic model offers 16GB of storage.

However, even if you go for the less expensive option, you can at least now pick a fun color other than black (aka Graphite), as Amazon has introduced a cheery purple option called Ube. (I almost wish I’d gone for this one, just for the whimsy of it, even though I prefer the feel of the aluminum.)

I should point out that you can use the Kindle Click, the new Bluetooth remote for page turning, with the basic Kindle, but I’m not sure why you’d need to, if not for some accessibility-related reason. The 6-inch Kindle was already the smallest, and now it’s lighter and easier to hold, too. (But perhaps I just don’t get the appeal of the remote? I’ve barely used the remote with my Kobo either!)

The big thing to consider here is whether you want to spend this much on a Kindle that lacks a warm light. While this is something of a personal preference, for some readers, no warm light is a dealbreaker. The warm light shifts the color tone of the Kindle’s display from a cooler bluish-white to a softer amber glow, which is meant to help reduce eye strain and improve reading conditions in low light or at night.

Whether you need this feature depends on where you like to read and how long your sessions tend to be. If you read nightly in bed with little light, you might prefer paying extra for a $199.99 16GB Kindle Paperwhite. (That version only comes in Graphite. The 32GB Paperwhite can come in Seaglass, too, but is $249.99).

If you often read by the pool or on vacation, you also might want to upgrade to a waterproof Kindle.

But if you can live without the extra features and want the best purse- or pocket-sized device, the 6-inch Kindle is still a viable option.

As parents become increasingly concerned about the potential dangers of unrestricted smartphone access for children, a growing number of companies are developing devices specifically designed for kids.

Today’s kid-friendly phones typically feature touchscreens, cameras, and communication capabilities similar to regular smartphones. However, the software on these devices is heavily modified to remove or restrict access to web browsers, social media, app stores, and other distractions.

Parents can usually manage these devices through a companion app, allowing them to approve contacts, monitor locations, set screen time limits, select available apps, and, in some cases, receive alerts regarding cyberbullying, explicit content, or online predators.

In addition to these modified devices, some companies offer a more minimalist approach, creating devices that focus solely on calling and texting.

There are also home phones designed specifically for children, like Tin Can and the recently launched Pinwheel Home.

Here’s a look at the major players shaping the kid-phone market.

The Bark Phone is probably one of the most popular kid-specific smartphones on the market.

Built on Samsung Galaxy hardware and powered by Bark’s monitoring software, it only allows calls and texts with approved contacts. Bark continuously scans texts, emails, photos, and supported apps for signs of cyberbullying, grooming, suicidal ideation, sexual content, and other potential concerns, then sends alerts to parents.

A big standout feature is that parents can gradually unlock web browsing, apps, and other features as their child matures. The phone also includes GPS tracking and screen-time management.

Current pricing for the standard model is $240, plus a required wireless plan beginning at $29/month. Higher-tier plans add internet access and unlimited texting.

Gabb is a well-known name in the kid-friendly phone category. Unlike Bark, however, Gabb focuses less on monitoring content and more on eliminating online risks altogether.

Its phones don’t include social media, a browser, or an app store. Instead, children get calling, texting, and a carefully curated selection of preloaded apps, including a camera, calendar, and calculator. The company also touts its “worry-free” music streaming library with millions of songs.

Additionally, parents can view location and manage certain device settings, while optional services such as “Gabb Guard” help block spam calls and unwanted texts.

Current phone pricing starts around $159.99, with cellular service starting around $24.99 per month.

With Pinwheel, parents approve every app, control contacts, schedule screen time, create custom routines, and monitor location history.

One standout feature is the ability to create different “modes” throughout the day. For example, limiting the phone to calling and navigation during school hours before unlocking additional features after homework.

Pinwheel phones start around $119, while the Caregiver Portal subscription begins at $14.99 per month without cell service. (Cellular service can be purchased separately through participating carriers.)

The company recently launched two landline phones for $68 and $79. It also offers a smartwatch at $160.

Launched in 2022, Teracube offers a tailored version of Android known as Thrive OS. This enables parents to approve every app download, filter web browsing, set app-specific and overall screen-time limits, monitor location, and create customized routines for school or bedtime. However, because it has more traditional smartphone features, it’s often recommended for older children.

Thrive currently sells for $99, with plans starting at $35 per month.

Unlike the other products in this roundup, Ooma MyPhone isn’t a mobile phone at all. This kid-focused home phone is designed as a modern replacement for the traditional household landline.

The service allows calls only between approved contacts through its Trusted Circle feature, offers scheduled Quiet Hours, provides address-based 911 service, sends emergency alerts when 911 is dialed, and lets parents review call logs through an online portal. There are no apps, internet browsing, texting, or social media.

The phone costs $99.99, while service starts at $7.99 per month.

For $100, Tin Can resembles a landline phone with a distinctive tin can design, which is the inspiration behind its name.

This device does not require a traditional phone jack, but connects via Wi-Fi. Only approved contacts can make calls, effectively preventing spam or unauthorized communications. Parents can easily manage contacts through a user-friendly companion app.

Tin Can offers a free plan for calling other Tin Can users, as well as a $9.99 per month plan that allows calls to all approved contacts.

A newer entrant in the space, Waffle recently opened preorders for its screen-free devices: Waffle Home ($119), a landline phone, and Waffle Go ($169), which looks like a portable walkie-talkie or kids’ toy with three simple buttons that can only make voice calls and listen to voice notes.

Parents can approve contacts via the companion app, as well as see location and control when devices can call.

TextNow, the Canadian telecommunications company, recently launched its new mobile phone for kids. It’s called Yello! and while it looks like a normal touchscreen smartphone, it doesn’t have an app store, social media, or unrestricted web access.

Parents can see their kids’ location and battery life, as well as control things like the games they play and who they have in their contacts.

Yello! costs $149.99 upfront, with a $19.99 per month plan.

#261. McDonald's stock rises as earnings beat, U.S. restaurants show improvement

McDonald's on Wednesday reported mixed quarterly results as traffic to its U.S. restaurants fell, leading to a slight decline in domestic same-store sales.

The fast-food giant reported a 0.2% decrease in sales at its U.S. restaurants that have been open at least 13 months. Even so, the segment performed better than the 0.5% decline that Wall Street anticipated, according to a survey of analysts by StreetAccount.

"In the U.S., we're encouraged by improvement as the quarter progressed following our intervention on value," McDonald's CEO Chris Kempczinski said in a statement.

McDonald's same-store sales returned to growth in September after falling in July and August, CFO Ian Borden said on the company's earnings conference call. However, in response to an analyst question, he clarified that he did not say that U.S. same-store sales would be better in the fourth quarter.

Kempczinski said in August that the company's U.S. business was falling short on execution, particularly on the implementation of its value offerings. Like many restaurants, McDonald's has leaned into discounts to attract budget-conscious diners over the last two years.

To reverse same-store sales declines, McDonald's focused its marketing on its Extra Value Meals and restarted regional and national promotions on its mobile app, which are important to its most frequent diners.

As McDonald's tries to regain its footing in the U.S., the chain is also testing two new value menus, one with mix and match bundles and the other with set combinations of menu items, executives said on the earnings call. The offering that resonates the most with customers and franchisees will be rolled out across the U.S. in early 2027.

Shares of the company closed more than 6% higher Wednesday.

Here's what the company reported compared with what Wall Street was expecting, based on a survey of analysts by LSEG:

The company reported third-quarter net income of $2.49 billion, or $3.51 per share, up from $2.28 billion, or $3.18 per share, a year earlier.

Excluding restructuring charges and other items, McDonald's earned $3.57 per share.

The company's same-store sales rose 1.5%, lifted by its international segments. That topped analysts' expectations of a 1.3% increase, according to StreetAccount.

McDonald's international developmental licensed markets business, which includes Japan and China, reported same-store sales growth of 4.2%. Wall Street was forecasting same-store sales to rise 2.1% for the division, based on StreetAccount estimates.

The company's international operated division, which includes top markets like Australia and Germany, saw same-store sales growth of 1.4%. Still, the business underperformed Wall Street's expectations. Analysts were projecting same-store sales would rise 2.1%, according to StreetAccount.

"In the majority of our large international markets, we're holding or gaining share," Borden said on the company's conference call. "It's a soft consumer environment, but we're doing well."

France was a weak spot, McDonald's said. Kempczinski said that franchisees in France recently voted on a strategy to turn the business around. In addition to a softer economy in the country, McDonald's has also seen short-term disruptions related to student protests.
