#481. Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella explains why he asked 60K employees to quit

Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella explained Wednesday why he offered buyouts to 60,000 of his employees in October, essentially urging them to voluntarily quit instead of reducing the workforce via another massive layoff.

Short answer: AI, but not, he insists, in the way you might expect. He’s not saying he’s replacing employees with AI, especially software engineers. He’s saying he’s reorganizing Microsoft into a company where everyone uses AI to do their work, and their jobs become focused on managing that AI.

The buyout was “to make sure that at least everyone felt like they had agency in making choices,” he told Andrew Ross Sorkin during the DealBook Summit.

“Let’s take software engineering. We went from, ‘Well, we don’t buy that AI can ever do software.’ To suddenly, ‘Wow, it can do all software, and why do we need software engineers?’ To now rediscovering that software engineering is very much alive and, in fact, is going to thrive, except the way the team is organized and the work that a software engineer does has now got a new operating point,” he explained.

For some people, “that reorganization and change is sort of hard, right?” he added, “but, I mean, it has to be done.”

He offered the example of the team that builds Cowork, the AI agent in Microsoft 365 apps. He said the team consists of just 11 developers who have mastered using AI to do their jobs. “They build a software factory that builds the product. They don’t build the product.”

He believes that every employee will one day similarly become a manager of agents, rather than a person directly doing the work. “That level of direction is what, by the way, is going to happen in all jobs.”

But Microsoft, as a 51-year-old company, didn’t grow up as an AI-first company. “So the question is, how quickly can an incumbent transform the work, the workflow, and the work artifact, and with all the wage support that goes with it?” he said. “It’s going to take a bit more time than anyone expects. And there is going to be in all of this, you know, people who may say, ‘Hey, I have some other choices I want to make.’”

When asked about this same topic — why ask 60,000 people to voluntarily resign — last week by Gergely Orosz on his The Pragmatic Engineer podcast, Nadella further explained, “We have created enormous anxiety in people sort of feeling that they don’t have control of their own destiny because the way we have talked about this AI revolution.”

He called out talk about the singularity (the hypothetical point at which AI surpasses human intelligence) and predictions that AI will take away all jobs. “All of that leads anyone who even wants to change to be sort of anxious … You have to sort of walk the hard walk of really making the structural changes, getting people slotted, giving them opportunity.”

In other words, the buyout was the message to employees: You can get with the new AI-first program, which will radically change your job, or you choose to get on with your life. Elsewhere.

The assistant works inline with the cursor — just like Grammarly — and also manages other agents. Superhuman is also making the agent proactive, which means it looks for your schedule, keeps an eye on your email, and even drafts responses. The updated Go agent also learns users’ writing styles and responds accordingly in email and Docs.

The productivity company is adding 20 new connected agents, including DeepL, Legora, Amplitude, Databricks, and Blackboard, through its Go SDK.

While Go might not directly compete with the likes of Instinct, Muse, and Fo, Superhuman wants users to choose its assistant over something like OpenAI’s Dots or Claude Duet.

#474. Nimble enters the item-tracking market with eco-friendly luggage tags and keychains

As Google steps up its competition with Apple in the item-tracking market, smaller brands are also looking to carve out a piece of the category. The latest entrant is Nimble, the sustainable accessories brand best known for its charging products.

Founded in 2018 by former Mophie executives, the California-based company announced Thursday that it’s expanding into the category with “Trace,” a seven-product collection of item trackers designed to blend tracking technology into everyday accessories.

Nimble’s new collection arrives as competition in the Bluetooth tracking market continues to heat up. Apple’s AirTag helped popularize the category, while Google recently introduced its first-party tracker, the $29 Pixel Tag, powered by its Find Hub network. Meanwhile, established third-party players, such as Chipolo and Pebblebee, continue to expand their lineups.

Nimble is taking a slightly different approach. Rather than introducing another stand-alone tracker to attach to a set of keys or slip into a bag, the company has integrated tracking technology directly into practical accessories, including wallets, keychains, luggage tags, and passport covers.

The lineup includes seven products, ranging from the $14.95 Trace Tag, which features a replaceable coin-cell battery, to the $49.95 Trace Folio, a passport cover with built-in rechargeable tracking. Other options include the Trace Keyfob ($19.95), Trace Slim Wallet Card ($29.95), Trace Keychain ($29.95), Trace Luggage Tag ($39.95), and Trace Wallet ($39.95).

The entire collection is compatible with both Apple’s Find My and Google’s Find Hub networks.

Ross Howe, co-founder and CEO of Nimble, told us that the company’s approach is designed to differentiate its products from traditional trackers.

“Most locators are built as utility gadgets, not as things you want to own. Few were asking why the locator couldn’t just be the nice object itself. So, for example, we made a wallet findable instead of making a tracker that fits a wallet. We also thought more options should be rechargeable.”

The Trace Luggage Tag and Trace Folio are arguably the most interesting products in the collection. Dedicated luggage tags and passport covers with built-in tracking remain relatively uncommon, giving Nimble an opportunity to stand out in a market. There are only a few alternatives, including Spotminder’s $59 passport cover with Apple Find My support, which is $10 more than Nimble’s option. Knog Scout offers a luggage tag, although I find its design a little too bulky.

Rechargeability is another appealing aspect of Nimble’s new collection. Several models support USB-C or wireless charging, offering an alternative to the disposable batteries found in many traditional trackers. Howe says battery life lasts for “months,” with the Trace Keyfob, for example, rated for approximately six months on a charge.

Sustainability is another major part of Nimble’s pitch. Staying true to its roots, the company has incorporated eco-friendly materials throughout the collection, including recycled plastics and plant-based, vegan aloe leather. The products have also undergone third-party testing for chemicals of concern.

“We’ve spent the last two years going through the chemistry of our products line by line to remove or limit as many harmful ingredients as possible. Trace is the first collection designed specifically to meet these higher standards. We’re not 100% yet, but the independent labs checking our work will help us get there,” Howe said.

The collection is available now through its website and Amazon. All seven products come in five color options: Black, Stone, Tangerine, Plum, and Teal.

#467. Anthropic’s IPO puts AI’s circular financing boom to the test

Arriving at public markets today, Anthropic’s IPO is poised to raise billions for the company and its investors. But the biggest surprise may be just how much of that money will end up going to supplier-investors.

Amazon and Google, two of Anthropic’s largest infrastructure partners, hold stakes valued around a combined $307.9 billion at the company’s updated IPO price of $83 per share. Amazon, which invested $18 billion into the AI startup, would see its 14.7% stake hit upwards of $159 billion, while Google’s 13.7% stake is valued at nearly $149 billion, according to share ownership figures disclosed in Anthropic’s IPO prospectus.

A handful of other AI infrastructure providers are also positioned to benefit. Nvidia, AMD, Microsoft, Broadcom, Micron, and TSMC have joined Google in committing $26 billion to Anthropic at its IPO price in a private placement.

In total, Google has invested $12.3 billion into Anthropic since 2023, with another promised $30 billion dependent on Anthropic meeting certain performance milestones. And with the exception of TSMC, Anthropic has announced multibillion-dollar strategic partnerships with its other infrastructure supplier investors — including Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, Microsoft, and Micron — that would see Anthropic purchasing their products.

This is the circular financing that has made some observers so skeptical of the AI boom. These companies are not just investors; they’re also Anthropic’s biggest suppliers. It might look like Amazon and Google are putting billions into Anthropic, but they get that money right back when Anthropic needs to train a model or serve Claude to users. From afar, it looks like unprecedented revenue growth for cloud companies and unprecedented fundraising from Anthropic — but it’s just the same money passing from one pocket to another.

Today’s IPO marks a critical change in that financing system, the moment it might just become self-sustaining. Those investments appear to have paid off, and are now liquid. Anthropic is still operating at a net loss of tens of billions of dollars each quarter — but it will no longer be suppliers and private investors footing the bill. Now, capital will come from a mix of public-market investors and corporate bonds, neither of which have the conflicts-of-interest that caused concerns before.

And critically, the company’s new bottom line looks pretty good.

Once it debuts, Anthropic is expected to have about $220 billion in cash, cash equivalents, and short-term investments, including proceeds from its IPO and the $26 billion private placement. Anthropic also has the ability to pay for future commitments in stock.

Joey Brookhart, who leads the Tokenomics team at SemiAnalysis, estimates that Anthropic needs roughly $280 billion in cumulative revenue over the next four years to pay for its compute bill, which Brookhart said is “achievable given where they will be next year from a revenue perspective.”

“The ownership stake is very worthwhile, and those companies no longer need any more support from [suppliers],” Brookhart told TechCrunch. “Amazon doesn’t need to prop them up anymore. The revenue is all coming from other customers.”

That doesn’t mean the server bills are going down, though. Anthropic has already committed to spend $900 billion, primarily for compute and infrastructure, according to a regulatory filing. Certain agreements have termination provisions, but Brookhart thinks the company is in a strong position to pay up.

On the other side, Anthropic generated $17 billion in third-quarter 2026 revenue, putting the company on pace for a $68 billion annualized rate for the year. If Anthropic continues to grow at its current trajectory, Brookhart believes it could easily cover the gap needed to fund its compute.

Gil Luria, head of technology research at D.A. Davidson, agreed.

“By the time the IPO is done, Anthropic’s going to have more cash on hand than any company has ever had,” Luria told TechCrunch, noting that the firm raised around $141 billion in capital and will take home around $91.7 billion in net proceeds at $83 per share. “And they’re not burning cash. They’re just going to have a war chest.”

A public listing will make Anthropic a more credible borrower, making it easier to raise money outside of public markets, says Brookhart. That’s despite recent reports that creditors might not see Anthropic as “investment-grade” material.

Luria distinguished between Anthropic’s supplier investments and neoclouds like CoreWeave, which he sees as more purely supported by circular financing. “CoreWeave wouldn’t exist if Nvidia didn’t prop them up, and it probably won’t exist if Nvidia doesn’t bail them out,” Luria said.

Brookhart also pushed back on describing Anthropic’s financing as purely circular, saying it was still “additive to the ecosystem.”

For both analysts, the biggest risk to Anthropic is simply that it will lose the AI race. Right now, the company’s growth is fueled by its status at the frontier of generative AI — but if it’s overtaken by OpenAI, Google DeepMind, or even cheap open-weight models, that could change fast. As Luria put it: “If we’re all using other people’s models, then Anthropic’s not worth a trillion and a half dollars. It’s worth nothing.”

But regardless of who wins the frontier, compute suppliers will likely be just fine. Neither Brookhart nor Luria believe Anthropic’s growth is at risk, despite some arguments that cheaper tokens and more competition will directly affect the AI company’s bottom line.

“For Amazon and Google, the risk isn’t actually that high because in that scenario, we’re using other models,” Luria said. “I’m arguing we’re going to use a lot more AI. I don’t know if it’s all Anthropic’s AI…. That’s still going to come from a data center managed by Amazon, Google, and Microsoft.”

#463. UK government forced to disclose secret Apple backdoor order

The U.K. government has been ordered to confirm that it issued a secret order demanding Apple to provide authorities with encrypted customer information.

The Thursday ruling from the U.K.’s Investigatory Powers Tribunal, an independent court that allows citizens to challenge government surveillance orders, is a rare rebuke for the government, which sought to keep the encryption backdoor order a state secret.

The tribunal’s ruling rejected the Home Office’s claim that disclosing the order would be damaging to national security.

California-based Apple refused the order, and blocked U.K. users from switching on its Advanced Data Protection data-encryption feature for iCloud, rather than comply with the demand. The U.K.’s secret order was later narrowed to allow access to British nationals’ data stored in Apple’s encrypted cloud, and Apple also challenged this second order.

Prior to the ruling, Apple was legally barred from disclosing the order, even after details leaked. When reached for comment, Apple told TechCrunch that it welcomes the decision to make public the fact that the U.K. Home Office issued a technical capability notice.

Apple previously said that it has “never built a backdoor or master key” to any of its products or services and “never will.”

A spokesperson for the U.K. Home Office, which has yet to confirm the existence of a notice, did not respond to a request for comment.

Rights group Liberty International, which brought the legal challenge on behalf of two individuals, said that this is the first known time that the government has been compelled to confirm the existence of a secret technical order.

A final hearing on what specific order details can be released is anticipated next year.

#460. At Nasdaq opening bell, Anthropic CEO recommits to pacing AI development

As Anthropic made its Wall Street debut on Thursday in the largest IPO in history, CEO Dario Amodei reaffirmed the company’s commitment to developing AI responsibly. In his speech ahead of the Nasdaq opening bell, Amodei vowed that the AI company would not bend to either business or political pressure and would “remain under human control.”

Reading the speech from a printout, the CEO began by talking about Anthropic’s origins as a way to build powerful technology to help humanity — something that he said was inspired by the loss of his father to a disease that became curable just a few years later.

He acknowledged that while human intelligence had made diseases like the plague, smallpox, and polio history for most of the world, there were still too few scientists with too little time to make progress in other areas of disease. AI, he said, could help with this by working in concert with humans to further medical progress.

It’s telling that Amodei began with a story highlighting AI’s potentially lifesaving applications, since he’s warned repeatedly about the risks of advancing the technology too quickly. In September, Amodei drew headlines when he outlined a plan to pace AI development, which included things like coordinating with other leading companies on safety standards and embedding evaluators from third-party organizations to verify that companies were following their safety commitments.

OpenAI CEO Sam Altman, Google DeepMind chief Demis Hassabis, and xAI owner Elon Musk said they agreed with Amodei’s position, although few concrete commitments have been made.

In this morning’s speech, Amodei again stressed the need to progress AI technology and the safety required in doing so. He referred to the “very real risks” of AI in the hands of bad actors, whether those were individuals or nation-states, and the potential for things like “economic disruption” and “losing control of AI systems.”

Anthropic believes these risks are avoidable, he noted, as long as there are safeguards in place. Amodei said the company would continue to be invested in the work of AI alignment and testing, and developing new ways to understand AI behavior so it could remain under human control.

“There is much work still to be done, and the most difficult safety work lies ahead of us,” he said. “The incredible talent of the company that has enabled us to build the most capable AI systems will need to be directed more so than it has already towards double and triple checking that those AI systems are safe and remain under human control.”

“And to state it unequivocally, we will pace the training and deployment of AI systems, taking as long as is necessary to have confidence that they are safe and will remain under human control,” Amodei concluded.

His sister, Anthropic co-founder Daniela Amodei, echoed these sentiments in an interview with CNBC on Thursday, noting that while the risks were real, the promise of AI was also “incredible.”

The round was led by Vertex Ventures Southeast Asia & India and January Capital, with participation from existing investors Square Peg and Lightspeed.

Everful is on track to reach $100 million in annualized revenue by the end of the year, about two and a half years after its launch, co-founder Sisi Song said. The company ships over 5 million items a month to customers in more than 60 countries and works with over 30,000 factory partners, she added. Its online wholesale catalog includes more than 20 million products.

Song, a former Alibaba executive, and serial entrepreneur Kevin Han, who sold a previous company to Amazon, co-founded Everful in 2024. The company began as a jewelry wholesale business before evolving into a multi-category, factory-direct sourcing platform.

Retailers and brands often rely on intermediaries, face high minimum order requirements, and experience long sourcing cycles, which tie up capital in inventory before sales trends are clear. Song noticed the growing gap between fast-changing consumer trends and traditional wholesale sourcing models while working as a venture investor at Bessemer Venture Partners, where she mostly focused on cross-border sourcing and meeting numerous startups in the sector. That same gap shaped Everful’s goal to simplify buying directly from factories through an AI-enabled platform.

The B2B sourcing industry is highly competitive. Big Tech giant Alibaba connects international buyers with manufacturers and suppliers, while Faire, a San Francisco-based wholesale tech platform, enables retailers to purchase wholesale inventory directly from brands. According to Everful’s co-founders, its key differentiator is responsibility for the sourcing process, rather than simply facilitating transactions.

“We take care of everything,” Han said. “We handle sourcing, order consolidation, quality control, and logistics. That’s something the marketplaces don’t offer.”

Everful is built for smaller retailers and brands without dedicated sourcing teams, Song said, and it also verifies the factories it works with. While peers also offer services such as quality inspection, logistics, and AI-powered sourcing, Everful aims to integrate these capabilities into a more streamlined purchasing experience, creating an end-to-end process that reduces the work retailers must handle themselves.

Han added that Everful uses AI agents to automate labor-intensive sourcing tasks, including tracking consumer trends, identifying suppliers, coordinating orders across factories, and managing logistics.

Because Everful sources from Chinese factories, ongoing U.S.-China trade tensions and tariffs bear directly on its business. Song said the co-founders have been thinking about geopolitical issues since day one of building the company, and that this has shaped Everful’s strategy from the outset, leveraging her experience at Alibaba and Bessemer Venture Partners.

She mentioned the Trump administration’s suspension of the de minimis exemption, which had allowed eligible shipments valued at $800 or less to enter the U.S. duty-free, as one example of a shift in trade policy.

Song explained such changes could favor a model that combines overseas sourcing with local retail sales. “We’re empowering local retailers and brands,” she said. “Long-term cross-border commerce will be cross-border B2B sourcing plus local selling.”

Everful plans to use the Series A round to expand its presence in the U.S. and Europe, particularly the U.K., France, and Italy, while investing in AI to automate product customization and other sourcing workflows. It also aims to deepen integrations with e-commerce platforms, building on its Shopify integration as it works toward becoming an end-to-end infrastructure platform for global sourcing.

The startup employs about 70 full-time staff globally, including 45 in research and development. It has also established an AI operations team to connect engineering and business functions.

#429. Measles is surging in the U.S. — and outbreaks can cost millions

Measles outbreaks aren't just a public health threat. They can also be a financial burden on the U.S. — and the cost could only deepen as the once-eliminated disease spreads more widely.

The U.S. is expected to lose its official measles elimination status following a review by the Pan American Health Organization this week, after cases ratcheted to recent highs this year.

More than 5,100 measles cases have been reported nationwide in 2026 as of early November, marking the country's worst outbreak in more than three decades. Eight deaths associated with the disease have been confirmed by states so far in 2026: six in Pennsylvania, one in New York and one in Wisconsin.

Based on this year's case count, a new tool developed by researchers at Johns Hopkins University estimates an economic cost of nearly $83 million for the outbreaks. That total includes $56 million in public health response costs, almost $25 million in lost productivity and more than $1.8 million in direct medical expenses.

The tool also estimates that 2025 measles outbreaks in the U.S. — which involved 2,289 confirmed cases nationwide – carried an economic cost of more than $37 million.

The costs extend beyond treating people who become sick, accounting for the resources health departments spend investigating and containing outbreaks, as well as wages lost when people have to miss work. The tool uses more recent, state-specific cost and financing data than an initial study by the researchers, which relied on historical outbreak costs.

The estimates from the tool are not a definitive accounting of what the outbreaks actually cost. But it illustrates the hefty bill tied to the spread of a disease that can be prevented through vaccination, and the potential economic consequences of falling childhood immunization rates. CNBC previously reported on how the return of measles in the U.S. exposes weaknesses in the nation's public health system that raise concerns about the return of other eliminated diseases.

"We're starting to see a whole bunch of people who decided not to get vaccinated as kids or have never gotten exposed to measles because they benefited from herd immunity, who now make it into adulthood and risk potentially getting quite seriously ill and requiring very significant medical expenses, or dying at the hospital," Bryan Patenaude, a health economist at the International Vaccine Access Center at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health, who helped develop the tool, said in an interview.

Measles outbreaks can trigger a costly public health response, even when many people who are exposed never become sick.

When a case is identified, public health officials must investigate the infection, identify and notify people who may have been exposed, coordinate with hospitals and schools, and sometimes organize vaccination campaigns to prevent further spread. Those efforts can divert staff and resources from other public health priorities.

Costs could differ heavily depending on the state.

Johns Hopkins' data includes national averages alongside state-specific inputs, including inpatient hospital costs and wages from government data or trusted health policy sources. In New York, for example, the average inpatient hospital stay is roughly $4,200 a day, while the minimum wage is $16 per hour.

The tool estimates that the 48 cases reported by New York in 2025 carried an estimated $1.1 million in costs, including nearly $749,000 in public health responses, $333,000 in productivity losses and roughly $24,500 in direct medical expenses.

Meanwhile, the 803 cases in Texas, the epicenter of the largest U.S. measles outbreak in 2025, carried estimated costs of nearly $13 million, according to the tool. That includes $9 million in public health costs, $4 million in productivity losses and around $288,000 in direct medical expenses.

The tool builds on a study published by Patenaude and other researchers in August, which examined the costs of measles outbreaks in the U.S. The researchers reviewed published studies and government reports to compile historical estimates of medical expenses and public health response costs across 18 states.

The paper estimated that outbreaks carried a historical average cost of nearly $59,000 per case, just from the public health response. The researchers also estimated how costs scale with outbreak size, separating fixed up-front response expenses from the additional costs associated with each new case.

"A lot of this is incurred up front, and that's because of the human labor that's involved in contact tracing and all of the different actors that sort of need to be mobilized to deal with the fact that there's an outbreak," Patenaude told CNBC. "It's a largely labor-driven cost."

That model estimated roughly $298,000 in fixed costs and about $16,300 for each additional case. But it relied on historical data, including a particularly large 2019 outbreak in New York City, where contact-tracing labor costs were high. The researchers also excluded productivity losses from that model because most of the historical studies they reviewed did not consistently account for them.

The economic burden of measles extends beyond the costs of treating infected patients and organizing a public health response.

People who are exposed to measles may have to quarantine, even if they never develop symptoms. Parents can also be forced to miss work to care for sick or exposed children, while workers who contract the disease may lose wages during their recovery.

A 2025 study by Joey Mattingly, a pharmacist and health economist at the University of Utah, used published epidemiological and cost data to model the potential economic burden of a hypothetical 1,500-case outbreak. His analysis included direct medical expenses, public health spending and lost productivity, and examined how the estimates changed under different assumptions, including hospitalization rates.

The study estimated that the hypothetical outbreak could cost about $90 million, including roughly $26 million in productivity losses.

Those indirect costs can add up quickly because measles is highly contagious and a single infected person can expose dozens of others. Those contacts may need to quarantine or miss work while health officials determine whether they have been infected, creating costs even when they never develop the disease.

Mattingly estimated that each person who is quarantined could amount to roughly $300 in lost productivity alone.

If a single measles case exposes 40 to 50 people who then need to quarantine, that could translate into an estimated $12,000 to $15,000 in lost productivity, even if none of those contacts become sick.

That ripple effect is one reason Mattingly argues that the economic costs of outbreaks can quickly outweigh the investment required to vaccinate people against measles.

"Even if you don't get the infection, the contact costs you," he said. "And so it's just showing that there's this multiplication or exponential growth on the economic side that happens."

The financial burden can also fall unevenly across communities. While the costs of responding to an outbreak may be shared among local, state and federal agencies, Mattingly said the impact is often felt directly by local health departments.

"It's not like your health department is sitting around waiting for measles outbreaks," he said. "They're working, doing all these other things, and imagine having to just drop what you're doing because there's been a huge outbreak in the community."

Patenaude said the findings could help state and local health departments better anticipate the resources needed to contain outbreaks — particularly as measles cases continue to rise.

"It's not super expensive to treat and deal with a single case of measles compared to a single case of cancer," he said. "But if you have these outbreaks popping up and you're having to constantly divert resources that could be spent elsewhere by state and local health departments on measles, then you're going to wind up spending a lot of money very quickly."

#426. These tech outages are giving Y2K, AI agent style

It’s going down, as the American rapper Yung Joc might say: the airline booking systems, banking platforms, and productivity platforms. In the past two weeks, the U.S.’s top businesses have faced technical outages that have disrupted the systems that run everyday life.

Apple Pay went down. NASA’s internal communication network went down. The FAA had to ground all flights for hours (only the third time in history since 9/11), and Microsoft was hit with an outage that took down Outlook, Teams, and Copilot for nearly two days.

The first thought, naturally, is an AI hacker invasion of some sort, especially after hackers managed to attack fridges in a grocery store and a blood bank in France a few weeks ago. Here in the U.S., the director of National Intelligence said these outages don’t seem related to state-sponsored terrorism, while President Trump called the issues “individual events.” Many of the companies themselves have said the outages were not caused by cyberattacks or AI attacks, but tech issues.

Only, there’s the fact that they all struck within a short timespan of each other, and just a few weeks after Cybersecurity’s so-called “Black October,” when a slew of also-unrelated cybersecurity incidents occurred on October 21, striking PHP, Node.js, LiteLLM, more.

While there’s no reason to view these outages as cyberattacks, there may be more than coincidence going on: It’s giving AI-native Y2K, frankly.

Dave DeWalt, an investor who worked in cybersecurity for more than three decades (including as president and CEO of McAfee and as vice chairman of the CISA Advisory Council for the Department of Homeland Security), also thinks it’s probably AI-related.

The technical outages are most likely the “growing pains of a security system that’s getting stronger, not weaker,” and that “AI is surfacing vulnerabilities at a speed and scale we’ve never had before through AI tools like Mythos and others,” he told TechCrunch.

The thing is, many of these technical flaws have always existed, but because AI is surfacing them so fast, enterprises are rushing to fix them before bad actors can exploit them. “That surge in patching is disrupting things in the short term,” he said.

This was, perhaps, the first moment when legacy meets modern, Anusha Iyer, a software engineer and founder of the security startup Corsha, told TechCrunch. “These industrial systems were never designed to be connected, let alone securely connected in the way we are pushing them to be today,” she said.

Virginia Senator Mark Warner, vice chair of the Senate Intel Committee, came to a similar conclusion (although investigations are still underway as to what could be happening). “These disruptions should serve as a wake-up call. It feels like every day brings another reminder that AI is moving faster than our ability to manage its consequences,” the senator wrote on X.

AI is reshaping how software is built, deployed, and operated, on a scale where everyone feels the sharp learning curve. Iyer cited the FAA outage as an example of legacy tech that is not as sophisticated or advanced as the technologies companies are trying to merge it with.

In less technical terms, she said the outage may have been caused (and preliminary findings point to this) when the FAA was trying to modernize its telecommunications infrastructure and the transition overwhelmed parts of its legacy hardware, where the new tech likely operated at a normal modern capacity while the legacy hardware couldn’t keep up.

When looked at this way, she said, AI is actually getting the bad rep. “It’s not rapid adoption of AI yet causing these outages,” she said. “The fault is the rushed mix of the legacy and modern systems without a clear understanding of operational and cyber risk or the ability to gatekeep and protect the legacy systems.”

Organizations need a more steadfast approach to AI adoption, including more testing and prioritizing what needs to be fixed and when, DeWalt said.

Iyer added, “These industrial systems need to be segmented more tightly and more access controlled, so as modern systems are introduced, it can be phased in and risk mitigated.”

Actually, there might be a silver lining to all of this for companies (and for venture investors). DeWalt said he’d rather see these U.S. enterprises find and patch their flaws, even in this messy way, than have bad actors find and exploit those vulnerabilities in AI warfare that could cause even more national security issues.

And maybe some aspiring founders were inspired by them. DeWalt expects a rise in companies focused on “AI-driven vulnerability management, autonomous remediation, safe patch testing and deployment, and resilience for the systems that keep critical infrastructure running,” he said.

“There’s real pain in this transition as we’ve seen in the past week,” DeWalt said. “But there’s also enormous opportunity for the companies that help organizations move at AI speed without breaking things.”

#419. Want to see your live AI usage on your Mac’s icons? This clever new app lets you

There’s a new way to keep an eye on your AI usage and when your limits reset: by using your Mac’s app icons. Yes, really!

Designer Michael Flarup has just launched a new Mac utility called IconSwapper, which lets you replace any app’s official icon with one of your own creation. While you can use this tool to change your icons’ appearance, design, shading, and more, one of the more interesting ways to use it is to swap the icons for AI apps like ChatGPT or Claude with versions that can display your live AI usage.

Doing so is fairly simple, and you don’t have to be technically minded to get this to work. After installing the app on your Mac, you simply choose which application you want to create a “live” icon for — meaning one that responds to a particular app.

Besides tracking AI usage in ChatGPT, Claude, and Cursor, you can also create live icons for a number of the built-in Mac apps like Messages, Mail, Safari, Finder, Music, and others like Chrome and Spotify, but these configurations tend to be more fun than they are practical. For instance, you can configure Safari’s compass to follow things like page loads and downloads, or you can make it so that the Finder guy’s eyes follow your cursor around the screen. (That one is hilarious!)

Of course, there are plenty of other ways to track your AI usage — you don’t need an icon to do that. You can track usage from your account or your harness, even from a fun little gadget. But tracking it from the icon adds a certain whimsy to your Mac desktop setup. (If your dock is crowded, note that you can also see your icon customizations from your tab bar as you switch apps.)

Beyond live icons, you can also change what an icon looks like using IconSwapper’s built-in tools.

There are a surprising number of built-in customizations that are as easy to use as clicking a few buttons or toggling a switch. IconSwapper also comes with a large library of alternative icons for every app that are already ready to use, or you can upload your own.

Flarup is based in Copenhagen, where he runs a design studio, and he has been drawn to icon design and customization for years. He’s been making his own Mac icons since the early 2000s and even wrote a book about app icons that celebrates the work of designers.

He had been working on a solution that would allow him to customize Mac icons, which had proven tricky because macOS has no official support for theming and protects its system icons. To work around this, he came up with a different technique that involves drawing directly on the icons without replacing them, allowing him to use shaders, motion, and display real data.

#411. Anthropic rival says companies can (mostly) skip the pricey models

On the same day that Anthropic went public in the largest IPO on record, with investor enthusiasm for the AI lab through the roof, far away at the Slush startup conference in Helsinki, the topic du jour was open source everything.

That’s the enormous opportunity being chased by companies like Fireworks AI, as enterprises seek ways to avoid paying frontier prices for their AI needs.

Fireworks runs AI models for other businesses. It specializes in open-weight models, which anyone can download, and helps customers tune them on their own data so they run faster and cheaper than the models of Anthropic, OpenAI, and other heavyweights, and are harder for rivals to copy. Customers have included Uber, Shopify, and Notion. Co-founder Chenyu Zhao and I spoke onstage toward the end of the day.

Zhao and many others believe companies are gravitating toward platforms like his because frontier models are pricey and, in most cases, he says, unnecessary unless you need the very latest and greatest generalized intelligence. There are also growing concerns that frontier labs absorb too much of their customers’ know-how, even if only through the prompts used to get answers, a worry Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella raised in July.

Fireworks has also gotten its first black eye, having disclosed two security incidents this fall. In one, hackers reached API keys and tokens stored as secrets for a limited number of accounts; in the other, attackers breached its cloud provider, made their way into a development system, and nabbed customer data, including billing records and some datasets.

Notion says AI chat transcripts from fewer than 1% of its workspaces were exposed, but it has disconnected from Fireworks. Uber says hackers stole the names, addresses, birth dates and driver’s license numbers of about 150,000 drivers through a vendor that TechCrunch identified as Fireworks. Fireworks will have to win back the confidence of customers like them.

Fireworks, assigned a $17.5 billion valuation by its investors in summer, is reportedly in “early talks” for yet another round, though Zhao declined to comment.

There’s money chasing everyone in the category, seems like. Rival companies Together AI and Baseten have announced massive rounds in recent months. On Wednesday, Palo Alto Networks said it will buy Applied Compute, which, like Fireworks, helps companies train and run custom AI models on their own data. The deal for the startup, founded just a year ago by former OpenAI researchers, is $4.5 billion, all of it in stock.

Here’s some of what Zhao had to say. This conversation has been edited for length and clarity.

You spent a decade at Google, including managing a 50-person engineering team on Google Cloud Vertex AI. Why leave when you did?

When you meet great co-founders, it’s obvious within five minutes that they’re really smart. I was sold after talking to Lin [Qiao] and Dima [Dzhulgakov]. It’s also important to find people who are genuinely nice, people you’ll enjoy working with.

How do you describe Fireworks to a CEO who has never heard of it?

I describe Fireworks as a specialized intelligence platform. OpenAI and Anthropic are building general intelligence. We use open models, which are often smaller, cheaper, and faster, and let companies add their own data to specialize them. That way you own your AI rather than being beholden to giant AI conglomerates. If you’re just using Opus or Sonnet, that’s not a differentiator.

How far behind are open models right now?

A few years ago I’d have said a year, a year and a half. Then came DeepSeek and a waterfall of fantastic open models. These days it’s at most six months behind, probably closer to three.

If the models are so close behind, why are businesses slow to switch?

Frontier models are a household brand, and before you find product-market fit, using the best model is low risk. But once you pass 10,000 or 100,000 users and you’re spending $10,000 to $100,000 a month on tokens, it’s time to think about open source.

In July, Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella warned that companies using proprietary AI models pay twice, once in money and again in know-how. Do you think that warning had a material effect?

We think we’ll live in a two-model world, with frontier and open models in parallel. You want the best intelligence for high-level planning, but you don’t need a frontier model for basic arithmetic. That’s why there’s so much interest in routing, and why people now think about cost per task, not just cost per token.

Anthropic went public today. What do you think of its valuation? There’s one scenario in which it just continues to cement its place in this sort of oligopoly; there’s another where it starts to lose traction.

On the record: I’ll probably call my broker tonight and buy a few shares. This is a long-term bet. In 10 years AI usage will be at a completely different scale, and that takes an incredible amount of capital, which is why these companies are going public.

Fireworks has had a couple of security breaches recently. What do you tell customers?

Every company is probably seeing an unprecedented frequency of attacks. For our incident, a lot of the motivation was LLM jacking: hunting through providers for API keys and secrets to sell tokens on illicit marketplaces. Our promise is zero data retention for inference traffic, and there’s no indication that has been compromised.

Notion has disconnected from Fireworks. Did you lose other customers?

I don’t think I can publicly name the specific customers that were impacted. Some, such as Notion and Cursor, have gone public already. It was a small subset of our customers that had this data impact.

How did that customer data get exposed, given your zero-retention promise?

Notion was using batch prediction, where you upload a dataset that’s stored at rest, so it’s not a zero-retention product by nature. We’re moving those services to an online flow so we keep data only as long as we need it.

Jensen Huang left Nvidia last week, and Nvidia has backed Fireworks since 2024. Does that affect you?

It’s been a blow to the industry, and we feel for Jensen and his family. But Nvidia has always been a strong supporter of open source, and I don’t see that changing. We expect it to keep investing in Nemotron, and we’re excited to keep partnering with them.

#388. Feds arrest top official of healthcare company, alleging illegal drug importation

U.S. authorities are accusing the two top officials of a Canadian company of operating a long-running scheme to illegally import prescription drugs into the U.S., according to court records filed Tuesday.

Bob Howard, his brother, who is president and a director of CANARX, is also wanted in the federal investigation, according to an affidavit from Dustin Dobbs, a special agent with the Food and Drug Administration's Office of Criminal Investigations. He has not yet been taken into custody.

Besides conspiracy and smuggling, the arrest warrant said John Thomas Howard was charged with introducing misbranded drugs into interstate commerce and illegal drug importation. No plea has been filed.

CANARX was featured in a CNBC investigation last year that revealed how the company and others known as alternative funding programs, or AFPs, are becoming more pervasive around the U.S. as drug costs skyrocket. The investigation found these companies have penetrated the country's healthcare system by offering high-cost specialty drugs at more affordable prices through private and public employers. In some cases, employers are requiring their staff to use an AFP.

AFPs often obtain the medications overseas and ship them directly to patients, a practice that federal authorities have said is illegal and poses safety risks. CNBC traced the trail of drugs from abroad to the U.S. via a supply chain that the pharmaceutical companies producing the medications have not authorized.

On its website, CANARX, founded in 2002 and based in Windsor, Ontario, Canada, described its business as the first international prescription provider for the U.S. and the safest such program in the industry. The company said it sources its medications from Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia and New Zealand.

But the affidavit said that the FDA's investigation found "there is probable cause to believe that (CANARX) illegally imported or caused the illegal importation of prescription drugs into the United States …."

The affidavit does not specify which medications the agency believes were illegally imported. The criminal complaint covers the period from Feb. 22, 2017, through April 15, 2026.

The company's general counsel, Joseph Morris, told CNBC in a statement: "The Howards deny all the charges against them, will defend vigorously, and expect to be vindicated in court."

The company has been under FDA scrutiny for several years. In 2023, the agency seized medication being shipped by CANARX that it says was potentially misbranded and counterfeit, according to an email sent to a patient that was obtained by CNBC.

And in 2019, the FDA sent a warning letter to the company, saying it was operating "in a manner that substitutes the FDA-approved drugs prescribed by the U.S. healthcare provider with unapproved drugs." Morris, in response to that warning, argued CANARX was not misleading patients, but agreed for the company to stop importing drugs that need to be administered by a doctor.

In an interview as part of the CNBC investigation, Morris said last year that the medicines shipped to U.S. patients come from highly regulated countries. He said CANARX was operating legally.

The central question for AFPs is the legality of importing prescription drugs into the U.S. for personal use. The FDA allows Americans to import medications for individual use in some cases if the drug is not available domestically. That is the basis on which the AFPs built their models for shipping medicines directly to patients.

But an FDA spokesperson told CNBC last year that "in most circumstances, a person cannot import prescription drugs from other countries into the U.S. ... as substitutes for FDA-approved drugs."

The retail and entertainment intersection is happening at a time when intellectual property is becoming more valuable than ever, according to experts.

As consumers encounter more marketing than ever, the companies that will win the battle for attention are the ones that can "cut through all of the noise," said Alex Michael, senior managing director at investment firm LionTree.

"The hardest thing to find and to get is audience these days," Michael told CNBC. "IP, in that sense, is the most valuable thing out there, and only becoming more valuable because it's attached to audience."

At the same time, he said, the way IP is distributed is broadening to include video, audio, short-form, long-form and live event content. Consumers are also redefining their loyalty to brands, which means companies have to "play much earlier in the consumer journey" than they have in years past to secure their trust, he said.

The trend is also likely helped by executives who have worked in both retail and entertainment: Gap's Kaufman came from Paramount, for example, and Dickson and Skydance co-CEO Ynon Kreiz held top roles at Mattel.

"The fundamentals are still there, and if you're going to have a good retail proposition, you need to have that balance. #303. Big-ticket holiday items may be harder to find as shipping costs rise

This holiday season, big-ticket items are expected to be in short supply with diesel prices near records, trucking rates close to a post-pandemic level and carrying costs hitting multidecade highs. All of that is forcing many retailers to rethink their holiday inventory strategy.

"We are going to have some of the lowest inventory levels on some of these big-ticket items we have seen in history," said Deborah Weinswig, CEO of retail analytics firm Coresight Research.

Weinswig said retailers she is working with are holding less inventory of big-screen TVs, grills, large consumer appliances like washers and dryers, and other items that take either big amounts of space or cost a lot of money to hold.

"It's freight getting it from there to here; it's carrying costs; then it's just the trucking to get it into the stores," Weinswig said. "We're talking three different pieces right here and each is up significantly year on year."

The surge pricing for supply chain services is being caused by domestic and international policy.

According to Evercore ISI, the rates for general trucking have increased more than 47% year over year during the week of Nov. 2.

Industry experts say a large factor has been a crackdown by the federal government on non-citizen truck drivers and non-U.S.-based truck drivers operating in the U.S. That has created a capacity crunch that has raised rates and is expected to continue to create upward pressure.

At the same time, fuel surcharges are spiking.

Most transportation providers charge customers an extra fuel surcharge to offset changes in the cost of diesel fuel. That surcharge has spiked 64% year over year, according to Evercore ISI data, as diesel prices have hit new record highs due in part to the U.S.-Israel war with Iran.

And inventory holding costs — or the total expense of storing unsold goods, which could include the costs for capital, service, storage and other risk factors — are closely tied to the yield on the 10-year Treasury note, which exceeded 5% in October and remains at multiyear highs in November.

Collectively, the higher costs could mean bigger discounts for shoppers willing to wait, according to Bill Catania, CEO of logistics technology company OneRail, which powers same-day delivery for companies such as FedEx and Lowe's.

"You're going to see more discount and price promotions as we get closer to the holiday to liquidate inventory, so they're not having to transfer it or dispose of it or position it some other way," Catania said.

Retailers are also facing a conundrum in their holiday inventory strategy when it comes to shipping costs versus opportunity costs. In other words, is it worth it to stock up now, or wait it out in hopes that shipping rates will come down some but potentially miss out on having the right product mix?

It's leading to a wait-and-see approach for stocking shelves after early holiday sales in October, executives said.

UPS CEO Carol Tomé said on the company's earnings call on Oct. 27 that it's "just starting to get peak season forecasts from our customers."

Similarly, Derek Leathers, CEO of Werner Enterprises, a trucker for big-box stores and major retailers, said retailers are cautious.

"When rates are higher and the cost of capital is more expensive, you are going to have a much more intense focus on not having excess inventory," Leathers said.

Still, 2026 holiday sales are expected to top $1 trillion as inflation drives growth and customer spending is expected to remain healthy, according to Bain & Company's annual forecast. Leathers said even with the surge in trucking and shipping rates, he's seeing that retailers are willing to pay up to stock up.

"The bigger concern right now is wanting to make sure they have their capacity locked in. There is way more dialogue around the assurance of having a truck or whatever mode that needs to be secured," Leathers said. "Not saying they are price insensitive, because they are certainly always concerned about price. But you can't sell it if it's not on the shelf."

ShipMatrix, a data analytics firm for the logistics industry, forecasts 2.4 billion packages will be delivered during the 2026 holiday peak, a 4% increase year over year.

It's an increase that will likely lead retailers to increasingly use gig delivery, especially later in the holiday peak. Gig workers allow retailers and delivery companies to adjust to changes in order flow and accommodate surges of last-minute shopping without spending time and money onboarding new team members.

"Gig economy delivery is great. You know, it fulfills fast, but there's better unit economics out there," Catania said.

Industry experts said they also expect to see more retailers use drones for last-mile delivery for smaller items.

A 2024 PwC study forecast that the cost of drone delivery would "drop by more than 70% over the next 10 years, which will not only match traditional last-mile delivery costs, but surpass them and create an inflection point for the industry."

Amit Regev, CEO of drone company Flytrex, said the company is in active talks with retailers about using drones for holiday deliveries.

"The expectation now is becoming so much more instant," Regev said.

Retailers have been increasing their use of drones. Lowe's in September said it was launching a drone delivery service in partnership with DoorDash and Alphabet's Wing. Amazon earlier this year said it was aiming to offer drone deliveries in nearly 500 U.S. cities and towns by the end of the year.

Flytrex currently delivers for Walmart and a number of restaurants including Little Caesars and Papa John's.

"Customers are looking for that instant gratification," Regev said. "So if it takes too long, they might not want to order with you. And from what we are seeing that's a stronger incentive for them to try and test or deploy deliveries using drones."
