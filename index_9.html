#474. I tried Apple’s Siri Recaps. They’re surprisingly useful — and a little unsettling

Buy One Founder Summit Ticket, Get a Second for Free. Register now.

Founder Summit begins Nov. 4, 9:00 a.m. ET. Get your pass savings & deals before it starts. REGISTER HERE.

On Tuesday, Apple rolled out a potentially controversial set of Audio Intelligence features into testing with the first developer beta versions of iOS 27.3 and watchOS 27.3. The betas bring four new AI features that allow Apple Watch Series 12 wearers to detect important sounds, identify songs, catch up on conversations, and take notes.

After just a day with Apple’s Siri Recaps feature on my Apple Watch, I can already see how its value may ultimately outweigh any creepiness factor that comes from the AI-powered audio recording and summarization it provides. However, I also see areas where AI-assisted note-taking can go too far, capturing things that aren’t worth remembering, and where there’s room for improvement.

It’s the two other features, Live Rewind and Siri Recaps, that have raised concerns. The features record and then transcribe or summarize the conversations happening with and around the Apple Watch’s owner. Live Rewind lets you go back to see what was said in the past 15 seconds, while Siri Recaps summarize audio throughout your day, saving notes of the important things that were said.

Still, some are put off by the idea that we’re now normalizing the idea that technology is always listening.

I find myself sympathizing with both camps. I often lament that we’ve allowed technology to become so pervasive in our daily lives, but I still see the value in what Apple has built.

I haven’t yet had an appropriate time to test Live Rewind, nor do I think this will be a feature I use as often. I’m more likely to say “sorry, what?” or “can you repeat that?” if I missed something that was said, before turning to my Apple Watch for help.

But with Siri Recaps, I immediately saw how this could become a feature I rely on regularly to save important notes for later.

I was able to test the feature in a real-world scenario yesterday, when we were dropping off my teen’s car for repairs and picking up a rental. At both places, employees shared a lot of information about time frames, prices, and policies that were delivered verbally. The information came too fast to stop and write anything down. Yet when I returned to Siri Recaps later in the day, it had captured key details that I may need to refer back to.

For instance, it noted the deductible, the repair time frame, the amount of gas we had in the rental at the start, and other things I wanted to remember.

One complaint is that Siri Recaps had mixed this information with other topics that were later discussed in the home, like an errand we needed to run and what time babysitting was this week, something we were discussing in order to create an appointment on the family calendar with an appropriate alert time frame.

I think these items should have been saved in a separate note titled “family schedule and errands,” rather than in the same note with the car repairs, but under a separate heading.

Because I configured Siri Recaps to run at all times, it also captured non-essential things, like our “plan” to watch the midterms coverage on TV, which is not the kind of thing you’d normally jot down a note about. It also was a stark reminder that the feature was listening in on our everyday conversations. That’s something that could feel invasive in different scenarios, if the subject being discussed was more personal in nature.

This had me considering turning off the Recap feature while at home in the evening, so family dinner table chatter doesn’t become note fodder.

While I appreciated that the Recap picked up a reminder about an errand I needed to run from one of the family conversations, other tidbits it saved — like what we were watching on TV — would only be valuable if you were exporting a Siri Recap note to Apple’s Journal app, because it was something you wanted to record for personal reasons.

I’d like to see these sorts of extraneous details saved as separate notes — or at least as dedicated sections that could be exported or deleted on their own at the end of the day.

I’d also love to be able to swipe left on an individual bullet point to remove it from the Siri Recap entirely, rather than having to enter edit mode, place the cursor, and then start deleting the text I don’t need.

Another option I’d like to have would be a setting that would allow you to automatically export any saved Siri Recap (the ones you click the bookmark icon to save) to Apple’s Notes app, instead of that being an extra step.

These Recaps could go into their own folder in the Notes app for easy reference. I can understand why this option is not the default, given how many different ways people use a general-purpose app like Notes. However, it would be useful for those of us who want to keep Notes as the central starting point for all our jotted-down reminders and to-dos.

Though I wasn’t immediately warmed up to the idea that my Watch was always listening, so to speak, I see the value of this sort of automated note-taking in daily life.

#468. Sequoia hits rewind, undoing 20 years of expansion

Buy One Founder Summit Ticket, Get a Second for Free. Register now.

Founder Summit begins Nov. 4, 9:00 a.m. ET. Get your pass savings & deals before it starts. REGISTER HERE.

Sequoia Capital posted a memo to X on Wednesday morning titled “The Sequoia of Tomorrow” that’s full of surprises. The firm has raised roughly $10 billion in new funds, it says. It is also closing its London office, shutting down its Arc accelerator and its Scouts program, merging its early-stage and growth teams into one, flattening its partnership, and killing the Monday partner meeting.

The framing is forward-looking, but the messaging is very much about rewinding to an earlier time when the team was smaller and focused more narrowly on Silicon Valley.

Sequoia says as much itself. The firm describes coming out of a roughly 20-year expansionary phase during which it built multiple businesses in multiple geographies and says an unintended side effect was hierarchy. Nearly everything being undone here was built during that stretch, under a succession of stewards running from Doug Leone to Jim Goetz to Roelof Botha.

One of the most surprising developments is that the firm is killing two programs that put Sequoia closest to young founders. Scouts, which Sequoia launched around 2009 and, anecdotally, half the industry has since copied in some form, gave small checks to founders and operators to invest on the firm’s behalf; it was a way to see deals through people closer to interesting new founders than any partner could be. Jason Calacanis famously pointed Sequoia to Uber through the program. Sam Altman brought Stripe to Sequoia as a scout, too. Other early scouts included Airbnb CEO Brian Chesky and Dropbox founders Arash Ferdowsi and Drew Houston.

Arc, launched in March 2022, was Sequoia’s answer to Y Combinator: a batch accelerator for pre-seed and seed founders, running several cohorts a year for seven weeks, with admitted companies getting $500,000 to $1 million up front.

Both were bets on being early and being everywhere. Now Sequoia says it wants to put more energy behind fewer people and things.

Also gone is the firm’s London office, which Sequoia opened in 2020. Several people will leave the firm as result. “We simply don’t see the need for a local presence,” the memo says, adding that Europe will keep producing “outlier founders” and that Sequoia can support them from the Bay Area. (If founders didn’t already feel pressured to move to the Bay Area or open an office in the region, they will now.)

Luciana Lixandru, hired from Accel in 2020 as Sequoia’s first partner in Europe, says she was offered a move to San Francisco and declined. (“Europe is where my family is, and where I want to build,” she wrote on X.) She says she remains a partner and will keep her board seats while investing personally in European founders.

For European entrepreneurs, the message is going to be particularly difficult to digest, especially as it follows Andreessen Horowitz shuttering its London office last year and Coatue closing its own in 2024. Sequoia didn’t say it won’t fund Europeans, but it’s definitely reinforcing here that the center of gravity is once again in the Bay Area.

The rewind extends to people, too. The memo notes that Carl Eschenbach, the former Workday CEO, is very much back in the fold (he returned as a full-time partner in March). It also says the team has “reinvigorated the GOAT,” Doug Leone.

Though the nomenclature at venture firms is all over the place — including Sequoia — the memo makes clear there are now seven general partners who primarily own and operate Sequoia: Alfred Lin and Pat Grady — who are the firm’s co-stewards — along with Andrew Reed, Shaun Maguire, Konstantine Buhler, Sonya Huang, and David Cahn, who joined the firm in 2023.

There are other changes as a result. Jess Lee, the firm’s first female partner and an early-stage investor who oversaw its Arc program, is no longer shown on Sequoia’s investor page but rather as an operator in the role of its chief product officer. Stephanie Zhan, an investor who had joined Sequoia in 2015, has meanwhile left the firm entirely, announcing last month that she is launching her own new firm.

Sequoia rarely lets anyone go entirely. Founder Don Valentine famously kept turning up at partner meetings for roughly a decade after handing over the firm. Goetz, who stepped back from his management responsibilities in 2017, is still featured on the firm’s site. Botha is the exception. He was voted out as senior steward last fall in a surprise shake-up days after sitting down with this editor — the firm framed it as a handoff to Lin and Grady — and he completely cut ties with Sequoia this summer, around the time he joined SpaceX’s board.

The changes are sure to make waves. Still, none of them are completely out of character. Sequoia iterates routinely, including, at times, reversing out of earlier moves. Most notably, in 2023, it separated from its China and India/Southeast Asia arms, which became HongShan and Peak XV, ending a two-decade run as a global franchise under one name.

The rest of the memo is structural. It says the team has decided to dispense with its “early” and “growth” team approaches and operate as one. It also notes the Monday partner meeting — a ritual at Sequoia and at nearly every firm — is finished on the theory that founders operate around the clock and their investors should, too. (These may be going away more generally. At least, in a sit-down with TechCrunch at Disrupt last month, the entire Benchmark team suggested it now mostly uses Monday meetings to socialize.)

Sequoia acknowledges the moves come at some cost. “It is scary to break something that’s already working just fine,” the memo says, and painful to say goodbye to good people. Now to wait and see if these were the right calls.

We live in a world of accelerating change. The best founders reinvent themselves over and over again. We must do the same.

It’s been a year since our generational transition. We’ve been busy! We’re no longer evolving the Sequoia of yesterday; we are now building the Sequoia of tomorrow.

Here are a few of the changes we’ve made, and what they mean for founders.

Focus We are best at high-conviction, highly selective venture capital investing. This is our focus. We have removed much of the complexity associated with the Sequoia Capital Fund, sunset our batch accelerator (Arc) and Scouts programs, and streamlined both operating and investing teams. Putting more energy behind fewer people and things means better support for the founders we back.

One Team There is no more “Early & Growth”. There is only Sequoia. We are one team, operating under one shared mission. This should make things more seamless for founders as they go from stage to stage.

London We are closing our London office, which means several people will be leaving Sequoia. This is not about the team — they are excellent. We simply don’t see the need for a local presence. We believe that Europe will continue to produce outlier founders and companies, and that we can continue to provide world class support from our home base in the Bay Area.

Partners We are coming out of a ~20 year expansionary phase, during which we built multiple businesses in multiple geos. One unintended side effect was hierarchy; it required real management to run the operating side of our business, and this crept over into the investing team. We’ve made several internal changes that have the effect of flattening the partnership. This allows every partner to flourish, and every founder to receive the full benefit of Team Sequoia — regardless of who your lead partner happens to be.

Firepower We’ve welcomed two rising stars (Liam and Sonali) and one transcendentally talented founder & operator (Dev). We’ve reunited with a legendary leader (Carl) and reinvigorated the GOAT (Doug). We’ve elevated and empowered killer operators: Hem, Natalie, Rishi, Stacey, and Theresa. We’ve raised ~$10Bn in new funds. Talent and capital are two of the key ingredients in building legendary companies. Our talent density and capital base are stronger than ever.

24/7 There is no more Monday partner meeting cycle. Founders operate 24/7, and so do we. Founders shouldn’t have to adhere to our process — we will do our best to adhere to yours.

Some of these changes were obvious. Others less so. Many were hard. It is scary to break something that’s already working just fine, and it is painful to say goodbye to great people. We ask founders to do the right thing even when it is hard, and we are committed to doing the same.

Some things remain unchanged. Our mission is to help the daring build legendary companies, from idea to IPO and beyond. Our objective function is not scale, but quality: delivering the best possible returns to LPs, and being the best possible partner to founders.

Excellence is a moving target, so we will always be changing. But for now, this is Sequoia.

We have seven General Partners who own and operate our organization, and we are on offense: building our business so that we can support you in building yours. We are true believers, and as we enter a golden age of multiple tectonic shifts in parallel (AI, space, biology, reindustrialization, and perhaps more), we are so fired up for what’s to come. Let’s build something legendary together!

— Alfred, Andrew, David, Konstantine, Pat, Shaun, and Sonya, on behalf of Team Sequoia

#440. OpenAI is already trying to shape the 2028 election

Buy One Founder Summit Ticket, Get a Second for Free. Register now.

Founder Summit begins Nov. 4, 9:00 a.m. ET. Get your pass savings & deals before it starts. REGISTER HERE.

OpenAI’s chief global affairs officer Chris Lehane thinks the 2028 presidential election could be a defining moment for AI. And he wants politicians to start developing policies that address Americans’ concerns about the technology, rather than simply paying lip service to anti-AI sentiment and the backlash against data centers.

In a new essay, Lehane compares AI’s potential to reshape the economy to President Franklin D. Roosevelt’s New Deal, the 1930s set of federal programs and reforms enacted after industrialization and the Great Depression shattered the old economic order. He observes that Americans today feel similarly anxious about losing control over their economic futures and urges politicians to find a “compelling third path” that allows people to share in AI’s benefits. He lays out five policy priorities, spanning worker protections and education to AI safety and data center development.

The essay comes on the heels of a stunning Democratic victory in the midterm elections, in which Democrats flipped control of Congress amid widespread voter frustration over the cost of living. Kate Brennan, who leads AI and corporate power policy at the AI Now Institute, told TechCrunch that Lehane’s rhetoric marks a notable shift from OpenAI’s policy agenda last year, which focused on accelerating AI development, reducing regulatory barriers, and expanding infrastructure.

Unsurprisingly, many of Lehane’s proposals would encourage wider AI adoption and support the infrastructure expansion companies like OpenAI need to grow. The original New Deal established protections for workers and placed constraints on corporate power. It’s less clear how Lehane’s vision would hold AI companies accountable for the economic disruption their technology could create.

“Invoking a New Deal metaphor is to invoke state capacity to actually benefit the public,” Brennan said. “This is written by a private company that is trying to advocate for policies for its own benefit. It is ahistorical at best and harmful at worst.”

While AI wasn’t a dominant issue in the midterms, opposition to data centers emerged as a major bipartisan concern in several races in the midterms. And Lehane may be right that AI will become a bigger part of the conversation over the next two years.

Affordability is already shaping up as a key issue for the 2028 election as Americans grapple with inflation, tariffs, and the rising cost of living. AI could become an increasingly large part of that conversation as voters stress about data centers hiking up electricity bills, AI replacing their jobs, and technology’s economic gains flowing only to a handful of wealthy companies.

Lehane acknowledges many of these concerns, but he offers few concrete proposals to prevent AI’s gains from becoming concentrated. Instead, he largely pitches AI as a path to greater prosperity for Americans willing to embrace it.

Take his proposals for the tax code. He suggests incentives for workers to learn AI skills, easier capital access for entrepreneurs, and new approaches to employee ownership and profit-sharing. Those are not the same as binding requirements that would ensure workers share in AI-generated wealth or that companies pay the costs of displacing workers.

“I was struck by how much of this policy is for a future…that is already predicated on mass AI adoption and AI benefiting society, which we have yet to see,” Brennan said. “All of it is predicated on benefiting from AI’s upside.”

Brennan said it would make more sense to see things like a revised corporate tax rate or tax incentives for hiring human beings rather than automating jobs, as well as ways to keep companies from amassing outsize market power.

Lehane’s emphasis on expanding AI’s economic opportunities, rather than limiting potential harms, extends to data centers. He notes concerns about data centers while emphasizing the construction jobs and local tax revenue they could generate. He then warns that politicians who support a data center moratorium could jeopardize America’s lead over China, going so far as to compare that stance to the 2002 vote to authorize the Iraq war.

Lehane also makes child safety and education central to his proposed agenda, stressing the importance of age-appropriate protections and safeguards around AI companions for minors. He also warns against keeping AI out of classrooms, arguing that doing so could lead to inequality between wealthy students and “children in rural communities.” But the argument takes for granted that AI improves learning, a claim that remains unproven and one critics say deserves more examination than more funding or individualized attention from human teachers.

Lehane also calls for enforceable AI safety standards, independent evaluations, public oversight, and the ability to restrict AI systems or use a kill switch to shut them down — though recent studies show that OpenAI and other AI labs haven’t published comprehensive plans for what they would do if a model escaped human control.

Brennan called these stances “disingenuous,” pointing to OpenAI’s history of opposing legislation designed to impose such measures. In 2024, for example, OpenAI opposed California’s AI safety bill, SB 1047, which would have mandated catastrophic risk evaluations and shutdown capabilities. Last year, OpenAI also sought to limit state regulation, arguing that states could undermine American innovation. The company has more recently shifted some of those positions, backing several state safety laws and saying it would be open to independent evaluators embedded in the company.

Lehane also argues that people should maintain control over consequential decisions made using AI, rather than handing off judgment entirely to machines. But that raises the question of what human control really looks like in a society where humans are increasingly being required to use AI in education, work, healthcare, and government.

Throughout his essay, Lehane frames AI as a technology that could give Americans back their sense of agency and economic opportunity. But he ties average Americans’ “middle class” success to participation in an economy increasingly shaped by companies like OpenAI. Whether his policies will meaningfully redistribute power or subtly entrench it is less clear.

TechCrunch reached out to OpenAI for more specifics on Lehane’s proposals and the company’s stances, but it did not respond by the time of publication.

The November 3 midterm results gave Democrats full control of Congress, setting the stage for what could be one of the most divided U.S. governments this decade, with Republicans in the White House.

Tuesday’s results suggest that anger over data centers, the large facilities that house the computers powering AI, is becoming a political force. With Democrats now in control of Congress, that could lead to new limits on where data centers can be built and how much say local communities have.

Data centers became a ballot-box issue in various states. An NBC exit poll showed how concerned voters are about AI: More than half of respondents believe that AI will do more harm than good, while almost 80% are against the building of data centers in their neighborhoods. Almost 60%, meanwhile, said data centers were a crucial or major factor in their vote. Nadine Smith, president and CEO of the racial justice organization Color of Change, called Tuesday’s election a referendum on “who this economy is built for.”

“National security matters,” Smith said, referring to a common argument from data center supporters that the infrastructure is essential for the U.S. to stay ahead in war and in its rivalry with China. “And so do the families in South Memphis breathing exhaust from the gas turbines powering an AI supercomputer.” (She was referring to xAI’s data center in Memphis, Tennessee, which uses gas turbines for power.)

Voters in several states pushed back against the AI buildout, even as pro-AI super PACs spent millions hoping to sway them this midterm. The anti-data center backlash was especially felt in states like Ohio, where OpenAI has eyed a $500 billion data center, backed by more than $100 billion in financing from Nvidia. In fact, the Ohio county where Meta had planned a 700,000-square-foot facility just passed a ban on data centers, and local communities across the state overwhelmingly voted against data center buildout measures.

Analysts at Morgan Stanley predict that Democratic governor-elect Amy Acton will also pose a major risk to Ohio’s data center buildout. Elsewhere, candidates in Texas, Maine, Michigan, Nevada, Georgia, and South Carolina all won seats after taking a stance against the rapid buildout of data centers. “It is clear that money does not buy outcomes,” said Anshi Bhatt, a tech and policy researcher, adding that in modern politics, creating a “local villain” is one way to build campaign momentum.

Likely next steps include more construction moratoriums or requiring data centers to pay for their own power grid upgrades instead of passing those costs on to nearby communities. Goldman Sachs, in fact, predicts data center construction delays in states like Georgia and Texas because of Democratic gains.

Democratic control of Congress could also improve the odds for bills like the one Sen. Bernie Sanders and Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez introduced earlier this year, though any such bill would also face a possible veto. It proposes a nationwide moratorium on new data centers until Congress adds new guardrails and ensures that the buildout does not raise electricity costs, harm the environment, or proceed without community approval.

There are also bills like the GRID Savings Act, proposed by New Mexico Sen. Martin Heinrich, which would prevent grid infrastructure costs from being passed on to communities during large AI data center buildouts.

The environment is also a major concern, especially at xAI’s data center in Memphis and at the data centers throughout Virginia, known as the world capital of data centers, where gas turbines are used for power. Maia Woluchem, program director at Data & Society, a nonprofit research group, believes that changes to energy regulations, land rights, and other environmental policies could affect the data center buildout, especially as congressional Democrats look further into the Trump administration’s rollback of certain energy protections, as the New York Times reported they might.

“Regardless of federal action, I could imagine that we will see additional movement at the state and local level, given the momentum that’s been building from the ground up,” Woluchem continued, citing governor-elect Gina Hinojosa, who supports a statewide moratorium on data centers, as an example. Both Hinojosa and Gov. Greg Abbott called for some limits, but Hinojosa centered much of her campaign on opposing data centers, a notable choice in Texas, whose electrical grid has proved vulnerable over the past decade.

But the hard part is still to come. Smith, for example, is now expecting a fight, or even more executive orders, to bypass what Congress is now likely to block. “Even among Democrats, a blanket freeze is contested,” she said, referring to some Democrats’ worry that a moratorium could slow AI progress and hurt national security.

She also expects AI lobbyists to start calling lawmakers. “Democrats ran on making Big Tech pay,” Smith said. “Our job is to make sure they remember it once the cameras leave.”

The backlash runs deeper than the buildings themselves, which aren’t new. One private equity executive told me recently that people don’t necessarily hate data centers, but rather AI, and that data centers have become a stand-in for people’s fears and anxieties about the technology. “It does look like the results are pointing to a real frustration with tech power at minimum,” Woluchem said, noting that this is also a bipartisan issue.

For the past month, people have told me they are worried about data centers’ effect on the environment and about their lack of say in whether they’re built.

Amazon, for example, built a data center in Gilroy, California, without many residents knowing, by satisfying a 45-year-old zoning law. Vague or outdated zoning laws, along with requirements that local officials sign nondisclosure agreements (NDAs) barring them from discussing data center projects, have only fueled people’s outrage about the buildout. Data centers have also received billions in tax breaks, and although many tech companies have touted the benefits, such as jobs and community programs, for many people, that simply wasn’t enough.

People want to know they still have a say in what happens in their communities. Many also fear that AI buildouts will mainly enrich a few wealthy people while their communities suffer and living costs rise.

“If AI infrastructure is truly a national priority, then the companies building it can afford to pay for their own power, meet clean air standards, disclose what they use, and build with the consent of the people who live next door,” Smith said. “Families should never be subsidizing the richest corporations in history through their utility bills.”

Batteries have transformed electronics, giving us powerful phones, tablets, e-readers, and smart watches. But eventually, every battery runs flat.

Maybe not for long. One startup, RadianX, envisions a world in which portable and edge devices can cut the cord for good without running out of power. The key is a better breed of solar panels.

RadianX has developed a new type of solar material from a family known as perovskites. The material is just as good as existing solar panels outdoors, but twice as good at harvesting light indoors.

“We can harvest any kind of photon, whether it’s an outdoor or indoor photon, and keep the device running longer — or running forever,” Josh Douglas, co-founder and COO, told TechCrunch.

The indoor efficiency of RadianX’s tech could be the key to unlocking what the company calls “edge energy.” Think an e-reader that never needs charging or an IoT sensor that never needs a battery swap. But as with any new category, unexpected uses could emerge, including long-runtime robots or wearables that have been held back by limited battery power.

To fund a commercial-volume production line, RadianX recently raised $5 million in a seed round led by SOSV and Twynam, the company exclusively told TechCrunch. Other investors included One Small Planet, Sachsen Ventures, Avila VC, California Innovation Fund, quantumEDGE, Riceberg Ventures, and Alumni Ventures. Alongside the seed money, Pacific Union Technology Precision Manufacturing is pitching in $4 million of non-dilutive investment to build a dedicated production line for the startup.

It’s not an entirely new idea — solar-powered calculators have been around for decades — but it’s one that has been overlooked more recently because the power demands of modern devices have outstripped traditional solar panels’ capacity.

“It’s power density,” Vivian Qu, co-founder and CEO of RadianX, told TechCrunch. “The reason why perovskites have been so interesting is the high power density”

Perovskites are also not a new idea, though the class of materials has struggled to gain a foothold. They’re still more expensive than typical silicon-based solar panels, and they’re not quite as durable, though they’ve made strides in recent years.

But they make up for those shortcomings in power delivery — RadianX’s 1.2 meter by 0.6 meter panel, for example, harvests light with 22.33% efficiency, which the company said is the best on record.

For RadianX, durability is also less of a concern. The company has been able to hit a key industry standard for durability, and by targeting indoor devices, the company’s panels are subject to less wear-and-tear. The panels, which can be as thin as 0.7 millimeters, can be sized to fit.

To wring more power out of the panels, RadianX is also developing power electronics tailored to perovskites. Most existing electronics are tuned for silicon panels, but “silicon has a very different light soaking characteristic,” she said.

Unlike many other solar companies, RadianX isn’t planning to sell bare panels. “RadianX is trying to package all of this and sell this to device makers as something they don’t need to worry about,” Qu said.

Ultimately, Qu and Douglas hope their power packages will land in everything from e-readers to factory sensors — and maybe a few places they haven’t dreamt of yet.

The automaker's industrial free cash flow improved to negative 4 billion euros, up from negative 4.3 billion the year prior.

Stellantis on Wednesday also reconfirmed its 2026 guidance and target to achieve positive free cash flow next year, which is a main focus for investors.

"Q3 was soft, but consensus expectations were far lower. Key here is that '26 Guidance was maintained while other auto OEMs and suppliers are cutting on elevated costs," RBC Capital Markets analyst Tom Narayan said Wednesday in an investor note.

Stellantis CEO Antonio Filosa told investors Wednesday that the company is ahead of schedule on its turnaround plan, which was announced in May. "We are very confident in reaffirming our '26 guidance. Those include '27 free cash flow positive," Filosa said Wednesday in response to concerns about the carmaker's ability to achieve its free cash flow target.

Earlier this month, Filosa told CNBC that European regulators need to do more to incentivize carmakers to manufacture vehicles in the region to fend off Chinese rivals.
