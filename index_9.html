#490. Rivian, Lucid, Scout CEOs aren’t counting on Democrats to restore EV federal support after midterm wins

Leaders of several U.S. electric vehicle manufacturers said they're not counting on increased federal support for their businesses once Democrats gain control of Congress in January, but they said they'll gladly take it if it does happen.

That was the overall sentiment from CEOs of Rivian Automotive, Lucid Group and EV startup Scout Motors during interviews with CNBC after Democrats' historic midterm election wins.

"We've honestly constructed Scout from day one to be in an environment where we're not going to count on, let's say, legislation or count on things that may or may not be there," Scout CEO Scott Keogh, an automotive veteran, told CNBC on Wednesday.

EV makers have had to contend with flip-flopping support from Washington over the years. The Trump administration has ended or rolled back support for climate change initiatives and EVs that were supported by the Biden administration, including an up to $7,500 customer incentive for purchasing an EV.

Legacy automakers such as General Motors and EV startups alike had counted on such credits to help them boost their sales and assist in electric vehicle profitability. But now they don't have that same level of federal assistance.

Rivian CEO RJ Scaringe said it's his hope that the Democratic-controlled Congress and Republican President Donald Trump can find middle ground to make progress for the EV industry, but Rivian's not counting on that.

"We try not to plan for those sort of things. The reality is it's probably going to have less of an impact than we think," Scaringe said Nov. 6 following the election results. "I think we're going to actually have a lot of challenges getting things done."

Fellow EV manufacturer Lucid, which is backed by Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund, is taking a similar approach.

"You know, they say, 'Plan for the worst and … hope for the best.' That's all we can say," Lucid CEO Silvio Napoli told CNBC on Monday. "Anything positive, we'll take it."

The lack of expected support comes as Democrats seem to be more focused on hot topics such as healthcare and data centers rather than climate change and support for alternative energy technologies such as EVs.

CNBC previously reported that House Democrats plan to bring to the floor bills reinstating the enhanced health insurance subsidies that expired under the GOP-held Congress and reversing the Medicaid cuts Trump signed into law last year. They also plan to focus on oversight of the Trump administration and affordability.

Rachel McCleery, executive director of the nonprofit policy advisory Coalition for Reimagined Mobility at SAFE, said she believes affordability concerns also could include the cost of vehicles, including EVs.

"I fully expect there will be legislative debates over what to do moving forward, including on EV and EV supply chain incentives," McCleery, a former U.S. Department of Treasury senior advisor and Ford Motor policy director, said. "Bringing down the cost of vehicles for U.S. consumers is top of mind for Democratic policymakers, and electric vehicles are a critical aspect of this agenda because of the multiple challenges greater EV adoption solves for."

Scout's Keogh said if legislators decide to offer any type of support, he would prefer it to be for EV production, which could then have a trickle-down effect on vehicle pricing and help companies amid inflated costs.

"I always prefer it on the production side," Keogh said. "Anything that can drive down production cost is going to, of course, get passed along to the customer."

Affordability has been a concern for EV adoption as the vehicles typically cost more than traditional models with internal combustion engines.

Sales of EVs spiked last year ahead of the Trump administration ending the up to $7,500 incentive after September 2025, but have since come down from that high.

Cox Automotive reports the average price for a new electric vehicle as of September was more than $54,800, according to the most recent available data. That compared to the average overall industry at just over $51,100 last month.

"Ultimately, incentives can help accelerate adoption, but the industry can't build its long-term strategy around them," Stephanie Valdez Streaty, Cox director of industry insight, said Friday. "The goal has to be making EVs affordable and compelling enough that they make sense for more consumers with or without an incentive."

#489. Want to see your live AI usage on your Mac’s icons? This clever new app lets you

There’s a new way to keep an eye on your AI usage and when your limits reset: by using your Mac’s app icons. Yes, really!

Designer Michael Flarup has just launched a new Mac utility called IconSwapper, which lets you replace any app’s official icon with one of your own creation. While you can use this tool to change your icons’ appearance, design, shading, and more, one of the more interesting ways to use it is to swap the icons for AI apps like ChatGPT or Claude with versions that can display your live AI usage.

Doing so is fairly simple, and you don’t have to be technically minded to get this to work. After installing the app on your Mac, you simply choose which application you want to create a “live” icon for — meaning one that responds to a particular app.

Besides tracking AI usage in ChatGPT, Claude, and Cursor, you can also create live icons for a number of the built-in Mac apps like Messages, Mail, Safari, Finder, Music, and others like Chrome and Spotify, but these configurations tend to be more fun than they are practical. For instance, you can configure Safari’s compass to follow things like page loads and downloads, or you can make it so that the Finder guy’s eyes follow your cursor around the screen. (That one is hilarious!)

Of course, there are plenty of other ways to track your AI usage — you don’t need an icon to do that. You can track usage from your account or your harness, even from a fun little gadget. But tracking it from the icon adds a certain whimsy to your Mac desktop setup. (If your dock is crowded, note that you can also see your icon customizations from your tab bar as you switch apps.)

Beyond live icons, you can also change what an icon looks like using IconSwapper’s built-in tools.

There are a surprising number of built-in customizations that are as easy to use as clicking a few buttons or toggling a switch. IconSwapper also comes with a large library of alternative icons for every app that are already ready to use, or you can upload your own.

Flarup is based in Copenhagen, where he runs a design studio, and he has been drawn to icon design and customization for years. He’s been making his own Mac icons since the early 2000s and even wrote a book about app icons that celebrates the work of designers.

He had been working on a solution that would allow him to customize Mac icons, which had proven tricky because macOS has no official support for theming and protects its system icons. To work around this, he came up with a different technique that involves drawing directly on the icons without replacing them, allowing him to use shaders, motion, and display real data.

Flarup tells TechCrunch that his renderer runs the icon artwork through custom Metal shaders in real time.

“The breakthrough was figuring out how to send the Dock a bitmap for another running app and have it draw that frame itself,” he explained. “I spent a lot of time getting the artwork to follow the icon through all the little ways macOS moves it, from Dock magnification to the Command-Tab switcher. Once I had that working, I could make the icon react to whatever’s happening,” Flarup said.

That lets users configure icons to do all kinds of fun, new things they never could before, like make light move across a gold icon as you drag your cursor over it, or animate an app’s icon while it’s thinking.

“That opens up a lot of fun ways to make your Mac feel like yours. I’ve always felt the software we use every day could be a little more fun and a lot more personal,” he added.

The app itself is a one-time purchase of $19 as a launch promo but will later sell for $29, its website says.

#483. Elon Musk says SpaceX has a plan to eliminate cell phone dead zones

CEO Elon Musk said SpaceX has completed the design of a miniature rooftop cell tower that could provide better coverage to mobile phones and eliminate dead zones inside buildings where signals from its Starlink satellites have trouble reaching.

These mini cell towers are expected to go into production in 2027, Musk said, during an interview at the DealBook Summit this week.

“We’ve got a plan that enables ubiquitous access on Earth, including via cell phone,” Musk said, noting the company is launching its Direct-to-Cell V2 satellite constellation next year, which will increase bandwidth 40-fold. “But there is a missing piece of the puzzle here which is that if you’re indoors, especially if you have a metal roof, that blocks the signal from space. So we need something that’s terrestrial that addresses situations where people are, you know, in a building with a thick roof essentially.”

The company’s efforts initially focused on buying wireless spectrum after receiving the Federal Communications Commission’s approval in 2024 to move forward with plans to offer a direct-to-phone version of its Starlink satellite internet service, in partnership with T-Mobile. SpaceX struck a $17 billion deal with EchoStar in 2025 and agreed this year to acquire a portfolio of low-band spectrum valued at $8 billion.

Combining that wireless spectrum with mini cell towers should boost signals and end the dead zone problem, he said. According to Musk, the company has completed the design of what he described as “a micro cell tower thing that you just put on your roof, like a Starlink dish.”

Musk, who has a history of missing his own production deadlines, demurred, saying the devices were “going into production, I think, next year.”

The idea isn’t new, but it wasn’t clear until this week how much progress SpaceX had made toward developing the hardware.

SpaceX President Gwynne Shotwell said during the company’s August earnings call that it wouldn’t use big cell towers and would instead deploy small, low-power cellular base stations.

Even with this latest progress report, SpaceX appears to be still working out the details. Musk said one approach would be to bundle the small cellular base stations together with Starlink terminals.

SpaceX’s plan to build a hybrid cellular-satellite network has fueled speculation, and even predictions, that legacy telecom companies could be in danger. But Musk doesn’t think so, because he expects an entirely new class of customers to drive demand for wireless bandwidth: AI.

“I don’t think this means, like you know, AT&T, Verizon, or others go out of business or anything,” he said, adding that maybe they’ll lose some revenue. “But I actually think that the demand for bandwidth will be tremendous in the future, and a lot, and that bandwidth will be driven primarily by superintelligence and robots, and they’re going to need a lot more bandwidth than humans.”

#480. Anthropic rival says companies can (mostly) skip the pricey models

On the same day that Anthropic went public in the largest IPO on record, with investor enthusiasm for the AI lab through the roof, far away at the Slush startup conference in Helsinki, the topic du jour was open source everything.

That’s the enormous opportunity being chased by companies like Fireworks AI, as enterprises seek ways to avoid paying frontier prices for their AI needs.

Four-year-old Fireworks runs AI models for other businesses. The Bay Area-based outfit specializes in open-weight models, which anyone can download, and helps customers tune them on their own data so they run faster and cheaper than the models of Anthropic, OpenAI, and other heavyweights, and are harder for rivals to copy. Customers have included Uber, Shopify, and Notion.

Fireworks co-founder Chenyu Zhao and many others believe companies are gravitating toward platforms like his because frontier models are pricey and, in most cases, he says, unnecessary unless you need the very latest and greatest generalized intelligence. There are also growing concerns that frontier labs absorb too much of their customers’ know-how, even if only through the prompts used to get answers, a worry Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella raised in July.

Fireworks has also gotten its first black eye, having disclosed two security incidents this fall. In one, hackers reached API keys and tokens stored as secrets for a limited number of accounts; in the other, attackers breached its cloud provider, made their way into a development system, and nabbed customer data, including billing records and some datasets.

Notion says AI chat transcripts from fewer than 1% of its workspaces were exposed, but it has disconnected from Fireworks. Uber says hackers stole the names, addresses, birth dates and driver’s license numbers of about 150,000 drivers through a vendor that TechCrunch identified as Fireworks. Fireworks will have to win back the confidence of customers like them.

Fireworks, which is backed by Sequoia and Benchmark, among other firms, was assigned a $17.5 billion valuation in summer. It’s reportedly in early talks for yet another round, though Zhao declined to comment.

There’s money chasing everyone in the category, seems like. Rival companies Together AI and Baseten have announced massive rounds in recent months. On Wednesday, Palo Alto Networks said it will buy Applied Compute, which, like Fireworks, helps companies train and run custom AI models on their own data. The deal for the startup, founded just a year ago by former OpenAI researchers, is $4.5 billion, all of it in stock.

Zhao and I spoke onstage about all of it on Thursday at Slush. Here’s some of what Zhao had to say, edited for length and clarity.

You spent a decade at Google, including managing a 50-person engineering team on Google Cloud Vertex AI. Why leave when you did?

When you meet great co-founders, it’s obvious within five minutes that they’re really smart. I was sold after talking to Lin [Qiao] and Dima [Dzhulgakov]. It’s also important to find people who are genuinely nice, people you’ll enjoy working with.

How do you describe Fireworks to a CEO who has never heard of it?

I describe Fireworks as a specialized intelligence platform. OpenAI and Anthropic are building general intelligence. We use open models, which are often smaller, cheaper, and faster, and let companies add their own data to specialize them. That way you own your AI rather than being beholden to giant AI conglomerates. If you’re just using Opus or Sonnet, that’s not a differentiator.

How far behind are open models right now?

A few years ago I’d have said a year, a year and a half. Then came DeepSeek and a waterfall of fantastic open models. These days it’s at most six months behind, probably closer to three.

If the models are so close behind, why are businesses slow to switch?

Frontier models are a household brand, and before you find product-market fit, using the best model is low risk. But once you pass 10,000 or 100,000 users and you’re spending $10,000 to $100,000 a month on tokens, it’s time to think about open source.

In July, Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella warned that companies using proprietary AI models pay twice, once in money and again in know-how. Do you think that warning had a material effect?

We think we’ll live in a two-model world, with frontier and open models in parallel. You want the best intelligence for high-level planning, but you don’t need a frontier model for basic arithmetic. That’s why there’s so much interest in routing, and why people now think about cost per task, not just cost per token.

Anthropic went public today. What do you think of its valuation? There’s one scenario in which it just continues to cement its place in this sort of oligopoly; there’s another where it starts to lose traction.

On the record: I’ll probably call my broker tonight and buy a few shares. This is a long-term bet. In 10 years AI usage will be at a completely different scale, and that takes an incredible amount of capital, which is why these companies are going public.

Fireworks has had a couple of security breaches recently. What do you tell customers?

Every company is probably seeing an unprecedented frequency of attacks. For our incident, a lot of the motivation was LLM jacking: hunting through providers for API keys and secrets to sell tokens on illicit marketplaces. Our promise is zero data retention for inference traffic, and there’s no indication that has been compromised.

Notion has disconnected from Fireworks. Did you lose other customers?

I don’t think I can publicly name the specific customers that were impacted. Some, such as Notion and Cursor, have gone public already. It was a small subset of our customers that had this data impact.

How did that customer data get exposed, given your zero-retention promise?

Notion was using batch prediction, where you upload a dataset that’s stored at rest, so it’s not a zero-retention product by nature. We’re moving those services to an online flow so we keep data only as long as we need it.

Jensen Huang left Nvidia last week, and Nvidia has backed Fireworks since 2024. Does that affect you?

It’s been a blow to the industry, and we feel for Jensen and his family. But Nvidia has always been a strong supporter of open source, and I don’t see that changing. We expect it to keep investing in Nemotron [Nvidia’s family of open AI models], and we’re excited to keep partnering with them.

$104.8 billion. That’s really the amount Anthropic raised for its coffers (with a possibility of $117 billion, if the bankers buy all of their allotment of shares). In addition to selling its Class A common stock, it also sold a previously announced $26 billion worth of a special non-voting Class N stock to some of the biggest tech names in the industry.

28% stake to Amazon and Google combined. All told, at the higher IPO share price, Google wound up owning 13.4% of the company. And it has racked up hundreds of billions of other commitments that are either conditional (and may not happen) or haven’t been priced yet.

All told, the company has disclosed a ballpark of $1.05 trillion to $1.08 trillion in infrastructure commitments in its foreseeable future. This was a huge IPO. But it is a pricey business to run.

"We are going to have some of the lowest inventory levels on some of these big-ticket items we have seen in history," said Deborah Weinswig, CEO of retail analytics firm Coresight Research.

Weinswig said retailers she is working with are holding less inventory of big-screen TVs, grills, large consumer appliances like washers and dryers, and other items that take either big amounts of space or cost a lot of money to hold.

"It's freight getting it from there to here; it's carrying costs; then it's just the trucking to get it into the stores," Weinswig said. "We're talking three different pieces right here and each is up significantly year on year."

The surge pricing for supply chain services is being caused by domestic and international policy.

According to Evercore ISI, the rates for general trucking have increased more than 47% year over year during the week of Nov. 2.

Industry experts say a large factor has been a crackdown by the federal government on non-citizen truck drivers and non-U.S.-based truck drivers operating in the U.S. That has created a capacity crunch that has raised rates and is expected to continue to create upward pressure.

At the same time, fuel surcharges are spiking.

And inventory holding costs — or the total expense of storing unsold goods, which could include the costs for capital, service, storage and other risk factors — are closely tied to the yield on the 10-year Treasury note, which exceeded 5% in October and remains at multiyear highs in November.

"You're going to see more discount and price promotions as we get closer to the holiday to liquidate inventory, so they're not having to transfer it or dispose of it or position it some other way," Catania said.

Retailers are also facing a conundrum in their holiday inventory strategy when it comes to shipping costs versus opportunity costs. In other words, is it worth it to stock up now, or wait it out in hopes that shipping rates will come down some but potentially miss out on having the right product mix?

It's leading to a wait-and-see approach for stocking shelves after early holiday sales in October, executives said.

UPS CEO Carol Tomé said on the company's earnings call on Oct. 27 that it's "just starting to get peak season forecasts from our customers."

Similarly, Derek Leathers, CEO of Werner Enterprises, a trucker for big-box stores and major retailers, said retailers are cautious.

"When rates are higher and the cost of capital is more expensive, you are going to have a much more intense focus on not having excess inventory," Leathers said.

Still, 2026 holiday sales are expected to top $1 trillion as inflation drives growth and customer spending is expected to remain healthy, according to Bain & Company's annual forecast. Leathers said even with the surge in trucking and shipping rates, he's seeing that retailers are willing to pay up to stock up.

"The bigger concern right now is wanting to make sure they have their capacity locked in. "Gig economy delivery is great. You know, it fulfills fast, but there's better unit economics out there," Catania said.

#451. A transcription firm used by police, prosecutors, and tech giants exposed customer data online

A transcription company that caters to law enforcement, government departments, and Silicon Valley tech giants has resolved a security lapse after leaving a large amount of internal data, including customer transcriptions and their source materials, exposed to the web.

According to Anurag Sen, a good faith security researcher who found the data, the files were located on an Amazon-hosted (S3) cloud server that was openly listed and accessible to anyone via an easy-to-guess web address — no passwords needed.

The security lapse underscores how simple flaws or misconfigurations can result in sensitive information getting exposed online, especially as AI makes it easier to uncover weaknesses that can put companies and their data at risk.

Sen discovered the exposed data online in October and alerted the company to the issue, but did not hear back. Sen later provided details to TechCrunch in an effort to flag the incident with the company for resolution.

Sen, 30, is no stranger to TechCrunch. For the past decade, he has searched for sensitive datasets exposed online with the goal of notifying the companies and organizations that their information is open to the internet. Like many security researchers, much of his efforts are driven by a duty to secure the internet, and he describes his work as an “unpaid air-traffic controller.”

From his island home in Indonesia, Sen has uncovered sensitive U.S. military emails spilling from a compartmentalized Department of Defense cloud server; databases exposing reams of customer records; the inner workings of a country’s national license plate surveillance system; and public systems that inadvertently sent out streams of SMS codes used by people around the world to log into their online accounts.

Given Sen’s propensity for finding often highly confidential information online, we verified the source of the leak and the data, and contacted Tigerfish.

Soon after making contact, Tigerfish secured its exposed S3 cloud bucket.

“We’ve blocked all public access to the bucket you identified, including directory listings and individual files,” Tigerfish president, Adam Goldberg, said in an email. “We’ve also verified that all of our other S3 buckets are blocked to public access.”

“We’re continuing to review and strengthen our storage security more broadly,” added Goldberg.

The company said that Sen’s email disclosing the data leak was caught in its spam filter.

It’s unclear if the company has notified its customers. Tigerfish did not respond to a follow-up email.

Discoveries of exposed datasets have consumed a large part of Sen’s early cybersecurity career so far. He scours the web daily using automated scanners and specialist search engines to identify databases, cloud storage, or other sensitive information that likely shouldn’t be left unprotected online.

The modern web runs on absolute trust, he said, and operates under the notion that the world’s data is kept secure. But he described how his findings reveal the reality of how “fragile” the internet is behind the scenes.

Despite the recent focus on how AI is accelerating people’s ability to discover vulnerabilities, Sen said that many of the data leaks and exposures that he finds come down to basic human errors.

“An engineer spinning up a test cloud database on a Friday, disabling authentication to test an API, and forgetting to shut the door before heading into the weekend,” was just one example, he recounted.

Sen’s findings over the years highlight the ongoing problems caused by such security mistakes, just as the new wave of vibe-coding and AI-generated development is creating new security problems due to insecure and unchecked code deployed to the web.

Similar to Amazon’s recent efforts to make it difficult to make S3 buckets public without going through clear multistep warnings, newer vibe-coding platforms like Lovable and Supabase have also made changes to their platforms to prevent user mistakes.

“Everyone often fears shadowy hackers,” Sen said. But he argues that the real crisis is leaving sensitive systems and databases “wide open to the public internet without a password.”

#437. Super-hot rock geothermal could end data center energy woes

Geothermal startups are in a race to see who can tap the deepest, hottest rocks. The winners could power the next phase of the grid, data centers and all.

The broader markets got their first taste of enhanced geothermal earlier this year when Fervo Energy went public. But this week was all about super-hot rock geothermal.

Geothermal has become a darling among investors, tech companies, and even the Trump administration. The basic technology has been around for decades, but advances in drilling techniques borrowed from the oil and gas industry has supercharged it. The U.S. has plenty of potential, too, with up to 57 terawatts of generating capacity, more than enough to power the next wave of AI data centers.

An emerging class of geothermal energy, known as super-hot rock, could help the industry unlock much of that potential.

The secret to super-hot rock geothermal is what happens to water at extreme depths. As it gets closer to the superheated core of the Earth, both temperature and pressure rise significantly. When they get high enough, ordinary water turns into a supercritical fluid — a sort of gaseous-liquid state that can carry tremendous amounts of energy. A single super-hot rock well could generate 15 megawatts of electricity or more.

Super-hot rock geothermal is inching closer to commercial reality. Quaise Energy said on Tuesday that it had landed Google as a customer. The hyperscaler said it would pay more than $500 million to secure 50 megawatts of power from a Quaise project in central Oregon, which will tap into searing heat three miles deep.

Google is paying a premium for the amount of power Quaise will deliver, but that’s in line with the company’s strategy of getting in early with potential suppliers. For instance, it was an investor in and an early customer of Fervo.

Meanwhile, on Monday, Khosla-backed Mazama Energy announced it had figured out how to keep its drill heads cool as they tap into super-hot rocks. And last week, XGS Energy was given a grant by the Department of Energy to develop and refine materials, well designs, and models to improve reliability at depths where super-hot rock exists.

XGS is dipping its toes in the super-hot waters, while Mazama and Quaise have made it their core focus, though they’re taking very different approaches.

Quaise’s technology sounds like science fiction: Starting at about 2.5 miles deep, the company will swap out its conventional drill bit for one powered by a gyrotron, bombarding rock with millimeter-wave radiation and turning it into ash. The hope is that the technology can make drilling under super-hot conditions cost-effective, something that’s challenging with traditional metal drill bits, which wear out faster at those temperatures.

Mazama, on the other hand, thinks that traditional drill bits will work just fine if they can be kept cool.

The startup’s newly announced technology, Carbon Chill, uses carbon dioxide in place of water-based drilling “mud” to keep the bit cool. At those depths and temperatures, the carbon dioxide turns supercritical, allowing it to hold significant amounts of energy. Just above the drill bit, the CO2 flows through a valve that looks similar to a rocket nozzle. As the gas flows through the valve and expands on the other end, the expansion rapidly cools it, allowing it to absorb significant amounts of heat. The CO2 also removes drill cuttings on its trip to the surface, where it’s filtered and reused.

In both cases, Quaise and Mazama are betting that their technology could leapfrog today’s state-of-the-art commercial geothermal power plants. By going deeper and hotter, they’re hoping to get substantially more power out of each well. Given that drilling accounts for about a quarter to a third of a geothermal power plant’s total costs, drilling fewer wells could make a project more competitive.

Investors have been betting that the technologies will soon prove themselves. Quaise has raised more than $270 million from investors, including Prelude Ventures, Collaborative Fund, and Engine Ventures, with Vinod Khosla stepping in as an angel investor, according to PitchBook. Mazama has raised $171 million from investors, including Khosla Ventures, Doerr Capital, and Bill Gates.

As with any new technology, there’s some risk that the new techniques won’t pay off. But early results suggest they will — Mazama drilled its second well 80% faster than its first, and its CO2-cooling tech cut drilling time another 20%, co-founder and CEO Sriram Vasantharajan told TechCrunch.

That, plus Google’s vote of confidence in Quaise, suggests the race to super-hot rock geothermal is, well, heating up.

#423. Experts warn: Nearly everyone in AI is susceptible to a honey-trap scheme right now

Even as the fall-out for Nvidia over Jensen Huang’s alleged honey-trap scandal is still unfolding, Silicon Valley has yet to reckon with a new reality: many more people among them are at risk.

“Nobody briefs founders on counterintelligence,” Aaron Brown, a former senior CIA officer and current founder of the software company Lumbra, told TechCrunch.

That’s because historically honey traps have been associated with politicians, the military, and national intelligence — the people “behind clearances and a workforce trained to expect an approach,” from spies and other attackers.

But now many of the most valuable secrets “sit inside companies building chips, models, and infrastructure,” Brown says, adding that a spy “wants what the CEO can touch. The roadmap, the export decisions, the customer list, who gets the technology. The CEO is the shortest path to all of it.”

Huang has been accused of falling for just such a honey trap by having an affair with Alice Huang. She’s a former executive of a Singapore neocloud who’s been accused of being a Chinese spy and of smuggling billions of dollars’ worth of restricted Nvidia chips into China.

After the Huang allegations broke, jokes flooded the internet about which tech CEO will be next, but it’s not just CEOs and founders who are at risk. The WSJ reported that OpenAI started warning its employees about outsiders trying to cozy up to them in search of company secrets. Anyone with access to sensitive information — from board members to executive assistants — can be the target of a honey trap.

And typically, the attackers aren’t lovers. Friendship is actually the oldest honey-trap scheme around, Brown said.

Honey traps gain access to their targets as investors, customers, recruiters, and consultancies. Their requests look legitimate on paper and don’t need their targets to fall in love. “They need you to feel comfortable talking,” Brown said. “They last longer because nobody’s looking.”

What’s interesting, too, is the irony. So many tech founders are moving to automate and digitize so much of human life and yet remain vulnerable to the oldest bug around, a person who preys on the human need to be understood by others.

“Why fight through a firewall when you can get close to the person who can open doors, influence decisions, or make requests nobody wants to question?” Perry Carpenter, a criminologist and chief deception strategist at KnowBe4, told TechCrunch.

Once targets realize they have been compromised, the threat can escalate: attackers now have a means of blackmail.

Another irony: AI has made honey-trapping much easier and cheaper. Planning a scheme used to take months or years of research, crafting a persona, knowing the target, and executing a plan. These days, it’s easy to use AI to research a target, generate fake social profiles, fake backgrounds, even fake voice calls.

“AI can help sustain an entire fictional backstory,” Carpenter said. “The technology helps set the stage, but the deception ultimately plays out through human relationships.”

The AI industry “became a national security industry faster than its culture caught up,” Brown said.

“Part of being honey-trapped is not knowing,” he added. “It doesn’t feel like manipulation. It feels genuine, even lucky.”

Carpenter said a good first step is looking for unusual patterns. Note, for instance, “coincidental encounters that happen a little too often,” he said. There are also obvious signs, like persistent curiosity about work and colleagues, or requesting secrecy about meeting up, and pressing for introductions or information.

Bigger red flags include if someone’s requests are getting bigger, pushing the boundaries of what you usually would be uncomfortable doing. “A simple gut check,” Brown said. “Would this person still be around if you lost your job tomorrow?”

It also helps to try and understand when emotions might be influencing a decision. Carpenter suggests putting up a clear boundary between one’s personal and professional life. Within companies, it also helps to “eliminate executive exceptionalism,” he said, the idea that the rules don’t apply to people in power at the company.

Brown added that company culture plays another role, too: Are employees comfortable with raising concerns, challenging unusual requests, and escalating possible threats? “One of the most dangerous security cultures is one where nobody feels comfortable questioning the CEO,” Carpenter said.

“Founders need to take risks, and they need to explore business opportunities,” Brown said. “Paranoia doesn’t help. Recognizing you’re a target does.”

#312. Jensen Huang's — and Nvidia's — legal exposure balloons

The still-unraveling scandal of former Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang's extramarital affairs and the tech giant's potential role in its advanced semiconductor chips ending up in China present a legal minefield for Huang and Nvidia.

Federal and state prosecutors and Nvidia employees may target Huang and the company in court, where some shareholders have already taken action.

That's on top of a rapidly growing pile of probes by Republicans who control Congress now and Democrats who will have majorities in both the House and Senate come January.

"It's not going to get any better for Jensen Huang; it's going to get far worse," said Salvatore Graziano, a New York securities litigation attorney at Bernstein, Litowitz, Berger & Grossmann. His firm is looking at shareholder lawsuit opportunities against Nvidia and Huang.

[Huang] "faces the Department of Justice, the Securities and Exchange Commission and private litigation," Graziano said. "The criminal exposure is very serious. The SEC could bar him from being an officer. And the potential liability [from shareholder] lawsuits can be very large."

Huang's and Nvidia's legal exposure stems from three issues that led up to his shock resignation Nov. 12.

There's his alleged affair with suspected Chinese spy Alice Huang, who worked for Megaspeed, a Singapore-based company under investigation for diverting advanced Nvidia semiconductor chips to China in violation of U.S. export rules. That, in turn, has raised questions about whether Huang and Nvidia were aware of any diversions.

There are Huang's two other alleged affairs with Nvidia employees.

And there is the sharp drop in the company's stock price in the wake of the scandal. Nvidia's share price has fallen 19% since Nov. 8, when Huang sent a company-wide apology acknowledging extramarital affairs.

Huang last week retained the law firm Paul, Weiss and its attorneys Melinda Haag and Walter Brown to represent him. Haag is a former U.S. attorney in San Francisco, and Brown is also a former federal prosecutor.

Huang's spokesman at Trident, a crisis communications firm, declined to comment when asked if Haag and Brown are representing him in any potential criminal case and other questions about his legal exposure.

Nvidia did not respond to a request for comment. Last week, Nvidia retained the law firm Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz to conduct an independent investigation into Huang allegedly being targeted by a spy, as U.S. intelligence reportedly has concluded.

The civil liability Huang and Nvidia could face is underscored by a hearing Graziano, the securities attorney, attended Tuesday in U.S. District Court in San Francisco. A judge at the hearing approved a $175 million settlement from Seagate Technology Holdings to resolve a shareholder lawsuit alleging it concealed sales of hard-disk drives to Huawei in China in violation of export bans.

The Seagate settlement came three years after the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security — which is investigating Megaspeed's business with Nvidia — imposed a $300 million penalty on Seagate for its sales to Huawei.

Graziano said his law firm is "actively looking at securities law liability for Nvidia, Jensen Huang and other senior executives in the company given all the recent developments regarding their [potential] knowledge and facilitation of illegal chip sales to Chinese entities."

There is already a pending class-action shareholder lawsuit in San Francisco federal court against Nvidia and Huang by the Rosen Law Firm, for allegedly misleading and false statements related to Megaspeed and Alice Huang.

"Several hundreds of investors have contacted us and expressed an interest to join the case — investors of all walks of life, from mom and pops to investors with very large losses," Rosen attorney Phil Kim told CNBC in an email.

While it is "not illegal to, you know, sleep with a spy ... it is illegal to pass certain types of information to a counterintelligence officer," she said.

That sluggishness, or what labor economists call a low-hire, low-fire economy, means that fewer people are getting hired, and fewer workers are leaving their jobs — even as a growing share of job holders report frustration with their career opportunities. In 2026, 21% of workers report feeling dissatisfied with their career prospects, according to Glassdoor reviewer data. Dissatisfied workers rated their jobs at a one or two out of five stars.

A stagnant job market can discourage even the unhappiest of workers from quitting, but hiring could soon ramp up. Indeed's Job Posting Index, or JPI, which tracks the level of job postings on Indeed relative to a pre-pandemic baseline, saw year-over-year growth for the first time in September 2026 since 2022. The upward trend suggests that the job market is "heating up," Glassdoor chief economist Daniel Zhao tells CNBC Make It.

As more opportunities open up, job holders might regain the confidence to exit, turning into "a wave of quits and turnover," Zhao says. Turnover could also help those who are out of a job secure employment as roles that were once filled become vacant.

"The labor market is a bit like a coiled spring right now," Zhao said at a press event Tuesday in New York City. "There's a lot of pent-up potential energy, and it's unclear exactly which way that that spring is going to release that energy."

In a healthy job market, employee dissatisfaction can forecast turnover rates. When employee dissatisfaction reached 17% in April 2022, per Glassdoor reviews, quit rates also reached their highest levels at 3%, seasonally adjusted, according to data from Bureau of Labor Statistics Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey data published by the St. Louis Federal Reserve.

However, when workers feel anxious about their future job prospects, turnover can stall. Though workers feel dissatisfied with their jobs at higher rates in September 2026 than they did in April 2022, only 2.1% of workers quit their job in the month, according to the BLS JOLTS.

"There aren't those opportunities to climb the career ranks internally or externally," Zhao says of the job market. "There's no outlet for the frustration or dissatisfaction that [workers] might be feeling."

Employee turnover was one focus of Indeed and Glassdoor's 2027 Hiring and Worklife Trends Report, published Wednesday. Their research, which assumed a consistent relationship between worker dissatisfaction and resignations based on historical data, found that the quit rate is 0.22% lower than what employee sentiment would typically suggest. While the figure seems small, it is equivalent to an additional 349,000 quits per month.

"Workers are very hesitant to quit, especially without something lined up, because hiring is very sluggish, and long-term unemployment is looming over them," Zhao tells CNBC Make It.

While unemployment levels are stable, people are remaining jobless for longer, according to data from the BLS. Those who face long-term unemployment, job seekers who have been out of work for 27 weeks or more, accounted for 27.6% of all unemployed people in October 2026.

Even if more jobs open, turnover across sectors could vary. Industries that are highly exposed to artificial intelligence like software development, engineering and banking previously dragged Indeed's JPI down, meaning employers in these sectors posted fewer jobs. Indeed's data shows that postings in these sectors have since seen growth, and could result in what experts call "pent-up quits," which occur when dissatisfied workers leave their jobs once market conditions improve. Job posts on Indeed for employers in industries with less AI exposure like healthcare have softened, according to experts.

However, business and professional services, which includes banking, shed 27,000 jobs in October 2026, according to data from ADP Research.

"As we look ahead to 2027, that increase in demand might not necessarily translate into more hires, and it also might not be enough hires to give workers the confidence that they can actually quit their jobs and move on to something better," Zhao says.

Even as the job market appears to be trending in a positive direction, further economic pressure could continue to place a strain on employers and their hiring plans.

"Nobody was really expecting 2026 to come with the geopolitical challenges that have happened and the the impact that has had on energy prices and inflation," Zhao says. "And so there's always the outside chance that something happens in 2027 that derails the job market from the path that it's on."

The Blue Lagoon traces its origins to 1992, when Blue Lagoon Ltd. was founded to study the water in the Blue Lagoon, an open-air pool of warm, milky-blue water set in a black lava field in southwestern Iceland. It grew into a business spanning a spa, hotels and skincare made with ingredients from the lagoon.

Shares will trade under the ticker "BLUE" on one of Europe's smallest stock exchanges, Nasdaq Iceland Main Market, part of the Nasdaq Nordic group, which also operates exchanges in Sweden, Denmark and Finland.

"Wellness is the new luxury, and we know that the wellness economy is growing faster than the economy as a whole, so we have tailwind from that," CEO Sigríður Margrét Oddsdóttir told CNBC on Thursday.

"Now is the right time for us to list," she added. "We are a company that thinks long-term, we have a clear vision for the next decade, and being publicly listed gives investors the opportunity to participate in our journey."

The company's operations are centered on Iceland's Reykjanes Peninsula, where, as well as the Blue Lagoon geothermal spa, it runs the Silica Hotel, the Retreat luxury hotel and spa, and the Michelin-starred Moss restaurant.

It also operates the Kerlingarfjöll Highland Base and Hrauneyjar properties in Iceland's highlands, and sells Blue Lagoon-branded skincare products internationally.

The 34-year-old company has averaged 11% annual revenue growth over the past decade, and next year is expected to be a record, with revenue growing by more than half, Oddsdóttir said.

The company plans to use the raised capital to fund expanding its existing properties, acquiring businesses, and developing new destinations.

In total, nearly 4,000 subscriptions were received in the initial public offering, worth about 15.3 billion kronur, about 7% more than the total size of the offering, the company said, citing strong demand from smaller investors, including retail investors.

Iceland's relatively small stock market had just 27 companies on its main exchange at the end of 2025, according to Nasdaq. The market is dominated by banks, seafood companies and other domestically focused firms.

The global wellness industry reached $7.5 trillion in 2025, up 8.9% from the previous year, according to the Global Wellness Institute. Spending on thermal and mineral springs grew 10.4% over the same period, with the institute forecasting annual growth of nearly 10% for the sector through 2030.

Tourism accounted for nearly 9% of Iceland's GDP in 2025, and is growing, according to Statistics Iceland. The country welcomed around 2.3 million international visitors that year, with the U.S. as its largest source market.
