"What's different today is the depth and breadth we're taking from an innovation perspective," Hill said.

CEO Elliott Hill's turnaround strategy includes improving different parts of the business at different rates based on priority. The Nike consumer has also been under increased macroeconomic pressure as geopolitical tensions mount and inflation rises.

#410. Republicans outspent Democrats for first time in over a decade in record 2026 midterm election political ad blitz

Political advertisers spent a record $12.06 billion during the 2025-26 elections, making it the first time a midterm cycle outspent a presidential cycle, according to a report from advertising intelligence company AdImpact released Tuesday.

Total spending exceeded AdImpact's June projection by more than $460 million.

The elections also marked the first time in over a decade that Republicans have outspent Democrats on advertisements, according to AdImpact.

Despite Republicans leading spending, John Link, senior vice president of data at AdImpact, said that "Dems still led or were generally even with overall airings and impressions."

The record spending extended across the board, with every type of race reaching a new high. Senate spending hit $3.39 billion, while spending on gubernatorial elections reached $2.61 billion, spending on races in the House of Representatives grew to $2.44 billion and state legislative races totaled $602 million, all records, according to AdImpact.

The 2026 midterm cycle was largely powered by megadonors, with SpaceX and Tesla CEO Elon Musk becoming the largest contributor by funneling more than $366 million into campaigns.

The biggest donors in this year's election disproportionately favored Republicans, according to a CNBC analysis of Federal Election Commission records. Fourteen out of the 20 largest donor groups supported GOP candidates, according to that analysis.

Republicans spent $5.51 billion on political advertisements, compared with $5.11 billion spent by Democrats, according to AdImpact.

While Republican spending increased by 27% from 2024, spending by Democrats fell by 4%.

Fundraisers told CNBC in interviews that Democratic donors' hesitancy largely stems from memories of Kamala Harris' defeat in 2024, unease about the rise of democratic socialism and distrust in party leadership.

Though they were outspent, Democrats made sweeping gains against Republicans in races nationwide, taking control of both the House and the Senate and flipping seats in some of the most competitive places, including Texas and Kansas.

It is not the first time spending did not align with the winners. In 2024, President Donald Trump defeated former Vice President Harris despite Democrats outspending Republicans.

While party majorities in Congress have switched hands, the most popular medium for political ads has not changed.

Broadcast television represented the largest category for spending in this year's election, accounting for 49%, or $5.85 billion, of political ad spending, according to AdImpact. Broadcast's share of political ad dollars is up slightly from 48% in 2024.

Broadcast TV's strength comes despite facing pressure from falling viewership and increasing competition from streaming.

Industry experts have told CNBC that political advertisers continue to spend on broadcast because it reaches local markets and active voting populations. The Associated Press also reported that airtime was so expensive during the midterm elections that some candidates, including Republican Ken Paxton in Texas, bought ads during national sporting events, figuring that reaching enough constituents through a national broadcast might actually be less expensive than buying across the state's local television markets.

The fastest-growing categories in this year's cycle were connected TV, or internet-connected devices like Apple TV or Roku, with $2.66 billion, and digital, with $1.91 billion. Both mediums grew double digits from the 2024 presidential election. Spending on cable and radio declined 7% and 13%, respectively, from 2024.

#409. Why Anthropic's blockbuster IPO couldn't escape the stock market correction

No one can say the Anthropic initial public offering wasn't a success for the company. Deal size and pricing were signs of what IPO experts say was insatiable demand for "ANT" shares. But what came within a day is something that investors better get used to: anxiety in the stock market about valuations being assigned to artificial intelligence revenue and business models that should keep Anthropic shares volatile in the period ahead.

The Anthropic IPO reinforces those fears rather than running counter to them, says Avery Marquez, director of investment strategies at Renaissance Capital, which researches IPOs and manages the Renaissance IPO ETF (IPO).

"The volatility in the AI trade we've been seeing for the past few months stems from valuations and whether we are in a bubble, and that's been heightened and highlighted by the Anthropic IPO," Marquez said on "ETF Edge" on Friday.

And in the case of Anthropic, which finished trading Friday down 9%, the valuation anxiety becomes "so much more prevalent," she added, because it is a new stock.

It is not just any new stock, but one that has Wall Street adding downside risks to research notes likely never seen before, and which may be the greatest understatement (or most subtle equities research joke) in the history of earnings analysis. "Anthropic could develop AI systems which become fully autonomous and wipe out humanity which may lead to a serious decline in earnings," Seaport Research Partners analyst wrote Jay Goldberg wrote on Nov. 18.

"It was challenging company to value ... and it's very richly valued," Marquez said. "We're seeing something more reasonable and investors grappling now with where should this company actually be valued," she added.

The fact that Anthropic pulled off the deal in this market and was able to command the price it did was an achievement, according to Yuri Khodjamirian, chief investment officer at Tema ETFs, which offers multiple funds that invest in pre-IPO shares of companies and held Anthropic in its Tema Photonics & Optical ETF (LAZR) well before IPO day. "We have to remember they pulled off a very large IPO in the middle of a very severe market with the volatility in the tech sector specifically," Khodjamirian said on "ETF Edge."

Khodjamirian says the investor demand for AI remains, but amid a reassessment of returns. And he says an equally hard task has now begun for Anthropic. Investors are unwinding positions in everything from tech stocks to gold and bitcoin, and repositioning for 2027 based on their new outlooks. "We are in a bit of vacuum," he said.

"From here on the market is trying trying to figure out what direction to go in," Khodjamirian said, whether that is based on Fed policy, geopolitics, the midterm elections results, or the AI trade. "In many cases, the chief investment officers I speak to, and the clients, say 'I'm just gonna take some risk off and wait and see until I get more info.' And with the Anthropic, the revenue picture and how the AI adoption curve progresses will be a vital stat to watch," he added.

Plenty of value has already been wiped out in tech, according to Khodjamirian, but investors need new signals to buy. That doesn't just go for Anthropic but for much of the market's tech leadership. "One of the things most difficult for investors to understand right now is some of the leadership in the market that has been taken by semiconductor companies," he said, adding that the peak was 22% of S&P 500 earnings. But these companies, like Nvidia and Micron, have a very different earnings profile from the rest of the market.

The VanEck Semiconductor ETF (SMH), which was down over 10% on Friday and around 15% for the week, was on pace for its worst week since September 2001, according to CNBC data on Friday afternoon. It also lost 15% in one week of March 2020. The tech sector of the S&P 500, tracked by the State Street Technology Select Sector SPDR ETF (XLK), turned in its worst weekly performance since the Covid crash.

Khodjamirian said while these stocks may look cheap on a P/E basis right now after the heavy selling, that requires faith in the earnings estimates, which he says will be more important as results are released for the next few years. "Right now it looks cheap, but will companies like Micron and Nvidia actually deliver?"

Nvidia shares fell 14% on Friday, adding to its worst stretch of weekly performance since 2008. What's more, the single biggest trade in the options market on Friday was a near $100-million bet that the VanEck Semiconductor ETF has a long way more to fall.

And the same caution about the outlook goes for Anthropic, he said. "That's why we need to watch Anthropic's annual recurring revenue number, because it is a key part of puzzle," he said.

Marquez said there is always hype in names like SpaceX and Anthropic when they go public, and it is hard to know whether the pricing of the deal is based on fundamentals, "but we just need more time and lots price discovery," she said. "It is not going to happen overnight," she added.

As an IPO researcher, she says this "discernment" is a healthy thing for the markets. There are few cases to look to as comps for Anthropic, other than SpaceX, and before that, way back to Facebook's IPO in 2012. And neither example is one of quick recovery. SpaceX shares remain well below their first-day closing price more than five months after its IPO. Facebook (now Meta), meanwhile, "did not do well for the first year after going public and we see where it is now. It's just the nature of these disruptive tech companies that come to market," Marquez said. "They lend themselves to more volatility and a longer period of price discovery for investors."

All eyes will next be on Anthropic's rival OpenAI, which has suggested it will be ready to go public in 2027. Marquez said the reaction to the Anthropic deal may be a signal that it was "a good idea to wait, allowing themselves to build more quarters of better financials and improve fundamentals a bit before taking on a market that is really quite discerning."

Tema does not own shares of OpenAI in any of its ETFs. But, "I can't say it wont be," Khodjamirian said. "They exist out there in the secondary market, and we will look at it, but we haven't made any decision. It depends on the ETF fit and if the portfolio manager wants to invest. We are always looking out for these interesting businesses but never just for the sake of private exposure," he added.

Tema does own Databricks, Stripe, Kalshi and Polymarket across its funds. It also was an investor in SpaceX pre-IPO through its Tema Space Innovators ETF (NASA).

Khodjamirian said demand from public market investors to access these "generational businesses" will continue to be the case, despite the market volatility, and will more often come before IPO day. In fact, he pointed to the filing from the world's largest asset manager and one of the biggest ETF companies in the world, BlackRock, earlier this week. It wants to bring out a new kind of exchange-traded product that would bring the ETF and interval fund structures together as a way to increase exposure to private companies, with shares trading on an exchange while the fund makes monthly repurchase offers.

Changes in the strength and shape of the polar vortex can influence weather patterns much lower down in the atmosphere, potentially bringing in colder weather to parts of Europe and North America.

This year, however, the collapse of the stratospheric polar vortex, or SPV, is taking place at the same time as an unusually powerful El Niño, a climate event that can boost global temperatures and disrupt rainfall.

The collision of these two events raises the risk of a prolonged cold spell that could lift heating demand and force European countries to draw down already-low reserves faster. That would leave the continent more reliant on imported liquefied natural gas (LNG) at a time when supplies from the Middle East are disrupted.

To be sure, Europe's natural gas storage levels started the winter at their lowest level in more than a decade.

The International Energy Agency warned earlier this month that low European storage levels and limited storage options in several LNG-importing countries in Asia and South America could intensify the competition for cargoes and put upward pressure on prices during periods of high demand.

Simon Lee, a lecturer in atmospheric science at the U.K.'s University of St Andrews, said the combination of a sudden stratospheric warming (SSW) with an El Niño event is not necessarily unusual, but meteorologists had been expecting a polar vortex breakdown in February — not as early as November.

The early SPV disruption is an "extremely rare" event that could influence winter weather in northwest Europe and the eastern U.S., Lee said, increasing the chance of colder conditions although likely not to the extent that a polar vortex event caused parts of Niagara Falls to partially freeze in January 2014.

#368. Retirees face a looming deadline for required withdrawals — missing it could trigger a tax penalty

Every year, millions of Americans must take required withdrawals from retirement accounts by Dec. 31.

Amid the busy holiday season, it's easy to miss the year-end deadline for required minimum distributions — a mistake that can trigger a hefty IRS penalty, experts said.

"One of the most common RMD mistakes is waiting until the last minute," said certified financial planner Alex Canellopoulos, director of wealth at Vista Capital Partners in Portland, Oregon.

Most retirees must start RMDs from pretax accounts at age 73. The withdrawal is based on the previous year's account balances on Dec. 31, your age and an IRS "life expectancy factor."

The first annual RMD deadline is April 1 of the year after you turn 73, and future withdrawals are due by Dec. 31. If you wait until April 1 for the first one, you'll need two RMDs that year.

RMDs also apply to certain inherited IRAs if the original IRA owner reached RMD age before their death.

In 2023, more than 14.8 million taxpayers age 70 or older had a traditional individual retirement account, according to the latest IRS data. The average year-end market value of the accounts was about $330,000.

Deferring RMDs until the last minute raises the likelihood of mistakes, such as overlooking certain retirement accounts or making withdrawal calculation errors, Canellopoulos said.

Some 7% of Vanguard investors at RMD age missed their yearly withdrawal in 2025, up slightly from 6.7% in 2024, according to a Vanguard analysis of about 400,000 RMD-age retirees with IRA balances published in December 2025.

Among those investors, the average missed RMD was $10,500 in 2025, Vanguard told CNBC.

Investors who don't take RMDs by the annual deadline may owe an excise tax to the IRS, worth up to 25% of the amount they should have withdrawn. For example, the excise tax could be up to $2,625 for a $10,500 missed RMD.

If investors miss a required withdrawal, they could reduce the penalty to 10% if they "timely corrected" the RMD within two years and file Form 5329, according to the IRS.

In some cases, the IRS could waive the penalty entirely due to "reasonable error" if investors take "reasonable steps" to fix the shortfall, according to the agency. Investors can file Form 5329 with a letter of explanation to request the waiver.

"If you don't ask, you're not going to get it," said certified public accountant Tim Steffen, director of advanced planning at financial services firm Baird. "I've never heard of anybody being denied the waiver."

Investors who give to charity may be skipping a specific donation strategy that can reduce or eliminate their annual RMDs, according to Lawrence Pon, a CFP and CPA based in Redwood City, California.

They can use a so-called qualified charitable distribution, or QCD, Pon said.

A QCD has a dual tax benefit: It doesn't count as taxable income and may also satisfy at least part of an investor's yearly RMD, Pon said.

The move requires a direct transfer from an investor's pretax IRA to an eligible nonprofit organization. They can donate up to $111,000 in 2026. While QCD eligibility begins at age 70½, transfers won't start counting toward RMDs until an investor reaches their RMD age.

Generally, taxpayers only get a tax break for large charitable donations if they itemize deductions on their tax returns. But QCDs still provide a tax benefit for charitable gifts when investors use the standard deduction.

QCDs "provide a real benefit if you are not itemizing [tax deductions]," said Pon.

While there's no tax deduction for QCDs, the transfer won't increase a taxpayer's adjusted gross income, or AGI. Raising AGI can cause other tax consequences, such as higher premiums for Medicare Part B and Part D.

#303. I never wanted the American Dream and I felt like something was wrong with me—until I moved abroad

I got my first job at 17, working the register at an Eckerd's drugstore in Texas. My manager was only a few years older than me, and while my coworkers seemed motivated by the idea of working their way up to his position, I felt no desire to follow that path.

It all felt suffocating: the added responsibility, the longer hours, the idea that this was the ladder I was supposed to want to climb. I wanted to work, but I couldn't wrap my head around building my whole identity around a job.

I never wanted the American Dream I saw so many people around me striving to achieve. For a long time, I wondered if something was wrong with me. It wasn't until I finally made a long-term move abroad about a decade ago that I realized it was okay to want something different.

After college, I got a job in travel sales. I was good at it. Talking about travel all day suited the wanderlust I'd carried since my first trip abroad in my teens.

I continued working in sales, marketing and the odd temp role for years. But they were always just jobs. I didn't feel like they defined me or feel a calling to an industry or title the way people around me seemed to.

Of course, I wanted financial stability, but I was unwilling to sacrifice my time and well-being to get it. I just didn't connect with the version of ambition that meant grinding toward a corner office. What I wanted the most wasn't status; it was freedom, growth and new experiences.

The wanderlust eventually caught up to me, and at 26 I moved to Spain to teach English. Even though I was working, people saw it as taking a year off from my career and warned me it could damage my resume. I simply didn't care. Living for my resume had never made sense to me.

Looking back, I think being abroad offered an escape from the pressure to chase the traditional path of the American Dream. There, I felt free to enjoy life and figure out who I was without worrying about what box I needed to tick next.

I wanted to stay in Spain, but I couldn't find a visa that would let me live there long-term, so I returned to the U.S. I became a serial job hopper for almost 10 years, quitting a job every year to spend summers visiting friends in the Netherlands, the U.K. and Switzerland or solo traveling in Spain on the money I'd saved up.

On the personal side, I wasn't in a rush to get married or have kids. In my 30s, single and childfree, I felt like I was failing at adulthood, even though deep down, I didn't actually want the things I was supposedly behind on. Settling down just because everyone else was doing it never felt right to me.

And I couldn't shake the dream of living in Europe. In my 20s, people mostly assumed my desire to travel was a phase. By my 30s, the conversations had shifted and I was getting pulled aside. Friends, family and colleagues all had some version of the same message: It was time to get serious and build a life in Texas.

But my gut said to try one more time. So I did.

In 2015, on my fourth attempt, I finally made it to Spain for good. It was scary to shake things up when everyone around me was settling down, but deep down I knew at 35, this was my chance to stop trying to conform and start building a life that felt right for me.

In my new home, the pace was slower. I quickly noticed that people actually took their vacations. Strangers at a dinner party didn't immediately ask me what I did for work. Adults in their 30s, 40s and beyond were out living full social lives. Single, childfree women my age didn't feel like an anomaly.

Living abroad didn't solve everything overnight. I still had to figure out how to support myself. But having the space to define myself professionally without constant judgment changed what felt possible.

In 2017, I launched She Hit Refresh, where I help women over 30 figure out how to move to Europe and build new lives for themselves. It's not something I could have created from anywhere else, with any other experience than my own.

In the process, I've met so many others who left corporate America behind to create something different for themselves. I've seen more women asking the same questions I spent years asking myself. Burnt out, disillusioned with hustle culture, realizing that checking all the boxes of the American Dream they grew up on didn't deliver the feeling they were promised.

We're all figuring it out together; each of us writing a version of a life that didn't fit a standard template. Moving abroad helped me understand that success, for me, was never about earning more or owning more. It was always about more ease, more time, more space, more life.

For years, I thought I was behind. I felt like something was wrong with me for wanting a different kind of life. Looking back, I just hadn't found my place yet.
