#432. Jensen Huang still thinks a succession plan creates unnecessary hierarchy

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang kicked off his return to the chipmaker he co-founded by holding an all-hands meeting that covered remorse, therapy, and even the company’s code of conduct, according to accounts from multiple insiders who spoke to the media.

The topic of succession planning also came up — a particularly sticky issue seen as a key risk at Nvidia. Huang reportedly told staff that Nvidia doesn’t require succession planning because of its unique business model, CNBC reported, citing an unnamed source who was present. Surprisingly, given the events of the last two weeks, Huang argued that a succession plan would create unnecessary hierarchy.

Investors and analysts saw the issue differently earlier this month, when Huang resigned as CEO following his admission that he had extramarital affairs with at least two people, including one whom the U.S. government suspects of being a Chinese spy.

Nvidia is a flat organization, unlike other large corporations that have a swath of C-suite executives and multiple layers of management. Huang sits at the top and says he has about 60 direct reports. Nvidia has just five executive officers running the show, including Huang. The other four are CFO Colette Kress, who was interim CEO for 12 days; Ajay Puri, who heads worldwide field operations; Debora Shoquist, executive vice president of operations; and Timothy Teter, executive vice president and general counsel, according to the company’s regulatory filings.

Huang has previously defended the company’s structure in numerous interviews, including one in March with podcaster Lex Fridman.

“When you’re designing a company, you should first think about what is it that you want the company to produce,” Huang said in the interview. “You know, I see a lot of companies’ organization charts, and they all look the same. Hamburger [company] organization charts, software organization charts, and car company organization charts. They all look the same. And it doesn’t make any sense to me. You know, the goal of company is to be the machinery, the mechanism, the system that produces the output. And that output is the product that we like to create.”

Huang’s comments to staff, and his previous remarks on the topic of succession, may not be enough to assuage the board or shareholders over the long term, however.

#423. Yale’s Sonnenfeld: Nvidia is a ‘detriment’ to US industrial might — not a champion

Investors cheered Jensen Huang’s return as Nvidia’s CEO on Monday, but renowned business academic Jeffrey Sonnenfeld of Yale thinks they’re celebrating a mistake. He sees a company that is largely responsible for putting sophisticated technology in the hands of the country’s adversaries, at a moment when the risks couldn’t be higher. Far from a champion of American industry, he says, Nvidia is “a detriment to U.S. global industrial might.”

As evidence, Sonnenfeld points to research suggesting Nvidia’s most advanced chips have reached China despite U.S. export controls and to sinking shares of Anthropic, which he links to the realization that China’s seemingly miraculous open source AI models are, in a sense, copies of top U.S. labs’ work, even as Nvidia has insisted that smuggling is a “non-factor.”

Indeed, Huang’s return was greeted with just as much enthusiasm in Asia, The Washington Post reported. “The king is back!” read one headline from China’s state-affiliated Star Market Daily.

The reinstatement itself followed heavy pressure from Washington. Nvidia’s chairman had effectively ruled out Huang’s return just last week. Then, in a torrent of private weekend calls led by Vice President JD Vance, top administration officials leaned on the board to welcome Huang back, with President Trump calling the board directly to vouch for him. “I made the deal,” Trump told CNBC on Monday.

Sonnenfeld says the directors who went along, and who returned Huang to the board even as it investigates whether foreign intelligence efforts targeted him, now face a “historic level of individual liability.”

The legal deluge has already begun. As we noted a couple of weeks ago, a potential class-action lawsuit was filed almost immediately. Days later, a shareholder derivative suit followed. Filed on Nvidia’s behalf against the directors themselves, the suit alleges the board ignored red flags about Huang’s relationship with suspected Chinese spy Alice Huang.

The class action is very much aimed at making Nvidia and Huang compensate investors for their losses, while the derivative suit puts the directors’ own behavior on trial, with any recovery going to Nvidia. Three more suits followed on November 18, the day after Nvidia’s most recent earnings call; all five were filed in Northern California, where Nvidia is based.

Huang and Nvidia are also juggling a Justice Department antitrust investigation, a reported SEC probe into Nvidia’s accounting, a possible FBI review, a California subpoena, an export-control case that landed partner Megaspeed on the U.S. trade blacklist on Monday, and congressional inquiries, including a Senate Banking hearing next week at which Huang has agreed to testify.

Here’s more of what Sonnenfeld had to say, in a Q&A that’s been edited for length and clarity.

So much has happened since we last talked. Huang was ousted, he’s come back. Do you think the board’s reliance on government assurances here is defensible or an abdication of its own duties?

They failed to rely on experts. That’s one of the reasons this board is going to be sued and lose, and these directors are likely to face a historic level of individual liability. They have a very inadequate directors and officers liability policy. They have also just failed in their diligence. They hired the Wachtell [law] firm. The Wachtell firm barely got off the blocks to do an investigation. And then they have already reversed themselves, so they didn’t use the expertise needed.

It’s wild that they also put Huang right back on the board of directors.

The board showed, in addition to incompetence and collaboration, just cowardice in their capitulation because Trump has nothing to wave over them. If this was SpaceX, where 46% of its business comes from the government [you could maybe see the case for Trump’s involvement]. For Nvidia, it’s not even 1% of its business that comes from the U.S. government, so [the board] didn’t have that threat. [Trump] can call off [federal] investigations, but he can’t call off the shareholder litigation. Some are already filed, and the estimates are there’ll probably be 30 of them, and they’re going to be extremely costly.

And this is not, by the way, just a blue or red state issue. We already have 14 GOP senators who’ve already come out against this, and very prominent conservatives, like Pete Ricketts and Josh Hawley and Tom Cotton and John Cornyn. Those are some issues that may help explain why Nvidia’s stock is tumbling today.Might the board say, “We saw the classified intelligence findings, and they assure us Jensen will be absolved of wrongdoing”?

They haven’t seen those findings, and we also haven’t seen what will be the result of the Senate Intelligence Committee, the Senate Special Committee on Investigations, the Senate Banking Committee, and all their House counterparts. The board hasn’t seen any of that, and even if they saw it, which they have not yet, that still wouldn’t matter.

Do you think the Commerce Department will continue to tighten its chip export controls to keep China from accessing so much compute or walk back some of those recent moves?

We already see that Megaspeed has finally been put on the sanctions list, as it should have been before. And we have documentation now that Nvidia, rather than a champion of U.S. global industrial might, is actually a detriment to U.S. global industrial might, because their chips have now turned out to be in the hands and in the systems of not just the Chinese military, as they got channeled to Northwestern Polytechnical University, which is a military institution — this is going into weapons that will, of course, be funneled to Iran and Russia, and they didn’t have access to that a year ago. [Editor’s note: China has long denied supplying weapons to either country. Nvidia chips have been found in Russian drones, however, and the U.S. ambassador to China said in September that President Trump had discussed selling arms to China, an account Trump has denied.]

The open source models that people have celebrated and marveled at — that China somehow has this ingenuity to be creating their own chips, so that Kimi and DeepSeek and these other celebrated open source models were producing a miracle of Anthropic-like results — turns out we know why. They are pirating it. They are “distilling it” from Anthropic using high-end Nvidia chips.

Nvidia has denied reports that its most advanced chips have been smuggled into China.

There’s an Israeli company, Lava, that just three weeks ago showed [dozens of] Blackwell chips are in China. We’re seeing, with the return of Jensen Huang, Anthropic plummet. Why would that be? Of course, this is because it’s hurting OpenAI. It’s hurting Grok. It’s hurting Perplexity. It’s hurting Gemini. And you’ve seen none of these tech titans celebrate this. We have the misogynist tech bros holding locker-room-type rallies about Jensen Huang’s return, but nobody else has spoken out in favor of this. [You’re not hearing from] the CEOs of Microsoft or IBM or J.P. Morgan or Walmart or General Motors or Caterpillar. Nobody thinks that this is a good move.

You don’t think Nvidia needed Huang back?

He was not the guy designing the chips. It’s important to point out that that’s a guy named Chris Malachowsky, a co-founder who’s still there who designed these GPUs and has a whole team of next-generation people like Jonah Alben doing the chip design. As for the great software that has leveraged these chips, that’s also not Jensen Huang. That’s a guy named Ian Buck, who’s been very much celebrated in the last two weeks. He’s the one who designed the CUDA software that leverages the chips. Those people weren’t going anywhere, and wherever Jensen Huang is has nothing to do with them.

Some are trying to make Huang’s forced resignation about the extramarital affairs to which he has admitted, calling it HR policy gone wild.

A CEO who doesn’t stand by his own code of conduct makes it completely unenforceable.

Wired quoted an Nvidia employee who said they’d never take an HR training course again if Huang was reinstated.

This thing is a sexual affair with a subordinate — whether it’s coercion, or even if it truly was somehow consensual, it’s not fair. Every decision on resource allocation, on staffing, on product decisions, is not objective. It’s either favoritism, or, if the relationship fails, retribution. All the other people on that team rise and fall with a sexual relationship; it’s something they have no control over.

#420. The questions Jensen Huang and Nvidia will still have to answer about Megaspeed

Jensen Huang’s return as CEO to Nvidia hasn’t eased the scrutiny the company faces. The chipmaker still needs to address multiple inquiries into allegations that its advanced GPUs were smuggled into China by a customer, the Singapore-based data center operator Megaspeed, in violation of U.S. export rules designed to slow China’s development of frontier AI models.

The central question for investigators is what Nvidia knew about where those chips ended up and whether Huang’s affair with a Megaspeed executive gave the company reason to doubt Megaspeed’s assurances. Huang’s return doesn’t settle that. U.S. Attorney General Todd Blanche has suggested Nvidia isn’t a target of an investigation, but California’s attorney general and Congress are pressing ahead, and the Securities and Exchange Commission is reportedly investigating, too.

The U.S. government opened an investigation into Megaspeed in 2024 and requested information from Nvidia about its billions of dollars in sales to the company, concerned that some of the chips Megaspeed bought for data center projects in Malaysia were in fact being diverted to China. Singapore also began investigating similar allegations in 2025. Megaspeed has denied any wrongdoing.

Diversion of this kind would be a fairly routine issue for a company like Nvidia, whose in-demand chips are the targets of smugglers and resellers. When it comes to compliance and liability for export-control violations, Nvidia and other chip sellers need assurances that the end users of their chips are obeying the rules. However, they also cannot ignore other information that might call the reliability of those assurances into question, export-control attorneys told TechCrunch.

And that’s why Jensen Huang’s affair with Megaspeed executive Alice Huang was such a problem for Nvidia, likely even more so than her alleged role as a source for Chinese intelligence agencies. If Nvidia personnel, from lower-level employees up to the CEO, had reason to believe that statements from Megaspeed weren’t reliable, the attorneys said, that would increase the company’s legal risk.

“Government investigators likely would want to know, notwithstanding whatever formal assurances were exchanged between the parties, what anyone at Nvidia actually knew about the end use and end users of the chips,” Dan Fisher-Owens, an attorney at the firm Berliner Corcoran & Rowe, told TechCrunch last week.

The concern Nvidia’s board faced when it decided to oust Huang is that he knew more about what Megaspeed was doing with Nvidia’s products than was reflected in any of the compliance statements. The Wall Street Journal reported that Alice Huang and her husband, Kang Wei, were part of a network of businesses that funneled chips to a company in Shanghai. Kang also reportedly introduced his wife to Jensen Huang, whom he had known for a decade.

On Monday, the U.S. government added Megaspeed and its affiliates to a list of companies restricted from U.S. trade due to national security concerns. The U.S. also issued new rules last week barring large data centers that purchase U.S. chips from serving customers in China and other nations seen as problematic by the U.S. government. Nvidia, meanwhile, said it would stop selling licensed chips to China and laid out plans to hire its first compliance officer and create a new board committee focused on export controls.

Nvidia’s board said it reinstated Huang on Monday after receiving “input from senior levels of the United States government” that left it confident his return posed no national security risk. President Donald Trump later said he called into the meeting to give them that assurance.

In a news conference Tuesday, Blanche said that Nvidia was “a good corporate citizen” and suggested it is not the target of an investigation, but Blanche declined to answer questions about the government’s investigation into Megaspeed, which the Justice Department said was ongoing as recently as last week.

“When you say the export investigation is still ongoing, I don’t know what you’re talking about,” Blanche said.

Other authorities may be more demanding. Nvidia faces investigations by California Attorney General Rob Bonta and, reportedly, the SEC, both tied to its corporate disclosures. “As we said publicly in early November, we don’t expect it to be safe for Claude to build successive frontier models fully autonomously, without meaningful human supervision or understanding,” Amodei wrote.

The problem with “meaningful human supervision or understanding” is that AI is already advancing faster than engineers and safety experts can fully audit it, leaving them increasingly reliant on other AI systems to act as judge and jury. The issue compounds when AI models engage in deceptive behavior, particularly when they recognize they’re being evaluated, making them harder to trust and control.

“Certainly the products that they offer don’t differ materially in their orientation towards automation of tasks; every company seems to be releasing models more and more capable of task automation,” Amodei wrote. “Every company claims to understand there are risks and downsides of such automation and to offer safeguards.”

#368. Jensen Huang broke Nvidia’s code of conduct, and his employees may not forget it

When Jensen Huang gave his first all-hands address since returning as CEO, there was an odd emphasis on Nvidia’s code of conduct. He admitted to violating the code, then emphasized that no one was exempt from it. But that “does not mean, the moment you violate a code of conduct, we walk you out the door,” Huang said, according to Wired. “Facts, circumstances, a lot of assessments” still matter. In other words, the code applies to everyone, but breaking it doesn’t automatically cost anyone their job, including, apparently, the CEO.

It’s an awkward stance for a company whose CEO has admitted to breaking its rules. A central part of modern human resources, a code of conduct can seem like a quasi-legal rulebook that lays out unacceptable behavior. But experts say most codes are better understood as a reflection of corporate culture. When an infraction goes unpunished, the main risk isn’t legal but cultural — that employees will feel embarrassed or even angry about the company they work for. That’s the risk Huang now faces at Nvidia.

Matt Plass, chief market strategy officer at LRN, an ethics and compliance training outfit, recently surveyed more than 2,000 workers across different industries for a study on workplace codes of conduct. As Plass sees it, the biggest risk is that employees will become ashamed of their workplace.

“People coming into the workforce now, ethics is incredibly important to them,” he told TechCrunch. “They’re really mindful of the ethical standards of their company. They’re really embarrassed and angry when senior leadership lets them down.”

The consequences of violating the rules aren’t always tangible, but they’re definitely real. Plass gave one example of a company that banned corporate travel to cut costs — only to have its CEO turn up in a VIP box at the Olympics.

“For over a decade afterwards, ‘the Olympics’ was used within that company as an example of the worst thing an employee could do. People would say, ‘Yeah, I shouldn’t have done that, but it’s not like I went to the Olympics.’”

Plass says companies that remove an executive typically do so after an investigation, then try to win back employees. “In most organizations they make a change at the top based on what’s happened,” he said, “and then they will have a hearts-and-minds initiative, to go out to their employee base and their customers and their investors and everybody else, and say, we get it. We’re sorry. We’ve changed. That’s essentially what they do.”

Of course, that playbook is harder to follow at a company like Nvidia, where Huang left without a clear succession plan, and there are serious doubts about whether the company could continue to thrive without him. In cases like that, Plass acknowledges there’s no easy answer.

“I would draw a distinction there between the kind of companies where the CEO is a kind of godhead figure … where the person and the company are almost impossible to separate,” he said. “There it’s much, much harder to deal with.”

In that context, Huang’s stutter-step over the code of conduct is easier to understand. Judging by the employee campaign for his reinstatement, Huang’s return hasn’t created a morale problem so far. But as the coming months put more pressure on Huang and his colleagues, sidestepping the code could end up costing Nvidia something harder to measure — its employees’ pride in the company.

#363. FCC approves SpaceX plan to put AI data centers in space

SpaceX was given conditional approval by the Federal Communications Commission on Wednesday to deploy and operate a constellation of up to 1 million satellites for its orbital data center business, a regulatory victory for Elon Musk and his bid to move AI data centers into space.

The FCC authorization was granted despite objections from Amazon, Viasat, and the American Astronomical Society, which flagged concerns over orbital debris and congestion, radiofrequency interference, and environmental consequences. Amazon, which is building a competitor to SpaceX’s Starlink service, argued in its petition that the application lacked basic details and described it as a “lofty ambition rather than a real plan.” Despite that lack of detail, Amazon also said the proposal “risks real harm.”

The FCC dismissed some of the key concerns raised by opponents, including potential threats to scientific research, because the agency determined they fell outside its regulatory authority.

FCC Chair Brendan Carr said in a statement that the action “opens a bold new frontier for the computing infrastructure needed to power America’s leadership and growth.”

Musk thanked Carr in a post on X, writing “Much appreciate. This will be a great asset to America and the world.”

This initial regulatory approval doesn’t mean orbital data centers are imminent.

SpaceX faces a number of technical and economic challenges, including how to dramatically reduce the cost of manufacturing and launching satellites, cool processors in space, and even how to secure financing for chips that are destined for orbit.

The company must also clear other regulatory hurdles. The FCC has not yet approved SpaceX’s plans to handle orbital debris for satellites that would operate above 600 kilometers, for instance. SpaceX requested a waiver of existing FCC rules, which requires satellites ending missions below 2,000 kilometers reenter Earth’s atmosphere within five years of completing those missions. The company is interested in moving some of these retired satellites into other orbits around Earth or even around the sun, according to the FCC authorization document.

SpaceX has said it plans to deploy the satellites, known as Starmind, at altitudes ranging from 500 to 1,002 kilometers. The FCC’s approval gives SpaceX the authority to use radiofrequency communications to maintain contact with and control its orbital data centers from Earth if their primary communications systems fail. The Starmind satellites would primarily communicate using lasers to connect with other satellites, including SpaceX’s Starlink.

The FCC has given SpaceX time to sort out these issues. Under the approval, SpaceX has seven years to get its first operational satellite into orbit and 14 years to deploy the entire constellation.

#321. U.S. officially loses measles elimination status amid ongoing outbreaks

The U.S. on Tuesday lost its official designation as a measles-free nation, more than 25 years after it eliminated the highly contagious virus.

The Pan American Health Organization announced the decision, which came after an independent panel of eight scientific experts convened last week to consider the country's epidemiological evidence. The director of PAHO, the regional branch of the World Health Organization, made the final call that measles is endemic — or always present — in the U.S. following 12 months of continuous transmission.

In other words, measles is spreading regularly in the country again, with ongoing outbreaks sparking new ones.

"The commission concluded that endemic measles transmission has now been reestablished in Bolivia, Mexico and the United States, and recommended that their measles elimination status be withdrawn — a recommendation which I have accepted," Dr. Jarbas Barbosa, PAHO director, said during a news briefing on Tuesday.

It's a devastating step backward for American public health, as declining childhood vaccination rates and vaccine hesitancy fuel outbreaks across the country that have sickened thousands of people and led to a tragic string of preventable deaths. Among the deaths where vaccination status was reported, all were unvaccinated.

More than 5,400 measles cases had been reported nationwide in 2026 as of mid-November, marking the country's worst outbreak in more than three decades. States have confirmed 10 deaths associated with the disease so far in 2026: seven in Pennsylvania, one in New York and two in Wisconsin.

The commission found that out of 52 U.S. states and territories it tracked, 48 experienced measles outbreaks during the reporting period between January 2025 and January 2026, PAHO Assistant Director Dr. Rhonda Sealey-Thomas said during the briefing. But Barbosa said the countries are already taking action to control outbreaks, such as strengthening vaccination, surveillance for timely case detection and outbreak response efforts.

The U.S. in particular has ramped up vaccination efforts to close immunity gaps, particularly in areas with low immunization coverage experiencing outbreaks, said Dr. Daniel Salas, executive manager of PAHO's Special Program for Comprehensive Immunization. He said PAHO expects the U.S. to present its corrective action plan in the coming weeks, outlining strategies to regain its designation.

In a Tuesday release, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention said it will continue to ensure all communities have the resources to prevent and respond to outbreaks and is committed to working with states and other local partners to restore the U.S.'s measles elimination status.

CDC Director Dr. Erica Schwartz said in the release that the measles elimination designation "is not what protects Americans. Vaccination does." She said the agency has invested $100 million in state and local health departments to reach communities where transmission is occurring.

Last week, Schwartz told Reuters that the agency is going to be "doing the same exact thing that we've been doing today" if the U.S. loses its measles-free status.

The decision also poses a new test for the Trump administration and its Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., who has taken steps to change recommendations for the childhood measles, mumps and rubella, or MMR, vaccine and has a long history of questioning its safety without evidence.

But Kennedy, in a Tuesday X post, said he continues to recommend MMR vaccination as "the most effective way to prevent measles."

"We are working to close vaccination gaps and rebuild public trust," Kennedy said. "We will answer families' questions honestly about vaccine benefits and risks and work with trusted health care providers and community leaders to prevent measles, limit its spread, and support timely treatment."

CNBC previously reported on how the return of measles in the U.S. exposes weaknesses in the nation's public health system that raise concerns about the return of other eliminated diseases. The resurgence of measles exposes eroding trust in health institutions and growing challenges in persuading people to protect themselves, their children and their communities.

Public health experts previously told CNBC that the return of measles ultimately boils down to fewer children getting vaccinated.

The MMR vaccine is about 97% effective against measles after two doses, while a single dose is 93% effective, according to the CDC. Yet data aggregated by the CDC shows that vaccination coverage for that shot among U.S. kindergarteners fell to 92.4% in the 2025-2026 school year, which is below the 95% threshold public health officials generally recommend to prevent sustained transmission.

The American Medical Association urged the public to get vaccinated after PAHO's announcement.

"We must treat the current measles outbreaks as an urgent call to rebuild trust in science-based recommendations and restore strong, fully-funded public health infrastructure to protect families and communities and prevent the return of diseases once eliminated," AMA President Dr. Willie Underwood said in a statement.

In a separate statement, the pharmaceutical industry's largest lobbying group called on policymakers to provide "clear, accurate information about the seriousness of measles infection and the effectiveness of vaccines against it, restore and sustain high vaccination coverage in every community, strengthen vaccination efforts, and invest in disease surveillance and rapid outbreak response."

"We have the tools to eliminate measles again and what we need now is the commitment to use them," said Dr. Michael Ybarra, chief medical officer of the Pharmaceutical Research and Manufacturers of America.

Measles isn't just a public health threat — it's also a huge financial burden on the healthcare system, CNBC reported.

Based on this year's case count, a new tool developed by researchers at Johns Hopkins University estimates an economic cost of nearly $83 million for the outbreaks. That total includes $56 million in public health response costs, almost $25 million in lost productivity and more than $1.8 million in direct medical expenses.

The U.S. now joins Canada, Spain and the U.K., which all recently lost their measles-free status after a review by experts at the World Health Organization. While the U.S. dropped out of the WHO, it remains a member of PAHO, its Americas branch.

#308. FAA pauses installation of new routers after ground stop

The Federal Aviation Administration said Tuesday it would pause the installation of new routers after problems with the technology led the agency to issue a rare nationwide ground stop on Friday.

Tens of thousands of flights were affected by the ground stop, which halted departing flights for more than four hours. It was the longest halt to U.S. flight departures since 9/11.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said Sunday that an overloaded router installed in southern Arkansas as part of an upgrade to a system overseen by L3Harris Technologies appeared to overwhelm systems that feed radar and other data for air traffic control.

The Wall Street Journal first reported the news about the pause.

The FAA and Department of Transportation have said L3Harris was working to convert the communications network from copper wires to fiber optic.

Democratic members of the Senate Committee on Commerce, Science and Transportation this week asked the FAA and the Department of Transportation for clarity on what went wrong.

"Identifying the device that failed does not establish why it failed," the senators wrote in a statement. "Modernization cannot come at the expense of operational safety."

The agencies have said they are working to figure out the root cause of the issue and have reiterated that there is no indication of a cyberattack or outside threat.

The senators also asked for a briefing with the FAA, L3Harris and Juniper Networks, which is making the new devices, by Monday. They asked the companies to lay out why the operational systems lacked sufficient redundancy and answer other questions about what led to the outage.

CNBC has reached out to L3Harris and Juniper Networks for comment.

Duffy on Friday called for Congress to approve $50 billion in funding for upgrades to the aging U.S. air traffic control system, more than he has previously asked for. Democratic lawmakers pushed back on the request, citing unspent money from existing funding, which includes Congress' approval last year of $12.5 billion for airport and air traffic control improvements over five years.

Senate Commerce Chair Ted Cruz, R-Texas, said Tuesday that he thinks there will be support for air traffic control modernization, but that the allocation should be specifically for safety upgrades.

"I don't think the full $50 billion will be funded," he said. "Some of that is not safety related, but we just discussed there, and what I expressed is that the request needs to be focused on safety modernization."

Duffy dismissed the idea that the router issue had to do with the introduction of artificial intelligence tools and said it was "completely unfounded" because the AI system is not in communication with the defective router. He added that the FBI is helping to investigate the root cause of the issue.

Aging equipment and a shortage of air traffic controllers have for years angered airline executives, who have complained about the cost of resulting disruptions.

#295. Booking Holdings creates new enterprise division as AI reshapes travel planning

Booking Holdings is bringing together two of its business-to-business travel operations in a new division it hopes will be transformational, betting that a larger, coordinated partner platform can generate growth as artificial intelligence changes how travelers discover and book trips.

The new enterprise unit, dubbed Booking Partner Division, will combine Booking.com Strategic Partnerships with Rocket Travel by Agoda, and is set to begin operating in January. Agoda CEO Omri Morgenshtern will lead the unit, which aims to help businesses such as banks, airlines and online travel companies sell trips or build branded experiences for their customers.

"We realize that if we focus our efforts both on the product side, but also on the go-to-market side, then we can potentially grow faster and serve better partners," Morgenshtern told CNBC in an exclusive interview.

He said existing partners will find new and better products, which will also help grow the partner base.

The move gives Booking a single organization to coordinate technology, travel supply and commercial expertise across business relationships. The company says partners will be able to use its global supply and technology through white-label platforms, integrated applications, branded solutions and hotel-chain rate distribution.

The portfolio includes more than 4.7 million properties, 33 million accommodation listings, more than 500 airlines and more than 200 car rental providers, according to the company.

Competitor Expedia Group has seen notable growth by prioritizing its enterprise efforts, including a strategy launched in 2022 to make its technology platform available to business partners looking to add travel to their own offerings.

From 2018 to 2023, Expedia’s business-to-business revenue grew at a 16% compound annual rate, while adjusted earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, or EBITDA, for the division grew at a 28% rate, the company said in 2024.

In 2025, Expedia’s B2B revenue rose 18% to $4.8 billion, and its segment adjusted EBITDA increased 22% to $1.26 billion. Those figures represented about a third of Expedia’s total revenue and more than a third of its adjusted EBITDA for the full year.

Morgenshtern declined to say how much the new division is expected to contribute to Booking Holdings' overall revenue and profit, but he said the opportunity for growth is immense, estimating the total addressable market at $2 trillion.

Online travel agencies have been pressured this year as investors scrutinize how AI will change the way customers search for and then book their travel.

Companies that offer lodging, experiences and other travel and leisure are grappling with how to stay visible if AI tools become the new starting point for planning and reservations.

Disney CEO Josh D’Amaro said on the company’s earnings call Tuesday that guests already use AI to discover and plan Disney trips, and that some are using the tools to book.

“Ultimately, we want Disney to show up in those tools,” he said, adding that Disney is exploring ways to let guests make reservations directly from them.

Booking anticipates offering partners AI capabilities that draw on the company's existing travel know-how while minimizing token costs, it said.

"I see a lot of AI demos," Morgenshtern said, drawing a distinction between a product that caters to a few customers and one that's scalable and works even when overloaded by travel emergencies. He said he's "super optimistic" about the bespoke product — a vertical agent — that Booking will offer to its partners.
