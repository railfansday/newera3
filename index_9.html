Security researchers have discovered hundreds of copies of a Chinese-made AI hacking tool publicly exposed to the internet for anyone to access, potentially allowing outsiders to launch cyberattacks against unsuspecting targets.

The hacking software, called Artex, is an open source autonomous penetration testing tool, meaning software that automatically probes computer systems for weaknesses, and it’s been linked to several cyberattacks since its public release in July. As open source software, it’s free for anyone to download and run on their own servers, making it a popular tool for those who cannot access Anthropic or OpenAI’s most advanced cybersecurity AI models for discovering security vulnerabilities. Microsoft has previously described Artex as a system of AI agents that can conduct cyberattacks “from reconnaissance through data exfiltration with little needed human interaction.”

In a blog post, cybersecurity firm Censys, which scans the internet for exposed or vulnerable systems, said that it identified more than 800 distinct deployments of Artex that were accessible from the web without needing a password to log in.

Silas Cutler, principal security researcher at Censys who wrote the blog post, said that not every instance of Artex is operated maliciously and many are used for authorized testers. But it’s unclear why hundreds of these Artex systems are exposed to the web, as the software does not require them to be accessible from the internet.

The findings underscore how even powerful hacking tools can be misconfigured to be publicly accessible by anyone, increasing the chance that someone will find and misuse them. The publicly accessible systems also expose attack tools, such as implants (hidden malicious software) used to compromise and maintain persistent access to a victim’s network.

Security companies have linked the use of Artex to several broad-scale cyberattacks, including against the Korean finance industry in September. Cybersecurity giant CrowdStrike attributed the attacks to a Chinese-speaking hacker who used an Artex instance in the attacks, in part because the hacker had left an “open directory” hosting the system. Censys said that its data showed the Artex instance in question had been publicly exposed for months, and appeared to contain more attack data that extended “well beyond South Korean banks,” including payment and gaming companies.

Artex was also used in an attack on IT support giant ConnectWise earlier in October, which allowed Chinese-linked hackers to spy on thousands of organizations whose data and files are hosted in Microsoft’s cloud. Microsoft, which has visibility into the attack, said in a blog post that the hackers used Artex to break into customers’ cloud environments and steal their data at such speed that defenders had “little time to detect and respond.”

Many of the attacks linked to Artex involved exploiting simple vulnerabilities, such as guessing weak or known passwords, and a lack of multi-factor authentication.

The findings show that simple vulnerabilities, including misconfigurations, can expose both the attacker and their targets.

Artex has since announced it would go closed-source, effectively limiting further releases for paid customers. There are still dozens of copies of Artex’s code available on code-sharing sites that can still be used in active attacks.

#444. From the front row to the hot seat — what the midterms mean for Big Tech

Buy One Founder Summit Ticket, Get a Second for Free. Register now.

Founder Summit begins Nov. 4, 9:00 a.m. ET. Get your pass savings & deals before it starts. REGISTER HERE.

Right before the midterm elections, OpenAI hired Jessica Schumer, daughter of Democratic Sen. Chuck Schumer. The company also said it would start calling its data centers “compute campuses.”

For the past two years, many of Silicon Valley’s biggest names have built closer ties with President Donald Trump and his administration. Several attended White House events, some companies scaled back diversity, equity and inclusion programs, and some executives donated millions to super PACs supporting his campaign and allied candidates. Administration policies also benefited the industry, including tax breaks, the pausing of some federal investigations, and looser AI regulation. Meanwhile, Meta faced lawsuits and billion-dollar fines, and OpenAI faced suits alleging its technology harmed users.

Now, with the midterms shifting the balance of power in Washington, those same companies are working to rebuild ties with Democrats, who have pledged to tighten oversight of AI. How far that gets them may depend less on image-polishing than on where the real fights are happening: in state capitols, in communities deciding whether to host data centers, and, as we’ve seen this past week, in the markets watching the AI boom.

J.B. Branch, director of federal AI governance and technology policy at Public Citizen, a nonprofit advocacy group that describes itself as nonpartisan, told me that the tech industry “realizes they have an optics problem.” He characterized moves like the Schumer hire and the rebranding as “PR washing.” He expects more of it, too, pointing to OpenAI’s partnership with the American Federation of Teachers, announced last year, as well as Anthropic’s newly launched Claude Corps and Meta’s America’s Workforce Academy. “I would imagine more of that sort of investment in these corporate, private-public partnerships,” he said.

These initiatives didn’t spring up overnight. Meta and other tech companies reportedly began looking for ways to strengthen their ties to Democrats as Democratic gains in the midterms looked more likely. Reached for comment about the midterm elections’ impact on Meta, a company spokesperson pointed TechCrunch to remarks that Meta President Dina Powell McCormick made this week at WSJ Tech. “You have to work with the government that the American people elect,” McCormick had said. “I have seen it across the board that you have to be bipartisan, and you want to be, because you want buy-in on the things that you’re trying to do.”

Not everyone expects them to work. Alice Marwick, director of research at the tech policy nonprofit Data & Society, told me she expects Big Tech to try to pivot back toward the middle, “if not the left. But it’s a little too late,” she said. “Tech companies should be asking themselves how their products can make people’s lives better, because right now, it doesn’t seem like they are.”

If Democrats follow through on pledges to tighten oversight of AI and Big Tech, the industry could face a tougher environment. Data center moratoriums are gaining traction, and a wave of AI regulation bills awaits drafting. At the same event, Powell McCormick said Meta wants to show that communities welcoming its investments will see “an enormous amount of benefit,” while those that don’t want them “will of course have the right to stop them.”

Still, the next two years could bring political gridlock in Washington. Aryan Ranjan, founder of Baraza, a political campaign management software company, told me most action will likely happen at the state level, since states have already sued tech companies and passed regulations. “A coalition built on newer, working-class voters is likely to push Democrats to frame AI around electricity bills, data-center siting and jobs rather than abstract AI safety,” he said of what to expect at the federal level.

Last December, the Trump administration signed an executive order creating the AI Litigation Task Force, a federal group meant to challenge state AI laws. The administration has argued that U.S. AI companies “must be free to innovate without cumbersome regulations.” Many states have nonetheless passed their own laws, including measures on chatbots and deepfakes.

It also remains to be seen how President Trump will respond, including whether he’ll sign more AI executive orders or veto bills that could be seen as anti-AI. Clearly, voters want stronger guardrails around the technology.

In a statement to TechCrunch, a White House official said: “American leadership in super intelligence is essential for our national security, economic prosperity, and the quality of life of the American people. President Trump is committed to ensuring America continues to lead the world in super intelligence while protecting American consumers. As the President has said, he’s willing to work with anyone to continue delivering victories for hardworking Americans.”

Branch argued that Trump’s political fortunes are now tied to the AI industry. On Wednesday, the day after the midterms, a trillion-dollar market sell-off spooked investors, some are whom are reportedly now reconsidering Anthropic’s reported trillion-dollar IPO. “There’s a very strong likelihood that there will be an AI bubble pop, and it’ll happen on Trump’s hands,” Branch said, predicting a possible market collapse. “Does he want to sink or swim along with the AI industry?”

For now, the rules companies face may vary by location. “For the next two years, a company’s AI rules may depend more on where it operates rather than on anything Washington passes,” Ranjan said.

That’s partly because 17 years ago, Sequoia pioneered the scouts model, in which select founders and operators write checks on the firm’s behalf. Amid increased competition for deal flow, many VC firms followed suit. But a consensus now seems to be emerging that the model has reached its limits in its current form.

Not everyone was sold on the model in the first place. “They pioneered the scout program but [to be honest] it never made sense to me!” VC Sheel Mohnot wrote on X this week. “The scouts themselves don’t really care that much about the investments, you can still miss (like they did with Uber), not sure what it gets you. To be clear, Sequoia didn’t make a case against scouts in the memo. Instead, it framed its decision as part of a broader need for more focus. But its goal of “putting more energy behind fewer people and things” is likely to resonate with others who’ve questioned the ‘spray and pray’ approach to scouting.

Rival firm Andreessen Horowitz (a16z), for instance, rolled out a global scout network aimed at spotting opportunities in the U.S. and also in smaller European hubs. But a16z partner Gabriel Vasquez told TechCrunch earlier this year that the firm had revamped its program to double down “on the best people.”

Under this new approach, Vasquez explained, scouts get more resources but are also expected to be more active, such as by co-hosting dinners with the firm. “They get more budget, but we have reduced the number,” he said.

This also seems to indicate that it is VC firms that have been driving an overhaul of the model. For founders, being a scout is attractive: it brings them closer to firms they may want checks from someday, or even a partner role with, and being seen as someone who can write a check can raise their standing among other founders.

Some scouts will undoubtedly mourn the end of Sequoia’s program. Among them is Station F boss Roxanne Varza, who wrote on LinkedIn that she was “sad” to hear that the program was getting cut off, and “hope[d] that other funds [w]on’t follow suit.”

Varza, a former Sequoia scout from 2020 to 2025, told TechCrunch that she had no information beyond what has been shared publicly, but observed that becoming a scout had often been a stepping stone for aspiring investment managers.

“I cannot name all of the incredible scouts that have gone on to join or build funds, but there are quite a few. And the scout programs may have helped them get there — allowed them to build an investment track record, allowed them to build relationships with various funds directly, and more,” she wrote to TechCrunch.

According to Varza, the program’s impact went beyond the individual level, instead helping the entire ecosystem become more mature. “I believe they have helped to foster the investment culture and early-stage investment landscape that we now see in Europe, for example,” Varza said.

While others may agree with Varza about the role these programs have played, they may still have run their course for two reasons. First, seasoned operators may get more financial upside as angels. When it’s their own money, they get to keep the returns, too. Second, leading VC firms are now big and well-known enough that the best founders tend to come to them, so they worry less about missing deals, except at the very earliest stages or in niches and geographies where they have less reach.

Still, the pull of those early and niche deals is why scouts programs are much more likely to get revamped than to disappear entirely. “While Sequoia may be cutting the program, venture partner-style deals are likely to continue,” said Varza in her email to TechCrunch. “Scouts was just a way to scale the format. #408. He arrested a hacker, then spent 18 months clearing his own name. Now he’s a cybersecurity VC.

Every time venture capitalists write a check, they’re secretly running numbers in their heads about different exit scenarios. It’s their job as fiduciaries. But nearly all will tell you (publicly, anyway) that every portfolio company is headed for an IPO.

Stephen Ward, the co-founder of Brightmind Partners, a two-year-old cybersecurity fund in New York, says otherwise outright. “You have to sell on the way up,” he says.

Ward’s unvarnished pragmatism is one reason he stands out. Another is the size of his firm’s debut fund late last year: a whopping $250 million fund, which he closed with co-founder Gur Talpaz. A third reason is his résumé, which includes a stint with the Coast Guard, nine years in the Secret Service, and CISO jobs at TIAA and Home Depot before he ever thought about investing.

One proof point that suggests the combination is working is SGNL, the identity security startup CrowdStrike agreed in January to buy for $740 million, 11 months after Brightmind led its Series A.

Now Ward is raising a second fund, targeting roughly the same size as the first one. This time, though, he’ll be doing it without Talpaz, who left in April along with another early colleague, leaving Ward as the firm’s sole general partner. He’s betting on himself at a moment when early-stage cybersecurity startups are raising more money than later-stage rivals for the first time in more than a decade.

His unemotional approach to exits traces back to an unlikely career. Ward says he grew up in northwest New Jersey between two farms, in a family that couldn’t afford college for his older brothers. After high school, he joined the Coast Guard, inspired by a commercial of a man jumping out of a helicopter, though he says he barely knew how to swim at the time. He spent four years in search and rescue.

Afterward, he studied criminal justice at Seton Hall in New Jersey and thought half-heartedly about law school. “I didn’t know what I wanted to do,” Ward told me last week over Zoom, wearing a pale hooded sweatshirt and looking more like a basketball coach than a venture capitalist. “I was kind of lost. I missed the military and the camaraderie and all of that.”

After graduating in 1998, he says, he mailed out some 300 résumés but no one responded. A Seton Hall professor who was also a chaplain to Secret Service personnel, as well as to members of the FBI and CIA, began introducing him around, and the Secret Service called first.

The Secret Service has two primary missions: protecting people and investigating financial crimes. Ward gravitated to investigations, then to “cyber” after the September 11 attacks in 2001. That year, he married a former college classmate, an American-born Syrian Muslim. Colleagues warned him the background check would be “miserable.” (They’re still happily married.)

What came next took Ward’s life down an unexpected path. Stationed in New York, he arrested a hacker who’d pulled off the largest credit card data theft of that era. Instead of charging him, however, Ward flipped him, turning him into an informant and running him for about three years. But the hacker kept hacking behind Ward’s back, and after he was caught again, he told the agency, as a diversionary tactic, that Ward was in on it.

By that time, Ward had left for the private sector, and he ultimately spent roughly 18 months clearing his name. “It was hard to get out of bed,” he said of that period, describing the investigation as “almost like a spouse cheating on you,” given his love of the agency. The hacker ultimately received a 20-year sentence, and Audible bought the rights to the story, turning it into an eight-episode podcast called “Operation Firewall.”

Ward’s corporate career has been a success by most measures, though he describes it as a series of jumps he really wasn’t qualified to make. His first private-sector job at J.P. Morgan, where he was recruited in 2007 to build a cybersecurity unit, was the hardest transition of his career, he says. He felt like “the dumbest person in the room,” missed his Secret Service crew, and twice thought about going back, unaware he was still under investigation. He stuck it out and now calls the investment bank the “best management school.”

Six years as CISO at the financial services company TIAA followed, and then two years at Home Depot starting in 2019. But in 2021, feeling burned out, he quit without anything lined up for the first time since age 16.

That chapter didn’t last long. The founders of Wiz, whose security software Ward had used at Home Depot, put him on a short list for a job at their company. Jeff Horing, a venture capitalist at Insight Partners, which had co-led Wiz’s $100 million Series A round in late 2020, interviewed Ward for the role, then offered him one at Insight instead.

It’s a good-news, bad-news story, given that Wiz was acquired last year by Google for $32 billion. When I asked him about it last week, Ward observed that the friend who took the Wiz job “made life-changing money,” but said it doesn’t bother him.

Regardless, he spent just three years at Insight, where he says he worked on Wiz, Abnormal Security, and Elisity, which IonQ agreed in October to buy for up to $1.3 billion. Then, at age 49, he thought about striking out on his own for the first time.

He says people told him it was a young man’s game. He left anyway and began cold-emailing limited partners. Ultimately, fewer than 20 LPs filled the fund, including Horing.

Ward started Brightmind in 2024 with Talpaz, a CrowdStrike corporate development executive whose deals overlapped with Ward’s during his time at Insight. Tayler Sipperly, another CrowdStrike corporate development alum, was the firm’s third employee.

The partnership ended this past spring. Asked what happened, Ward, using carefully worded legalese, cited “differences in opinions regarding operations and the future of Brightmind.” In August, Talpaz and Sipperly co-founded a new venture firm with Elia Zaitsev, CrowdStrike’s global CTO for the past three years. Called Cognition, it’s now reportedly targeting a $170 million first fund to back cybersecurity startups.

Sipperly says that he and Talpaz “continue to support the existing Brightmind portfolio companies as well as its limited partners” and that they “remain on the board of a number of the Brightmind portfolio companies.”

Brightmind’s first fund has made 14 investments to date, with one exit: that SNGL sale.

Some investors asked why Brightmind didn’t ride out that bet even longer. But Ward says he’s seen enough across his career that he feels confident in warning founders when they’re “going to race past the turn.”

In cybersecurity, he says, the best sales come between a startup’s third and seventh years. Indeed, he says his team tracks every security acquisition and what buyers are building versus buying. If Palo Alto Networks is likely to buy something, he says, CrowdStrike will experience “FOMO, then Zscaler is going to look over and go, ‘What did they just do? I should probably do the same thing.’”

Understanding how the pieces fit together is the trick, Ward explained, offering some hypotheticals. If CrowdStrike is building a product, he said, it’s unlikely to buy a portfolio company that makes something similar. Palo Alto Networks might love a company, but not if buying it would force its customers to switch away from something it bought five years ago.

“It’s very easy to go, ‘Good match,’ ‘terrible match,’” he said. “And that’s really what we spend a ton of time figuring out.”

He has timing on his side, at least. Early-stage cybersecurity startups are outraising their later-stage rivals for the first time in more than a decade, according to PitchBook, which counts $4.7 billion worth of seed-through-Series B deals through September.

That total is already ahead of the entirety of 2025, even though the number of deals fell to 248 from 358 last year. Seed rounds in particular are ballooning, per PitchBook. The average cybersecurity seed round jumped to $8.4 million in the first nine months of this year, from $5.54 million a year earlier.

Ward questions how much of that money is really going to AI security. By PitchBook’s count, 278 deals in the first half of 2026 were bucketed as such, but his team’s manual review found only 30 true ones. He doesn’t think Anthropic or OpenAI will “take over the security world,” but instead will do “just enough.”

For real-time protection, which he terms “zero second,” he favors cyber-specific models that run locally on a customer’s own systems, like that of Empirical Security. He also looks for startups that could trounce established companies that he considers vulnerable, name-checking 27-year-old Qualys and 24-year-old Tenable.

Ward says Brightmind’s second fund won’t be entirely cybersecurity, focusing about 20% on AI infrastructure deals. He expects a meaningful number of teams to come from Israel, too. (Roughly 40% of the debut fund’s startups are based in Tel Aviv.)

Asked about the founders he’s most comfortable backing, the response is immediate: second-time founders who stick together.

Brightmind is comprised of five people right now: COO Iva Messy, also a Seton Hall graduate, came from venture firms Felicis and Andreessen Horowitz (a16z) on the recommendation of Ward’s cousin, Carl Eschenbach, a former Workday CEO who is now back at Sequoia. Sven Wollschlaeger, a Midwesterner whom Ward calls one of the hardest-working people he’s known, spent three years alongside Ward at Insight and is a principal at Brightmind.

CTO Jamie Mneimneh, whom Ward first hired at Home Depot as a distinguished engineer, is the most technical person of the team. He built the cloud computing environment on AWS where the firm tests startups’ products. Mneimneh also built the AI agents that now source most of Brightmind’s deal flow, which is how the firm avoids hiring a bench of analysts and keeps management fees down, says Ward.

Taylor Cleveland, Ward’s chief of staff at Home Depot, joined the firm a few months ago.

For all the career changes, Ward says the real force driving him hasn’t changed. He grew up without money and still worries it could happen again, saying he’s being paid to produce a “3x net fund,” and he’s “not going to celebrate until we get that.”

Said Ward: “I don’t know what’s wrong with me. I have a level of being very naive to like, not being qualified to do something. I’ve just always looked at people and been like, ‘Okay, you know more than me today, but tomorrow, it’s going to be a little less, and the day after that, it’s going to be less, and eventually, I’m going to catch you and surpass you, and I’m going to know more than you.’ That’s how competitive I am.”

#394. Coordinated disturbances haunt grids worldwide. Here’s how researchers solved the mystery.

Buy One Founder Summit Ticket, Get a Second for Free. Register now.

Founder Summit begins Nov. 4, 9:00 a.m. ET. Get your pass savings & deals before it starts. REGISTER HERE.

For years, mysterious electrical anomalies have been flummoxing grid managers around the world. Every 60 seconds, the frequency on the grid would drop a tiny bit.

Even weirder: The sags happened at the exact same time on power grids across countries and continents.

It was a mystery that Dirk Witthaut, a researcher at Forschungszentrum Jülich in Germany, couldn’t pass up.

Germany’s largest transmission system operator, Tennet Germany, brought the puzzle to Witthaut, and it took years of investigative and modeling work to determine the cause. From Europe to Australia, the U.S. to Malaysia, the same pattern shows up in the data.

Such frequency drops aren’t unheard of, but they are troubling. And the regularity with which they have been occurring can make the phenomenon feel even more ominous.

The grid’s orderly operation relies on the frequency being kept within a certain range. If the oscillations get get too large, or if grid operators don’t know the cause and respond improperly, they can lead to blackouts. Even if the sags don’t cause blackouts, they can damage equipment in power plants if they go unchecked, especially in hydropower turbines.

Hydropower plants have historically been a main driver of so-called ultra-low frequency oscillations (UFLOs). Every turbine vibrates, and if those vibrations occur with a certain regularity, known as the resonant frequency, they can magnify, sometimes to dangerous levels. Think of a wine glass breaking before an opera singer’s mighty pipes — that’s resonance.

So Witthaut and his team started investigating hydropower, the most obvious potential culprit. They used a computer model of a hydropower plant control system on the Scandinavian grid to test the hypothesis.

While they managed to find conditions that could generate a resonance that peaked once per minute, they couldn’t explain why it persisted for so long. Typically, resonances in mechanical systems will come and go, and so will the accompanying signal on the electrical grid. But this frequency drop kept happening minute after minute after minute. Every 60 seconds, without fail.

It then dawned on the team that anything operating with such clockwork required, well, a clock. How else could they explain the uncanny timing?

To that hypothesis, the researchers started close to home, installing more than 80 smart power quality meters around the Jülich campus. The research site is outfitted with medium- and low-voltage distribution lines, transformers, solar panels, and battery energy storage systems that serve as uninterruptible power supplies (UPS). It also has a high-performance computer system as well as the usual office computers powered by much smaller UPS devices. The power quality meters sampled a range of attributes, including frequency, voltage, current, and power, once every three seconds.

Before long, the research team had its answer. The measurements recorded on the transformer attached to the battery energy storage system peaked every minute, just like on the bigger power grids.

It turns out that many stationary batteries, from data center UPSes to large grid-scale batteries, check their state-of-charge every minute. Since these devices are internet-connected, their clocks are in near perfect synchronicity. Every time one checks its charge, the disturbance is imperceptible on the wide grid. But when they all do it together, that disruption becomes apparent.

To find evidence that UPSes were the main culprit on the grid, Witthaut and his team turned to data provided by grid operators. They found their answer in Ireland.

There, data centers have grown to consume a large fraction of the country’s electricity. By 2022, data centers represented 18% of the load on Ireland’s grid, and each facility is served by a fleet of UPSes. Sure enough, between 2016 and 2021, the minute-by-minute frequency drop on the Irish grid quadrupled from 3 milli-Hertz (mHz) in 2016 to 12 mHz in 2021. More recently, some drops have exceeded 20 mHz.

Ireland’s 50 Hz grid is built to withstand swings of plus or minus 200 mHz, though the grid operator would rather limit the swings to 100 mHz. In that range, 20 mHz isn’t a lot, but data centers aren’t the only loads on the grid. If other loads also cause the frequency to sag, the extra bump from every UPS checking its state-of-charge could push the grid toward blackout conditions.

The problem is entirely preventable. Rather than check state-of-charge exactly on the minute, Witthaus and team recommend that UPS and grid-scale battery manufacturers add some randomization to the schedule.

While UPSes are driving the problem today, there’s a risk that the phenomenon could persist. The researchers also tested IoT devices. In devices as diverse as a smart oven and Raspberry Pi clusters, they were able to detect periodic power fluctuations. The fix is also a simple software patch, but many IoT devices go unpatched. A recent study found that less than 3% of IoT devices are running the latest firmware.

Regulators may soon have to step in to demand changes, but they can only address their corner of the grid. Given the global nature of the issue, it’ll ultimately require global coordination. The mystery may be solved, but the problem could remain for years to come.

Doomscrolling, the habit of spending excessive amounts of time consuming content on social media, has become incredibly widespread. A survey from last year found that 64% of Americans say they doomscroll.

Researchers have warned that doomscrolling can negatively affect several aspects of your well-being, including your mental health and attention span. Spending long periods scrolling can lead to brain fatigue, difficulty focusing, and disrupted sleep. And if a lot of the content you’re consuming is negative or stressful, it can leave you feeling disheartened, anxious, and emotionally drained.

It’s hard to break the cycle of doomscrolling, but there are plenty of apps that provide content that’s engaging and productive.
