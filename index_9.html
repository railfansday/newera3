#485. Nvidia created a weird bit of intrigue by not filing a routine SEC report

During Nvidia’s quarterly call on Tuesday, CFO-turned-interim-CEO Colette Kress dropped this little oddity: She said Nvidia wouldn’t be immediately filing its quarterly results report, known as a 10-Q, with the SEC. “We plan to file our 10-Q in the coming weeks.” And she offered no reason why.

The delay was widely noticed. Among the many comments on X about it, economic and investment analyst Edward Dowd called it a “red flag.” Nvidia has typically filed the 10-Q the same day it reports results.

The 10-Q offers far more detail about a company’s operations than an earnings press release, including sections that disclose “legal proceedings,” which include lawsuits that could affect the company; and “risk factors,” which cover other potentially business-impairing items.

The announcement of the delay was so unusual that UBS analyst Timothy Arcuri asked about it after the public earnings call. Nvidia told him the delay was related to whether the company would need to disclose additional information about its ongoing investigation into Huang in the 10-Q.

“The company announced that the 10-Q will be filed ‘in the coming weeks’ – later than usual. When queried on our callback, management indicated this was related to the ongoing investigation and whether any additional risk factors would need to be added to the filing,” Arcuri wrote in his research note. (Arcuri still rates the stock a “buy” by the way, although he did lower his price target to $220 from $240. The stock is now trading around $150).

Notably, on the same day that Nvidia reported earnings, The Wall Street Journal reported that the SEC is investigating Nvidia over whether the company’s disclosures misled investors.

So the mystery thickens: What will Nvidia disclose? We’ll have to wait and see. (Nvidia has not yet responded to our request for comment.)

The delay suggests the company may have more to say about its mounting legal troubles. Nvidia is now facing multiple federal investigations over allegations that its partners were selling restricted Nvidia AI chips to China. So far, the scrutiny has come from a number of congressional committees; the Department of Justice looking into Megaspeed, the company run by a woman with whom Huang had an affair and who is also suspected of being a Chinese spy; and now, reportedly, the SEC.

Additionally, Nvidia is conducting its own internal investigation into Huang after he admitted to having an affair with Alice Huang (the Megaspeed executive), and allegations surfaced that he also had affairs with other Nvidia employees.

There’s little doubt that Wall Street will scrutinize that 10-Q once it’s finally filed. The intrigue brings to mind the old saying: “Doctors bury their mistakes. Lawyers put theirs in prison. And accountants hide theirs in the footnotes.”

The conversation took place against the backdrop of a series of recent large-scale outages — at Apple, at Microsoft, at the NSA, and at the Federal Aviation Administration — that have led to concerns that America may be under attack by cyberthreat actors. “What does that mean? Are you a prepper?” Sorkin asked.

Luckey didn’t deny the characterization. “I own a missile base 200 feet underground,” he answered, to audience laughter. “It’s a funny joke, but a missile base is actually a pretty bad way to run from the apocalypse. #475. Satya Nadella predicts AI agents will reshuffle the economics of the web

Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella predicts that the rise of AI agents is going to reshape the economics of the internet, impacting the business models of the companies that rely on advertising and what he referred to as “human web” traffic.

“I think this is going to be interesting,” he said, speaking to the changing models. “It’s the same thing that’s happening in other categories, right? Which is, if you’re in love with a particular business model, you have to kind of scramble,” he said.

#472. JetBlue unveils new first-class seats to bring back profits

JetBlue Airways unveiled its new domestic first-class seats Wednesday as more of the country's carriers bet on the premium travel boom to make them profitable again.

JetBlue announced it would offer a domestic first class — BlueFirst — in December 2024 and plans to start flying aircraft with the new seats and full on-board product next month. The carrier showed off the new cabin at its home hub at John F. Kennedy International Airport on Wednesday. Retrofits will continue during 2027, CEO Joanna Geraghty told CNBC late last month.

JetBlue will have 12 of the seats on its Airbus A320 fleet and 30 extra-legroom seats, down from 42 of those options previously. Because the airplanes don't have ovens, JetBlue said it is offering an array of cold food options like sesame noodles, pastrami sandwiches and tortellini salad, though they vary by route.

The rollout comes as the New York airline tries to stabilize after last posting a profit in 2019. Last month, JetBlue executives sought to calm investors after shares in the airline hit a record low following a report that bondholders had contracted restructuring advisors.

"We have no intention, no plans to file for bankruptcy. We are not engaged with any restructuring company," Geraghty told CNBC in an interview on Oct. 27.

Geraghty told CNBC's Phil LeBeau on Wednesday that JetBlue has "met with a few of the creditors over the last couple of weeks as part of ... normal investor engagement." She said the company made it clear to bondholders that JetBlue doesn't need to reorganize.

The airline's shares are up more than 40% this month, more than other passenger carriers, as of midday Wednesday, though they are still off about 11% so far this year.

JetBlue is betting that resilient travel demand, especially for roomier seats will help fuel its turnaround plan, which aims to get the carrier to an operating profit next year and $1 in adjusted earnings per share in 2028.

Airline executives have said they expect fares to stay elevated next year as they grapple with higher costs, including but not limited to fuel, such as labor and airport fees. Geraghty said Wednesday that even if fuel prices moderate next year, "I don't think fares are going to fall as much as people expect."

Airlines large and small are racing to outfit their cabins with roomier, more expensive seating options to help cover ever-higher costs, from jet fuel to labor to airport operation fees.

For example, a ticket in the new recliner first-class seats between Kennedy airport and Fort Lauderdale-Hollywood International Airport in Florida is going for $847 roundtrip in the first week of March while standard economy is $446.

On Tuesday, budget carrier Frontier Airlines unveiled its new first-class seats, which will take up the first two rows, and said it would outfit its Airbus fleet with premium seating that has extra legroom and in-seat power through row eight. The airline will take out a row of seating on its planes to make the pricier ones fit, Chief Marketing Officer Bobby Schroeter told CNBC in an interview Monday.

JetBlue last month reported that it ended the third quarter with $2.2 billion in liquidity and that its unencumbered asset base is now $7 billion, including $2.8 billion in aircraft and engines.

JetBlue had total debt of $8.4 billion as of Sept. 30, the carrier said in its earnings filing.

Geraghty told CNBC last month there is a "fundamental disconnect between the narrative in the bond market and what our financial results actually demonstrate."

When asked whether JetBlue's eye on keeping costs down includes furloughs, layoffs or buyouts, Geraghty last month said "absolutely not."

“Somebody was telling me about sort of even the transportation systems and the amount of stuff that’s discoverable but with unpatchable firmware,” Nadella also added on the topic of vulnerable systems. “So we’ll have to really modernize for sure … and then we will have to assess what the threat vector looks like, what our own ability to both detect and then defend in real time looks like.”

“The patch [now] needs to go instantly from being available to being patched in production, because it’s being exploited in the wild, right? So that’s going to create instability.”

#457. Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella explains why he asked 60K employees to quit

Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella explained Wednesday why he offered buyouts to 60,000 of his employees in October, essentially urging them to voluntarily quit instead of reducing the workforce via another massive layoff.

Short answer: AI, but not, he insists, in the way you might expect. He’s not saying he’s replacing employees with AI, especially software engineers. He’s saying he’s reorganizing Microsoft into a company where everyone uses AI to do their work, and their jobs become focused on managing that AI.

The buyout was “to make sure that at least everyone felt like they had agency in making choices,” he told Andrew Ross Sorkin during the DealBook Summit.

“Let’s take software engineering. We went from, ‘Well, we don’t buy that AI can ever do software.’ To suddenly, ‘Wow, it can do all software, and why do we need software engineers?’ To now rediscovering that software engineering is very much alive and, in fact, is going to thrive, except the way the team is organized and the work that a software engineer does has now got a new operating point,” he explained.

For some people, “that reorganization and change is sort of hard, right?” he added, “but, I mean, it has to be done.”

He offered the example of the team that builds Cowork, the AI agent in Microsoft 365 apps. He said the team consists of just 11 developers who have mastered using AI to do their jobs. “They build a software factory that builds the product. They don’t build the product.”

He believes that every employee will one day similarly become a manager of agents, rather than a person directly doing the work. “That level of direction is what, by the way, is going to happen in all jobs.”

But Microsoft, as a 51-year-old company, didn’t grow up as an AI-first company. “So the question is, how quickly can an incumbent transform the work, the workflow, and the work artifact, and with all the wage support that goes with it?” he said. “It’s going to take a bit more time than anyone expects. And there is going to be in all of this, you know, people who may say, ‘Hey, I have some other choices I want to make.’”

When asked about this same topic — why ask 60,000 people to voluntarily resign — last week by Gergely Orosz on his The Pragmatic Engineer podcast, Nadella further explained, “We have created enormous anxiety in people sort of feeling that they don’t have control of their own destiny because the way we have talked about this AI revolution.”

He called out talk about the singularity (the hypothetical point at which AI surpasses human intelligence) and predictions that AI will take away all jobs. “All of that leads anyone who even wants to change to be sort of anxious … You have to sort of walk the hard walk of really making the structural changes, getting people slotted, giving them opportunity.”

In other words, the buyout was the message to employees: You can get with the new AI-first program, which will radically change your job, or you choose to get on with your life. Elsewhere.

Nimble’s new collection arrives as competition in the Bluetooth tracking market continues to heat up. Apple’s AirTag helped popularize the category, while Google recently introduced its first-party tracker, the $29 Pixel Tag, powered by its Find Hub network. Meanwhile, established third-party players, such as Chipolo and Pebblebee, continue to expand their lineups.

Nimble is taking a slightly different approach. Rather than introducing another stand-alone tracker to attach to a set of keys or slip into a bag, the company has integrated tracking technology directly into practical accessories, including wallets, keychains, luggage tags, and passport covers.

The lineup includes seven products, ranging from the $14.95 Trace Tag, which features a replaceable coin-cell battery, to the $49.95 Trace Folio, a passport cover with built-in rechargeable tracking. Other options include the Trace Keyfob ($19.95), Trace Slim Wallet Card ($29.95), Trace Keychain ($29.95), Trace Luggage Tag ($39.95), and Trace Wallet ($39.95).

The entire collection is compatible with both Apple’s Find My and Google’s Find Hub networks.

Ross Howe, co-founder and CEO of Nimble, told us that the company’s approach is designed to differentiate its products from traditional trackers.

“Most locators are built as utility gadgets, not as things you want to own. Few were asking why the locator couldn’t just be the nice object itself. So, for example, we made a wallet findable instead of making a tracker that fits a wallet. We also thought more options should be rechargeable.”

The Trace Luggage Tag and Trace Folio are arguably the most interesting products in the collection. Dedicated luggage tags and passport covers with built-in tracking remain relatively uncommon, giving Nimble an opportunity to stand out in a market. There are only a few alternatives, including Spotminder’s $59 passport cover with Apple Find My support, which is $10 more than Nimble’s option. Knog Scout offers a luggage tag, although I find its design a little too bulky.

Rechargeability is another appealing aspect of Nimble’s new collection. Several models support USB-C or wireless charging, offering an alternative to the disposable batteries found in many traditional trackers. Howe says battery life lasts for “months,” with the Trace Keyfob, for example, rated for approximately six months on a charge.

Sustainability is another major part of Nimble’s pitch. Staying true to its roots, the company has incorporated eco-friendly materials throughout the collection, including recycled plastics and plant-based, vegan aloe leather. The products have also undergone third-party testing for chemicals of concern.

“We’ve spent the last two years going through the chemistry of our products line by line to remove or limit as many harmful ingredients as possible. Trace is the first collection designed specifically to meet these higher standards. We’re not 100% yet, but the independent labs checking our work will help us get there,” Howe said.

The collection is available now through its website and Amazon. All seven products come in five color options: Black, Stone, Tangerine, Plum, and Teal.

#444. Anthropic’s IPO puts AI’s circular financing boom to the test

Arriving at public markets today, Anthropic’s IPO is poised to raise billions for the company and its investors. But the biggest surprise may be just how much of that money will end up going to supplier-investors.

Amazon and Google, two of Anthropic’s largest infrastructure partners, hold stakes valued around a combined $307.9 billion at the company’s updated IPO price of $83 per share. Amazon, which invested $18 billion into the AI startup, would see its 14.7% stake hit upwards of $159 billion, while Google’s 13.7% stake is valued at nearly $149 billion, according to share ownership figures disclosed in Anthropic’s IPO prospectus.

A handful of other AI infrastructure providers are also positioned to benefit. Nvidia, AMD, Microsoft, Broadcom, Micron, and TSMC have joined Google in committing $26 billion to Anthropic at its IPO price in a private placement.

In total, Google has invested $12.3 billion into Anthropic since 2023, with another promised $30 billion dependent on Anthropic meeting certain performance milestones. And with the exception of TSMC, Anthropic has announced multibillion-dollar strategic partnerships with its other infrastructure supplier investors — including Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, Microsoft, and Micron — that would see Anthropic purchasing their products.

This is the circular financing that has made some observers so skeptical of the AI boom. These companies are not just investors; they’re also Anthropic’s biggest suppliers. It might look like Amazon and Google are putting billions into Anthropic, but they get that money right back when Anthropic needs to train a model or serve Claude to users. From afar, it looks like unprecedented revenue growth for cloud companies and unprecedented fundraising from Anthropic — but it’s just the same money passing from one pocket to another.

Today’s IPO marks a critical change in that financing system, the moment it might just become self-sustaining. Those investments appear to have paid off, and are now liquid. Anthropic is still operating at a net loss of tens of billions of dollars each quarter — but it will no longer be suppliers and private investors footing the bill. Now, capital will come from a mix of public-market investors and corporate bonds, neither of which have the conflicts-of-interest that caused concerns before.

And critically, the company’s new bottom line looks pretty good.

Once it debuts, Anthropic is expected to have about $220 billion in cash, cash equivalents, and short-term investments, including proceeds from its IPO and the $26 billion private placement. Anthropic also has the ability to pay for future commitments in stock.

Joey Brookhart, who leads the Tokenomics team at SemiAnalysis, estimates that Anthropic needs roughly $280 billion in cumulative revenue over the next four years to pay for its compute bill, which Brookhart said is “achievable given where they will be next year from a revenue perspective.”

“The ownership stake is very worthwhile, and those companies no longer need any more support from [suppliers],” Brookhart told TechCrunch. “Amazon doesn’t need to prop them up anymore. The revenue is all coming from other customers.”

That doesn’t mean the server bills are going down, though. Anthropic has already committed to spend $900 billion, primarily for compute and infrastructure, according to a regulatory filing. Certain agreements have termination provisions, but Brookhart thinks the company is in a strong position to pay up.

On the other side, Anthropic generated $17 billion in third-quarter 2026 revenue, putting the company on pace for a $68 billion annualized rate for the year. If Anthropic continues to grow at its current trajectory, Brookhart believes it could easily cover the gap needed to fund its compute.

Gil Luria, head of technology research at D.A. Davidson, agreed.

“By the time the IPO is done, Anthropic’s going to have more cash on hand than any company has ever had,” Luria told TechCrunch, noting that the firm raised around $141 billion in capital and will take home around $91.7 billion in net proceeds at $83 per share. “And they’re not burning cash. They’re just going to have a war chest.”

A public listing will make Anthropic a more credible borrower, making it easier to raise money outside of public markets, says Brookhart. That’s despite recent reports that creditors might not see Anthropic as “investment-grade” material.

Luria distinguished between Anthropic’s supplier investments and neoclouds like CoreWeave, which he sees as more purely supported by circular financing. “CoreWeave wouldn’t exist if Nvidia didn’t prop them up, and it probably won’t exist if Nvidia doesn’t bail them out,” Luria said.

Brookhart also pushed back on describing Anthropic’s financing as purely circular, saying it was still “additive to the ecosystem.”

For both analysts, the biggest risk to Anthropic is simply that it will lose the AI race. Right now, the company’s growth is fueled by its status at the frontier of generative AI — but if it’s overtaken by OpenAI, Google DeepMind, or even cheap open-weight models, that could change fast. As Luria put it: “If we’re all using other people’s models, then Anthropic’s not worth a trillion and a half dollars. It’s worth nothing.”

But regardless of who wins the frontier, compute suppliers will likely be just fine. Neither Brookhart nor Luria believe Anthropic’s growth is at risk, despite some arguments that cheaper tokens and more competition will directly affect the AI company’s bottom line.

“For Amazon and Google, the risk isn’t actually that high because in that scenario, we’re using other models,” Luria said. “I’m arguing we’re going to use a lot more AI. I don’t know if it’s all Anthropic’s AI…. That’s still going to come from a data center managed by Amazon, Google, and Microsoft.”

#439. UK government forced to disclose secret Apple backdoor order

The U.K. government has been ordered to confirm that it issued a secret order demanding Apple to provide authorities with encrypted customer information.

The Thursday ruling from the U.K.’s Investigatory Powers Tribunal, an independent court that allows citizens to challenge government surveillance orders, is a rare rebuke for the government, which sought to keep the encryption backdoor order a state secret.

The tribunal’s ruling rejected the Home Office’s claim that disclosing the order would be damaging to national security.

California-based Apple refused the order, and blocked U.K. users from switching on its Advanced Data Protection data-encryption feature for iCloud, rather than comply with the demand. The U.K.’s secret order was later narrowed to allow access to British nationals’ data stored in Apple’s encrypted cloud, and Apple also challenged this second order.

Prior to the ruling, Apple was legally barred from disclosing the order, even after details leaked. When reached for comment, Apple told TechCrunch that it welcomes the decision to make public the fact that the U.K. Home Office issued a technical capability notice.

Apple previously said that it has “never built a backdoor or master key” to any of its products or services and “never will.”

A spokesperson for the U.K. Home Office, which has yet to confirm the existence of a notice, did not respond to a request for comment.

Rights group Liberty International, which brought the legal challenge on behalf of two individuals, said that this is the first known time that the government has been compelled to confirm the existence of a secret technical order.

A final hearing on what specific order details can be released is anticipated next year.

#438. Feds arrest top official of healthcare company, alleging illegal drug importation

U.S. authorities are accusing the two top officials of a Canadian company of operating a long-running scheme to illegally import prescription drugs into the U.S., according to court records filed Tuesday.

John Thomas Howard, treasurer and a director of the company, CANARX, was arrested Sunday on smuggling and conspiracy charges, according to the records.

Bob Howard, his brother, who is president and a director of CANARX, is also wanted in the federal investigation, according to an affidavit from Dustin Dobbs, a special agent with the Food and Drug Administration's Office of Criminal Investigations. He has not yet been taken into custody.

Besides conspiracy and smuggling, the arrest warrant said John Thomas Howard was charged with introducing misbranded drugs into interstate commerce and illegal drug importation. No plea has been filed.

CANARX was featured in a CNBC investigation last year that revealed how the company and others known as alternative funding programs, or AFPs, are becoming more pervasive around the U.S. as drug costs skyrocket. The investigation found these companies have penetrated the country's healthcare system by offering high-cost specialty drugs at more affordable prices through private and public employers. In some cases, employers are requiring their staff to use an AFP.

AFPs often obtain the medications overseas and ship them directly to patients, a practice that federal authorities have said is illegal and poses safety risks. CNBC traced the trail of drugs from abroad to the U.S. via a supply chain that the pharmaceutical companies producing the medications have not authorized.

On its website, CANARX, founded in 2002 and based in Windsor, Ontario, Canada, described its business as the first international prescription provider for the U.S. and the safest such program in the industry. The company said it sources its medications from Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia and New Zealand.

But the affidavit said that the FDA's investigation found "there is probable cause to believe that (CANARX) illegally imported or caused the illegal importation of prescription drugs into the United States …."

The affidavit does not specify which medications the agency believes were illegally imported. The criminal complaint covers the period from Feb. 22, 2017, through April 15, 2026.

The company's general counsel, Joseph Morris, told CNBC in a statement: "The Howards deny all the charges against them, will defend vigorously, and expect to be vindicated in court."

The company has been under FDA scrutiny for several years. In 2023, the agency seized medication being shipped by CANARX that it says was potentially misbranded and counterfeit, according to an email sent to a patient that was obtained by CNBC.

And in 2019, the FDA sent a warning letter to the company, saying it was operating "in a manner that substitutes the FDA-approved drugs prescribed by the U.S. healthcare provider with unapproved drugs." The central question for AFPs is the legality of importing prescription drugs into the U.S. for personal use. In a separate civil lawsuit that is pending, Gilead Sciences accused CANARX and other companies of illegally importing Biktarvy, its medication to treat HIV.

#395. Retailers are turning to entertainment to win shoppers’ attention

Legacy retailer Gap Inc. made an unconventional hire earlier this year: a chief entertainment officer.

Gap is part of a growing cohort of retailers investing in entertainment and experiences both in-store and through exclusive online content to drive engagement.

"Brands are stories, and we are the storytellers," CEO Richard Dickson said at the Goldman Sachs Global Consumer and Retail conference in September. "I think you could see that come to life when you look at some of the work that we've done, putting our brands in the center of culture and cultural conversation."

At the time, Dickson was president and chief operating officer of the company, witnessing firsthand the sectors' intersection before departing for Gap.

"I think the post-Covid consumer attitude around shopping certainly evolved a lot," said Bryan Eshelman, who leads the retail practice at consulting firm AlixPartners. "It has gone from shopping as a fulfillment task to shopping, in many instances, more as an entertainment form."

But with the rise of social media, content creation and short-form offerings, experts said audiences are becoming hungrier than ever for something new and original.

The retail and entertainment intersection is happening at a time when intellectual property is becoming more valuable than ever, according to experts.

As consumers encounter more marketing than ever, the companies that will win the battle for attention are the ones that can "cut through all of the noise," said Alex Michael, senior managing director at investment firm LionTree.

"The hardest thing to find and to get is audience these days," Michael told CNBC. "IP, in that sense, is the most valuable thing out there, and only becoming more valuable because it's attached to audience."

At the same time, he said, the way IP is distributed is broadening to include video, audio, short-form, long-form and live event content. Consumers are also redefining their loyalty to brands, which means companies have to "play much earlier in the consumer journey" than they have in years past to secure their trust, he said.

The trend is also likely helped by executives who have worked in both retail and entertainment: Gap's Kaufman came from Paramount, for example, and Dickson and Skydance co-CEO Ynon Kreiz held top roles at Mattel.

"The gray areas have never been more pronounced as to what is classically defined as retail or media or entertainment or live events," Michael said. "I think it's a really interesting time for nontraditional players to be making content because you're seeing this massive consolidation of these legacy entertainment companies," he said. "I think there are just more open lanes for independent creators and also brands and anyone outside the traditional studio system to create content."

That's where retailers might tap creators, Akaks said, because they can use their platforms to reach consumers directly.

"I do think there's a world where we're going to see creators and brands sort of co-creating IP that maybe they're owning together," he said.

Once those companies get consumers into the store, they're also leaning into experiential retail, making shopping a more immersive experience than it otherwise would be, like Dick's Sporting Goods offering interactive activities and simulators in its stores.

According to data from AlixPartners, companies investing in experiential retail such as Coach and Ralph Lauren saw close to 10% year-over-year increases in consumers' average basket size.

"The idea that retailers would potentially invest in some distributed electronic content to help drive traffic, and then when you get there, it's experiential — it really goes into the kinds of flywheels that entertainment companies are also trying to build and grow," said Jeff Goldstein, co-lead of the media and entertainment practice at AlixPartners.

Yet, the entertainment that retail brands are developing is only one piece in a larger puzzle to retain customers and attract new ones. Translating that into tangible sales growth isn't necessarily a given.

"The basics of having great product in stock at a transparent price that feels like value is not swept away by animatronics," AlixPartners' Eshelman said. "The fundamentals are still there, and if you're going to have a good retail proposition, you need to have that balance. You can't mask bad product at a bad price with no inventory with glitz and glamour."

#379. Big bets against AI debt, from CoreWeave to Oracle and the Mag 7, could soon hit the ETF market

Concerns about balance sheets and valuations in the artificial intelligence sector have hit the market hard since October, and investors who have shorted publicly traded tech companies in the AI trade have profited. Most investors don't think as much about "shorting" debt, but there will be a way through exchange-traded funds to profit from widening credit default swap spreads of top AI companies.

Both Tuttle Capital and Defiance ETFs have filed for funds that target the credit default swaps of companies closely linked to the AI trade, from CoreWeave and Oracle to the Magnificent Seven: Alphabet, Amazon, Meta Platforms, Microsoft Corporation, Nvidia, Tesla, and Apple.

Credit default swaps are contracts in which investors pay for protection in case the borrower can't repay its debt. Wider spreads indicate investors view the bonds as riskier. CDS is a closely watched indicator of issuer health in the debt markets, especially in the AI market this year. The ETFs are designed to gain when the CDS spreads of these companies widen, either as a group in the case of a Magnificent Seven CDS fund, or individually in the case of proposed ETFs targeting Oracle, CoreWeave and Meta. The funds would lose value when the market assesses the creditworthiness of these debt issuers as improving.

Both ETF managers filed for CoreWeave and Oracle CDS funds, specifically, among broader AI and semiconductor company CDS portfolios.

If the exchange-traded fund managers get approval from the Securities and Exchange Commission, these portfolios would be the first of their kind in an ETF market that has become more aggressive in recent years about launching higher-risk strategies revolving around more complex financial instruments than just public stocks and bonds. The ETF managers plan to employ leverage in the portfolios, which both increases risk and the potential for outsized returns on any given day, similar to the way existing single-stock leverage and inverse ETFs function.

The ETF managers say these CDS funds can serve an important role in a market on edge about AI debt.

"As AI infrastructure investment accelerates and companies take on significant capital commitments to fund that growth, we see an opportunity to give investors more targeted tools to manage credit risk and hedge potential financial stress across some of the biggest names driving the AI buildout," Sylvia Jablonski, CIO of Defiance ETFs, said in a statement to CNBC.

Neocloud companies like CoreWeave are a major focus for AI skeptics, an AI bubble watch compounded by the exit of Jensen Huang from Nvidia, who is seen as a critical player in financing the AI buildout, with Nvidia called by some the "central bank of AI."

Apollo Global Management CEO Marc Rowan on Tuesday said that neocloud company AI projects could face an existential crisis if Nvidia funding dries up. "There was a strong incentive for Nvidia as a business matter to get these projects off the ground," Rowan said in an interview with CNBC's "Squawk on the Street." "If that banking function, that guarantor function, gets taken away, access to capital for a large number of neoclouds is going to disappear."

In its earnings filings and conference call on Tuesday, Nvidia shifted the tone in discussing the way it finances the AI ecosystem.

Matthew Tuttle, founder and CEO of Tuttle Capital Management, said it is less likely that these ETFs will find an audience among retail investors than smaller institutional players.

"It may be a stretch for the average retail guy," he said, but he added that having managed leveraged and inverse ETFs, it is clear that many investors "get interested" when a particular asset is seeing outsized moves, as has been the case with AI company-linked CDS in 2026. "The stuff that moves a lot is the stuff that tends to generate the most interest," he said.

Both prospectuses stress that these funds are not designed to move inversely to the reference companies' stocks and can lose money while a company's shares decline. They also note these funds don't short bonds or bet directly against a company's stock, and to buy CDS protection, they pay premiums, which are a unique cost to the fund manager whether or not credit conditions worsen.

The CDS of these companies have been on the move in a major way as part of the AI trade over the past year.

CoreWeave's 9.75% bond due in 2031 is yielding close to 16%, while spreads on its five-year credit default swaps were recently between 1,100-1,200. Spreads over 1,000 are generally considered a sign of distress.

CoreWeave executives have taken their messaging about the debt public, with co-founder and chief strategy officer Brian Venturo posting on X.com on Wednesday that he had purchased $30 million in unsecured company bonds and was going to hold them until maturity in 2031. "I've never been more confident in CoreWeave," he wrote.

Market commentators have noted since Venturo's post that he has sold over $1 billion in shares of the company's stock.

Oracle CDS contracts have moved up over the past year from below 80 to a current range above 300.

The CDS spreads on the biggest tech companies, including Amazon and Meta as examples, rose over 100% within the past year, as their free cash flow turned negative from spending heavily on AI. Even though the market is much less concerned about their balance sheets than that of the neoclouds, major tech company CDS spreads did hit five-year highs earlier this month.

Tuttle said that these ETFs, if approved, would be the "easiest" to use for smaller institutions and family offices that want CDS exposure and "are either too small to trade with the Goldmans of the world or lack the in-house expertise to trade CDS on their own."

"It is a different way to hedge the AI trade," Tuttle said.

ETF investing experts expressed skepticism about this approach and market reception. "I don't see much demand materializing for these types of products," said Nate Geraci, president of NovaDius Wealth Management. But he added it does make sense to target these ETFs "at retail investors who are seeing scary headlines around AI capex spending. Yet another way to speculate (gamble)," he said.

Todd Sohn, chief ETF strategist at Baird Strategas, said he foresees confusion with how these funds will work. "How many non-professionals know what CDS are?" he said.

Tuttle says it won't take billions in assets to make an ETF focused on AI CDS a minor financial success. "My criteria is I want to see a runway to $20 million, which is about our breakeven. I see it here. It doesn't mean it will happen, but I think we have a decent chance."

#366. CEO turned informant: How the FDA built a case against executives promising cheap medications

His brother and the president of the company, Bob Howard, is wanted on similar charges and was still being sought as of last week, according to court records.

The charges mark the first criminal case by U.S. authorities against a company that's part of a cottage industry known as alternative funding programs, or AFPs, that promise to lower or eliminate high prescription drug costs for Americans.

The cheaper medications are typically sourced from international markets, a business model that the Food and Drug Administration and Department of Homeland Security say is illegal and can put patients' health at risk.

At issue is the unregulated nature of drugs intended for foreign markets and, in some cases, the risk of counterfeit products. And while AFPs generally argue that the importation of medication is allowable under U.S. law by individuals for personal use, authorities contend the operations go too far.

CANARX, based in Windsor, Ontario, was established in 2002 and was featured in a CNBC investigation into the booming AFP industry.

Joseph Morris, general counsel for CANARX, told CNBC last year that the business was operating legally and was confident in the safety of the prescriptions it provided. Last week, Morris told CNBC that the Howard brothers "deny all the charges against them, will defend vigorously, and expect to be vindicated in court."

The FDA's case against CANARX started with a tip about a similar healthcare operation called ScriptSourcing. It is detailed in a 35-page affidavit — dated Oct. 29 and unsealed last week — written by Dustin Dobbs, special agent with the FDA's Office of Criminal Investigations.

Based in Bonita Springs, Florida, ScriptSourcing marketed its international prescription program to self-funded employer health plans. Founder and CEO Gary Becker was described on the company's website as a "health insurance and risk management consultant for more than 30 years."

ScriptSourcing had been on the FDA's radar since at least 2022 for potential drug import violations, according to the affidavit. In August 2025, Dobbs, posing as someone interested in the AFP business, emailed ScriptSourcing and asked if the company was FDA-compliant.

A ScriptSourcing employee in responses to Dobbs said the company was FDA-compliant and that it had more than 2,000 name brand specialty medications and worked with more than 3,000 self-funded health plans.

However, that same year, the agency learned of two alleged incidents in which medications from ScriptSourcing's international drug import program had harmed patients, according to Dobbs' affidavit. One patient had been hospitalized due to a small bowel obstruction after she used misbranded Mounjaro sourced by the company via a Canadian pharmacy, and another was allegedly seriously injured after receiving a counterfeit medication.

Facing scrutiny from the FDA, ScriptSourcing CEO Becker turned on the Howard brothers of CANARX.

By May 2026, Becker had become a cooperating witness for the government in its investigation into the Canadian firm, Dobbs wrote. Becker told the FDA that he had done business with CANARX on drug importation and that the company had given him a "road map" on how to operate, according to the affidavit.

ScriptSourcing provided the FDA with a spreadsheet of financial transactions with CANARX between 2021 and 2026. It showed approximately 16,549 prescription drugs have been dispensed, the affidavit said.

The phone calls "show that John Howard was aware of the routes for importation of drugs from Canada into the United States and has tried to mold them to fit [CANARX's] illegal drug importation program," Dobbs wrote in the affidavit. Among the shipments en route or being prepared by CANARX at the time the information was provided was a package that contained Neupro, an FDA-regulated drug for the treatment of Parkinson's disease and restless legs syndrome.

The FDA did not respond to a request for comment from CNBC on the details in the affidavit.

Gary Becker's attorney, George Karavetsos, declined to comment when reached by CNBC, but confirmed on Monday that ScriptSourcing was no longer in business.

The AFP business model was at the center of a civil lawsuit originally filed by Gilead Sciences in December 2024 that alleged several companies, including CANARX, illegally imported its HIV drug Biktarvy. CANARX has not responded to Gilead's allegations.

Gilead said in a statement to CNBC last week in response to charges against the Howard brothers that it "welcomed the government's efforts to hold accountable those whose conduct may put patients at risk."

"Pharmaceuticals manufactured for foreign markets are almost always considered unapproved and misbranded drugs under U.S. law, whether counterfeit or even if made by the same manufacturer that produced the version sold in the United States," Dobbs wrote in his affidavit.

Family offices continued to boost their stock holdings in the third quarter, although they hedged the artificial intelligence trade with a larger bet on financials, according to the CNBC Family Office Portfolio Tracker.

Family offices increased their allocation to public stocks to 36.4% of their portfolios in the third quarter, up from 35.6% in the second quarter, according to the Tracker.  Their stock holdings have jumped from 33.6% in 2024, a substantial increase for family offices, which rarely deviate from their long-term financial allocations.

The CNBC Family Office Portfolio Tracker is powered by data from Addepar, the foundational data and artificial intelligence platform used by financial professionals globally that now has over $10 trillion on its platform. Unlike surveys, Addepar's data provides a real-time look into the portfolios of hundreds of single-family offices — aggregated and anonymized —representing a total of more than $1.4 trillion in family office assets.

The increased allocation to stocks, whether from passive market gains or active buying, shows that family offices aren't pulling back from the market despite growing fears of an AI bubble and weakness in tech stocks.

"As we've studied family offices for more than 15 years, they've demonstrated time and again that they take the long view," said Eric Poirier, CEO of Addepar. "But they do like staying invested. So with the run-up in AI thematically, they're staying exposed, they're risk on."

At the same time, family offices are hedging their tech bets. According to Addepar, family offices have lower exposure to AI stocks when compared to the broader S&P 500. While tech stocks account for 40% of the S&P market cap, family offices have an average allocation of 28% of their equity portfolios in tech stocks, according to the Tracker.

They are offsetting the tech risk in part with financials. Financial services account for 11% of the S&P, but the average allocation for family offices is 18%. Poirier said the lower tech weighting and higher financials exposure suggests that family offices see financials as a potential offset to AI risk.

"They're fading the AI trade to some extent, but still staying quite exposed," he said.

Family offices are also continuing to dial back their exposure to alternative investments. Alts account for 47% of their portfolios, down from 48.5% a year ago. The biggest decliner was real estate, down to 7.2% from 9.4% in the third quarter of 2025. Higher interest rates have driven down valuations in many real-estate portfolios and funds, likely accounting for much of the drop.

Hedge funds also saw a decline, from 8.9% a year ago to 7.5%, as many funds grapple with more volatile stock markets and declining tech stocks. Their investments in private credit declined slightly, to 1% of their portfolios. Their private equity holdings fell for the second straight quarter, to 6.4% of their portfolios.

They also saw a decline in their allocation to private companies — companies held directly rather than through a private equity fund. Family office holdings of private companies fell 0.3% to 15.7%, likely due to slight declines in valuations. Still, private companies remain by far their largest segment among alts, as family offices continue to focus on direct investing.

Cash piles grew slightly for family offices — to 8.6% — suggesting they want to keep adequate dry powder on hand in case of a market correction or crisis.

The big question for family offices this year and next is how they manage rising yields and inflation fears. Some were positioning for inflation at the start of the year with bets on infrastructure and real estate, yet the surge in the yields driven by oil prices was faster than many expected.

Family office holdings of fixed income have steadily increased for three straight quarters, to 8.1% in the third quarter.

"Family offices tend to be able to withstand market volatility more so than other investors," Poirier said. "That said, their response to the historic rise in fixed income and rates has been more muted relative to even five-year averages."

Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi received a subdued welcome on her first trip to China as premier, signaling Beijing's limited appetite for repairing ties strained by a simmering row over Taiwan.

At gatherings such as this week's Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in Shenzhen, protocol details — including who greets a visiting leader at the airport and where they are positioned in official photographs — often convey political messages.

Takaichi was met on the tarmac Wednesday by a retired Chinese diplomat, rather than a serving official, and Japan's ambassador to China, Kenji Kanasugi.

The low-key reception contrasted sharply with the warmer welcomes given to other visiting leaders, including South Korean President Lee Jae Myung, while U.S. President Donald Trump was greeted personally by Chinese President Xi Jinping.

In the leaders' group photo, Takaichi was placed in the back row, off to the side and several places from Xi, who stood alongside Trump and Russian President Vladimir Putin.

"Beijing sought to downgrade her official status to that of a head of government rather than a head of state," said Jeremy Chan, senior analyst at Eurasia Group.

During Thursday morning's greeting line, Takaichi spent several minutes trying to talk to the Chinese leader without an interpreter present, while most leaders exchanged handshakes and kept walking.

"It has become increasingly difficult for her to engage in formal or even informal conversations with Xi," said Kei Koga, an associate professor at Nanyang Technological University in Singapore, who sees Takaichi's intention as to still "project the image that she could still communicate with him despite the ongoing tensions."

At a press briefing Thursday afternoon, Takaichi said Japan's position on Taiwan remained unchanged as stated in the 1972 Japan-China Communique, adding that her leadership has been communicating with Chinese counterparts on rare earth export controls.

Takaichi's subdued reception signaled Beijing's lack of enthusiasm to repair ties with Tokyo, analysts said, potentially prolonging China's curbs on critical mineral exports that have gripped Japanese industries for the past year.

"China's frosty reception of Takaichi shows quite clearly that Beijing is not yet ready to forgive her over her remarks about Taiwan," Chan said, expecting the bilateral relationship to remain strained until Takaichi leaves office.

That could leave Japan grappling with continued restrictions on rare earth supplies from China next year, said Tianchen Xu, senior economist at the Economist Intelligence Unit.

Relations deteriorated sharply after Takaichi said in November 2025 that a Taiwan Strait crisis could constitute a situation threatening Japan's survival and warrant a self-defense response.

While the substance broadly aligned with Japan's existing security policy, previous prime ministers had generally avoided stating it so explicitly in a Taiwan scenario.

China reacted angrily and demanded that Takaichi retract the remarks. She has not done so, while stating that the door to dialogue with Beijing remains open, with minimal high-level contact over the past year.

China has since tightened restrictions affecting Japan, including controls on dual-use and rare earth exports with potential military uses. It also discouraged its citizens from traveling to Japan and halted Japanese seafood imports.

Takaichi's visit marked the first trip to China by a Japanese prime minister since Shinzo Abe traveled to Chengdu in December 2019 for a trilateral summit with Chinese and South Korean leaders.

No formal Takaichi-Xi meeting has been publicly scheduled during her visit. Meanwhile, Xi is expected to join Trump and Putin for a historic trilateral gathering on Thursday, alongside a bilateral meeting with Trump.

Takaichi needs to show a "reflection of what she said about Taiwan a year ago," said Huiyao Wang, founder of the Beijing-based Center for China and Globalization, which is often seen as close to Beijing's thinking.

Wang said Tokyo should show greater flexibility, pointing to Trump's comments that the U.S. would avoid getting involved in the Taiwan issue. He argued that Japan holding firm on the issue would not help relations with Beijing.

Such reflection, he said, "would really help to clarify some of those misunderstandings between the two countries."
