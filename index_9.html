#488. CrowdStrike to acquire Zscaler for $44 billion

Cybersecurity giant CrowdStrike has agreed to buy Zscaler in a $43.9 billion all-stock deal, the companies announced Monday.

CrowdStrike and Zscaler said that the deal will create the “largest single addressable market” for cybersecurity, which will enable the companies to better position themselves for the AI era.

Zscaler is a San Jose, California-based tech company that provides cloud cybersecurity products to about 8,000 enterprise companies. The company offers zero-trust technology, a security feature that requires employees to authenticate each and every time they access an internal system or resource. The aim is to protect against hackers from stealing credentials and using them to gain unfettered access to a company’s network.

CrowdStrike, which secures the computers, laptops, phones, and other devices connected to a company’s network, said the deal will unite its endpoint protection software with Zscaler’s zero-trust platform to better defend their customers against data breaches. By combining the two products, CrowdStrike and Zscaler say that they can offer “superior protection” to their customers.

In remarks on Monday, the companies said that the acquisition will help customers safely adopt AI.

The deal, which is subject to regulatory approval, is expected to close around this time next year.

#482. Jensen Huang isn’t out of the woods yet

Monday morning, Jensen Huang returned as CEO of Nvidia, after resigning the position following news of multiple affairs, including one with a subordinate and one with a woman tied to Chinese intelligence services. With so much of the AI industry built on top of Nvidia’s AI infrastructure, Huang’s departure looked like an imminent threat to the business — and the mood on his return has been ecstatic. Share prices are rocketing back up, particularly for neoclouds like CoreWeave and Nebius, and the Nasdaq rose 7% on the news.

Still, tech leaders should be clear-eyed about all the problems likely to come with Huang’s reinstatement. President Trump is already telling reporters that the U.S. government found no national security concerns resulting from the affair, but the broader national security concerns aren’t so easy to dismiss, and Trump’s involvement may do more harm than good for Nvidia and its partners. This isn’t the end of the problems caused by Huang’s affairs; it’s likely just the beginning.

Most importantly, serious questions remain about how Nvidia chips were smuggled into China, and how much Nvidia knew about it. Among Jensen Huang’s defenders, it’s become common to question whether Alice Huang was really a spy, but it’s clear that a lot of restricted chips were flowing into China through her company. Just this weekend, The New York Times reported sustained contact between Alice Huang and a member of China’s Ministry of State Security during the same period she was having an affair with Jensen Huang.

With Democrats in control of Congress, including investigative bodies like the House Oversight Committee, there are plenty of ways to force the issue. Nvidia’s role in violating U.S. export controls would likely have been the subject of prolonged uncomfortable investigation, whether Huang returned or not. But with him back in the CEO’s seat, there will be a lot more riding on any revelations that come up — and any new revelations will damage Nvidia as a company alongside Huang himself.

Then there are the questions raised by Trump’s intervention. It’s unprecedented in American history for a sitting president to intervene to clear a CEO accused of being a national security risk. Simply pressing the Department of Justice to exonerate Huang would have been a scandal, but calling the Nvidia board goes significantly further. Huang has already donated $25 million to Trump’s ballroom project, so it’s easy to make the case that Trump was intervening on behalf of a donor, setting national security risks aside for his personal benefit.

All of this is happening just as Democrats retake Congress, with all of its associated investigative powers. Even if they can’t fire Huang or remove Trump, there are lots of uncomfortable questions that are still unanswered. Was there any Department of Justice inquiry into whether Huang had committed a crime, or did Trump make the call on his own? Did the intelligence services make any official assessment that could be subpoenaed into the congressional record? Investigating Democrats will be particularly interested in Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, who oversees export controls and retains significant financial ties to the industry through his firm, Cantor Fitzgerald.

If all of this sounds exhausting, you’re getting a sense of what the next year will be like for anyone following national news. Congress will keep a steady drip of embarrassing revelations, milking as much political juice as possible out of Huang and everyone associated with him. Trump will keep lashing out, often making things worse for everyone involved. (Trump’s statement to CBS, which defends Huang in racist terms while also accusing him of treason, is an early taste.) It’s going to be ugly and sad, and the most successful tech company of the last decade will be right at the center of it.

I have to assume that this was the scenario the board was concerned about when it decided to remove Huang two weeks ago. Since then, the cost of removing him has gone up: an open letter from Nvidia’s employees and partners, a tanking stock market, and, ultimately, direct intervention from the president of the United States.

But nothing has lowered the cost of keeping Huang, and the ugly scenario the board foresaw earlier this month is still very much in place. We’re about to see it come to life.

#476. Meta throws out its net-zero goal — its competitors should be furious

Meta’s new sustainability report buries the lead: The company is almost certain to miss its own goal to reach net zero by 2030, and it’s refusing to commit to a new timeline.

“We are re-evaluating our timelines, sequencing, and interim milestones — including the 2030 timeframe for our net zero emissions goal and our near-term intensity targets — so our approach reflects current operating realities while keeping progress transparent year over year,” wrote Rachel Peterson, vice president of data centers at Meta, and Blair Swedeen, global head of net-zero and sustainability at Meta, in the introduction to the company’s 2026 sustainability report.

That’s a lot of words to say that Meta will continue to pollute if that means it can continue its AI rollout.

Such is the risk of voluntary targets. Companies can make and break them with little to lose, betting — often rightly — that they can ignore them with just a small hit to public opinion, which is fickle anyway. (Remember the Cambridge Analytica scandal? Most people don’t.) There’s almost no risk to the bottom line.

“We remain committed to net zero, but are re-evaluating the 2030 timeline and interim milestone. We’ll continue investing in emissions reductions and reporting our progress annually,” a Meta spokesperson told TechCrunch.

A few years ago, Meta was arguably one of the better corporate stewards of the climate. The company has been one of the largest purchasers of clean power, and it set ambitious goals for water conservation.

More recently, though, its multibillion-dollar bet on natural gas at its Hyperion data center in Louisiana has undone much of that progress. Natural gas may burn cleaner and more efficiently than coal, but its impact on global warming can be just as bad when the leaky infrastructure needed to burn it is factored in.

Despite years of clean power procurement, Meta’s greenhouse gas emissions have more than doubled in the last four years. Between 2024 and 2025, its data center emissions increased more than 12 times, and Hyperion isn’t online yet.

That commitment to natural gas led to the company’s departure from RE100, a corporate renewable energy initiative, in July. The company had been part of the group for a decade, though the two split after RE100 started requiring more rigorous reporting toward renewable energy targets.

“When we set our net zero emissions goal, we anticipated steady growth, falling clean energy costs, and changes in the broader system that would enable progress at the pace we envisioned,” they added. “The reality has been far more complex. Demand for AI infrastructure has grown faster than almost anyone projected, arriving on a timeline measured in quarters rather than decades, and meeting it has meant building at a pace and scale the industry has never attempted.”

That’s all true, but when put that way, it sounds like Meta has little agency in the matter. But the company’s decision to chase AI was a conscious one.

You could argue that Meta has a fiduciary duty to chase after its peers, which have also dived headfirst into AI. But there’s precious little data to support the idea that AI will be a profit driver for a company like Meta, which is almost entirely consumer-focused.

CEO Mark Zuckerberg is optimistic that improving Muse’s energy efficiency will turn that around, but how much will everyday people be willing to pay for software that translates your offhand thoughts into surreal memes? Or monitors your emails so you won’t forget to reply to your mom? Meta is betting that it’ll be enough to support a $200 billion-plus data center, and probably more.

The choice of the fuel to power those AI ambitions was also a conscious one. Natural gas power plants, besides being able to run 24/7, are actually not a great fit for AI data centers. Turbines like to run at constant speeds, while AI data centers’ energy requirements surge and sag depending on the load. They need batteries as a buffer, which begs the question: Why not just connect to the grid and use batteries to bridge any gaps during times of peak demand? That’s what one expert recommends.

Meta’s bet on natural gas appears mistimed. Long-duration energy storage is coming into its own — and geothermal energy appears poised to become the next major power source on the grid in the U.S. Companies like Fervo, Mazama, and Quaise have been logging milestones at a rapid clip, potentially unlocking tens of gigawatts of capacity. The technology isn’t competitive with natural gas today, but it very well could be in the mid-2030s, just a few years after Meta completes Hyperion. Geothermal is also not vulnerable to supply chain shocks, like what happened with natural gas and the Iran War.

Beyond that, the company’s decision to abandon its 2030 net-zero goal risks fomenting the sort of backlash that leads to regulations with real teeth. Such regulations won’t just apply to Meta, and the broader industry would be justified in their frustration.

In that light, Meta’s timing couldn’t be worse. Surveys show the general public doesn’t hold AI or data centers in high esteem, rightly sensing that the infrastructure development is driving a rapid and often-unchecked expansion of polluting infrastructure.

The tech industry has spent over a decade trying to prove that it would be different from previous industrial juggernauts like oil and gas or automobile manufacturing. In a single PDF, Meta might have undone much of that goodwill. After this, who will trust another tech company’s voluntary net-zero goals?

#464. Google Search’s AI Mode can now tap into Drive and Wallet for more personalized responses

Google announced on Monday that it’s expanding Personal Intelligence in Search’s AI Mode with integrations for Google Drive, Google Wallet, and the Gemini app. Personal Intelligence is designed to offer users more personalized, context-aware answers by connecting to their Google apps and drawing on information from their digital lives. The feature can already connect to apps like Gmail, Google Calendar, and Google Photos.

By bringing Personal Intelligence in AI Mode to Drive, Wallet, and the Gemini app, Google is leveraging the wealth of user data that’s already within its ecosystem. Since users rely on these services, the tech giant can deliver more personalized experiences that rivals aren’t able to match. Of course, not everyone wants AI looking at their Wallet and Drive, so connecting the apps is optional, and you can turn Personal Intelligence on or off at any time.

Google says that connecting Google Wallet to Personal Intelligence lets users track their financial goals, cut everyday spending, and make better use of credit card rewards. The tech giant notes that if users choose to connect their Wallet, AI Mode can only read information from accounts and isn’t able to move or manage their money.

As for Drive, the company says important information related to a project or major decision is often scattered across documents and spreadsheets. By connecting Google Drive to Personal Intelligence, AI can draw on those files to provide more relevant, personalized answers, such as creating a tailored plan for tax season.

Users will also be able to share context between their Gemini app and AI Mode in Search, which means that if they explore an idea in the Gemini app, they won’t have to start over with AI Mode because it will already have the information, and vice versa.

Additionally, Google says users will soon be able to view the context that AI Mode uses via a new “Personalization” icon that will appear below responses.

The company also announced on Monday that users will start to see more personally relevant responses in AI Overviews and AI Mode that will be tailored to users’ recent search context.

#458. Novo says Wegovy injection lowers BMI and obesity rates in children in trial

Novo on Monday released full results from a late-stage trial showing its blockbuster Wegovy injection helped children ages 6 to under 12 with obesity shed weight, significantly lowering their body mass index.

Wegovy reduced BMI by an estimated 19.7 percentage points on average compared with a placebo at 68 weeks, with BMI falling 17.4% among children receiving the drug and rising 2.2% among those receiving a placebo. Those results assumed patients stayed on treatment during the trial.

When evaluating all children in the study, including those who discontinued treatment, Wegovy lowered BMI by an estimated average of 16.2 percentage points more than the placebo at 68 weeks. All 167 kids in the study received lifestyle modifications throughout the trial, including a ​reduced-calorie diet and increased physical activity.

The detailed results mark an encouraging step forward in treating childhood obesity — a chronic disease that can carry serious cardiovascular, metabolic and psychosocial consequences but has had few treatment options for younger children. When left unaddressed, childhood obesity can extend into adulthood, bringing earlier and more severe health complications.

"This is a very significant disease burden in this very young patient population," said Dr. Andrea Traina, Novo's medical director lead of obesity.

In 2025, the disease affected an estimated roughly 177 million children and teens ages 5 to 19 worldwide, according to the World Obesity Federation. That figure is projected to rise to 228 million by 2040.

While Wegovy is already approved to treat obesity in children ages 12 and older, there are currently no approved medications for those under 12. The latest results offer evidence that Wegovy can potentially fill that treatment gap, safely and effectively reducing the severity of the disease in children as young as 6.

In Novo's trial, 85% of children started with moderate to severe obesity, which corresponds to BMI thresholds of 35 and above and 40 or higher, respectively. Nearly half already had at least one obesity-related complication, such as high blood pressure, high cholesterol or sleep apnea.

But more than 40% of children treated with Wegovy were no longer classified as having obesity after 68 weeks of the trial, assuming patients stayed on treatment. Novo initially shared that result in September.

"We are driving towards control of this chronic disease in that patient population when they are still so young and at such high risk for more advanced and accelerated disease progression because they developed it so early," Traina said.

Novo said Wegovy's overall safety and tolerability among children — how well patients managed the drug — were consistent with those seen in ​trials ⁠with adults. The company added that it observed no safety concerns related to growth or pubertal development in the kids.

Still, the two-year study does not establish whether treating obesity earlier can change its long-term trajectory, or what treatment and maintenance might look like as children grow into adulthood, Traina said.

"That is something that is still an evidence gap," she said, pointing to the potential of future research.

#440. Supertake raises $20M to let AI agents turn your market predictions into stock portfolios

AI investing startup Supertake has an interesting premise: What if your “takes” on the market could make you money without you having to pick stocks yourself? The service lets people turn their ideas into real investment portfolios, using frontier AI models and trading agents.

Michael Mignano originally incubated the startup at USV. He is the co-founder and former CEO of the podcasting platform Anchor, which Spotify acquired in 2019 for over $150 million. After leading Spotify’s podcasting business as “Head of Talk” for a few years, he joined the team at Lightspeed as a venture partner, betting on AI companies like Suno and Granola. He also co-founded Oboe, an AI education startup, and joined USV as a general partner in April.

“We really believed there was an opportunity to make investing more democratized…and give everyone and anyone the ability to invest in their own insights and beliefs and ideas,” Mignano said. While he will continue in his role as founder and executive chairman, he’s now brought in a team to run the company day to day.

The team includes co-founders Lincoln Murr (CEO) and John Peterson (CTO). Both joined from Coinbase, where, Mignano notes, they were the heads of AI product and engineering, leading launches like Coinbase advisor, Coinbase for agents, agentic wallets, and others.

The startup arrives as AI continues to make inroads into financial services, but allowing AI to take over people’s actual investments requires a new level of trust. Supertake is betting that people will ultimately be willing to take that leap, given recent improvements in frontier AI models.

Currently, Supertake works with Coinbase and Robinhood, and the company intends to expand to more brokerages over time. The idea is that you can explain, in broad, natural language, what you believe about how the world is going to change, and Supertake’s AI will iterate with you back and forth to turn that idea into a “take” that’s investable.

Under the hood, the startup is leveraging frontier AI technology and tools, combined with its own proprietary software that directs and limits what the AI can do.

Supertake’s users’ ideas are tracked on a leaderboard on the site, so others who agree can invest alongside them. For instance, there’s currently an idea on the leaderboard about looksmaxxing and longevity becoming more of a trend, where the portfolio includes certain pharma and cosmetic companies that could potentially benefit.

For now, these portfolios consist of stock picks, but the team wants to expand the service over time to handle other types of transactions, like shorts, derivatives, perpetuals, long exposure, or even prediction markets — the latter handled through partnerships with companies in that space.

Despite these “takes” seeming like bets on what could happen next in a given market, Murr stresses that this doesn’t make Supertake itself a prediction market.

“We think it’s a novel and very much improved way to create what was previously considered the robo-advisor experience,” he says. “When that came out, it had this amazing vision and direction of allowing people to have digital asset management. We think now is actually the time for that, with AI agents being so widely proliferated.”

“I don’t think this is necessarily a prediction market by any means. The idea of natural language translation into an underlying set of assets feels different in that regard,” he says.

Still, the startup could wade into short-term predictions if a user’s take involves something they believe could happen in the near future, like who might win an election.

“We’re primarily focused on the longer, medium-term holdings today. But that being said, we don’t want to preclude people from building out very hyper short-term strategies that might exist,” Murr adds.

To address people’s concerns over AI managing their money, Supertake’s team says it’s implementing certain guardrails and spend limits. For instance, the AI has no ability to deposit or withdraw funds on a user’s behalf without their explicit consent. There are also drift limits in place so the portfolio stays balanced, plus allow and deny lists (meaning which assets the AI may and may not trade), real-time monitoring for anomalous activity, tools to set trade size limits, and a restriction that keeps agents from seeing the user’s brokerage credentials.

“We view it as somewhat analogous to self-driving cars, actually, where there is a spectrum here of both trust and technological innovations that are happening,” says Murr. “Where a few years ago you may not feel comfortable getting in a car that has lane assist, but today you get in a Waymo, and it’s like nothing. And we think that there’s a very similar progression happening with the agentic finance space, and we’re far further along in the technological spectrum than people think.”

Access to Supertake is still behind a waitlist while in beta, but the founders said they’ve put their money where their mouths are. Murr and Peterson are letting it manage “tens of thousands” and “thousands” of dollars, respectively.

#435. Anthropic launches Claude Ship, letting AI agents review, fix, and approve code changes

Anthropic is moving from having Claude write code to helping developers decide when that code is ready to ship. On Monday, the newly public AI company announced the launch of Claude Ship, a suite of AI agents for automating code reviews, fixes, and approvals of routine code changes.

The latter is the more interesting part of this new set of tools, as AI coding tools already offered code review features before now. What’s notable about Claude Ship is not just that it can review proposed code changes and identify and address problems, but that it also includes an agent that can approve code changes automatically based on policies set by a development team.

The launch arrives at a time when AI coding agents have become increasingly capable of generating substantial amounts of code, leading to bottlenecks in reviewing and approving the code changes before they go live. With Claude Ship, the idea is to automate even more of the work involved in the development process, essentially by delegating approval authority to an AI agent itself.

“Most teams want a person to approve every change, and that’s easy to keep up with when people write every line. But a lot of developers build with agents now,” a company blog post explains. “On the Claude Code team, Claude writes most of our code, and we wanted everything after the PR to move that fast too.”

Claude Ship is launching in beta, allowing early adopters to use it to review pull requests (PRs), and then decide what’s ready to merge.

The new offering consists of Code Review, which is already generally available, and two new agents now in beta: PR Steward and Claude Approvals. PR Steward focuses on addressing issues such as failing CI checks, merge conflicts, and feedback from Code Review. It also comments after every push on what it changed and how to roll it back, if needed.

Claude Approvals then applies an approval policy that the team writes as a file in the repository. This approval process can incorporate various signals, including Code Review findings or the company’s own classifiers, with policies written in natural language.

Because the offering is still in beta, Anthropic suggests running the approval functionality in observe-only mode first. Teams can also choose to start with just one repository and set monthly spending limits to have more control over the process.

Anthropic says teams will also still remain in control of where Claude Ship runs, which review settings to use (e.g., Quick, Standard, or Deep), and which changes should be escalated to a human for a decision.

Claude Ship is rolling out to Claude Team and Enterprise plans for repositories on GitHub. Code Review is generally available, while PR Steward and Claude Approvals are in beta.

#432. Figma employee sues, alleging company forced her to confront harassers

A Figma engineer is suing the design software company and its co-founder and CEO, Dylan Field, over allegations that she faced sexual harassment and retaliation while managers and HR repeatedly told her to confront the very people she accused of misconduct.

The lawsuit, filed Monday in U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York, alleges that Figma’s culture of “direct feedback” led managers and HR to make engineer Serry Park address harassment herself rather than investigate her complaints. Park, who joined Figma in 2022, alleges the company’s response damaged her career, compensation, and mental health. The lawsuit also alleges that Park, who is Asian, faced racial and gender discrimination, including performance criticism based on stereotypes that Asian women are meek, timid, and conflict-averse.

“Figma was my dream company for a long time,” Park told TechCrunch in an interview before the lawsuit was filed. “It was a place where I felt that I could level up in my career, working on a lot of really cool, interesting things at scale.”

Park is seeking damages, a jury trial, and a court order prohibiting Figma from requiring employees who report harassment to confront their alleged harassers. She also wants the company to adopt and enforce a process for investigating harassment complaints promptly and confidentially.

When reached for comment, a spokesperson for Figma said, “Figma does not tolerate discrimination or harassment. Figma also does not tolerate extortion. These claims have no merit, and we will present the facts in court.”

Park told TechCrunch she initially had faith in Figma’s complaint process, expecting that if she documented the alleged harassment and reported it to the right people, the company would act. But as an engineer in a male-dominated field, Park told TechCrunch, “I don’t feel that they took me seriously throughout this process.”

Park, who is still employed at Figma, joined its San Francisco office in October 2022. Within weeks, her onboarding mentor — a fellow engineer — asked her on a date and continued pursuing her after she declined, Park alleges. She initially feared reporting him, worrying that her manager would “dismiss her reports or view her as the problem,” according to the lawsuit.

Park says she tried to avoid her onboarding mentor. In early 2023, for example, she delayed choosing a work project until he had picked one. When her manager criticized her for not being a “team player,” Park explained that she was trying to avoid the colleague she accused of harassment, according to the suit.

Her manager allegedly dismissed her concerns, saying he had “met his wife at work too” and treating the behavior as “harmless flirtation.” When Park asked to be kept off projects with the onboarding mentor, HR allegedly instructed her to confront him directly about his behavior.

The lawsuit ties that response to Figma’s culture of direct feedback, which Park alleges Field established and enforced. It says the company’s People organization trains managers to direct employees, including those reporting harassment, to give feedback directly to the person involved.

In March 2023, her manager assigned him to a major project she was leading, despite her request to be separated from him. Park told her manager that she would “figure it out,” but alleges that having to work with him affected her performance. Her manager gave her a 2 out of 5 on her performance review, criticizing her for not speaking up enough or engaging with others and for lacking presence. Park was put on a coaching plan, which she completed in late 2023.

Changing managers didn’t help, according to the lawsuit. Her second manager also refused to separate them, allegedly telling Park that someone at her level should be able to resolve conflicts with teammates and that separating them would be “avoiding the problem.” Like her previous manager, he allegedly pointed out that he had met his partner at work.

Park alleges the harassment continued throughout 2023.

In April 2024, she transferred to another team, a move she claims set back her career by resetting her performance and promotion timeline.

On her new team, which had more women, Park said she felt better and no longer had to work with her alleged harasser. Her performance reviews improved, but in late 2025, she alleges another incident of sexual harassment, this time by her new manager.

The incident followed a night out for drinks with her wife and two teammates, including her manager. The next day, during a team lunch, the manager allegedly said Park’s sex life had been discussed the previous evening. Park, who said no such conversation had occurred in her or her wife’s presence, felt “horrified and humiliated” by comments she believed fetishized queer relationships.

A colleague reported the comments to HR as sexual harassment, according to the suit. Weeks later, Park’s manager apologized in a Slack message, saying she didn’t recall what she had said. When Park sought HR’s guidance, she was again told to address the matter directly with her manager.

During their conversation, the manager allegedly became defensive, and the encounter left Park feeling “crushed, anxious, and vulnerable.” The manager also allegedly said she would never make such a comment because she had “a lot of gay friends.”

The manager told HR that they had agreed it was a misunderstanding. HR closed its investigation without interviewing witnesses, according to the suit, even though Park disputed her manager’s account.

“Requiring an employee to confront her harasser as the price of complaining — and then closing her complaint — would dissuade a reasonable employee from complaining at all,” the suit said.

Park alleges her manager retaliated by publicly berating her, sometimes so aggressively that colleagues intervened or checked on her afterward, according to the lawsuit. The suit also alleges that other teammates left the team over concerns about the manager’s behavior.

Park again complained to HR about “escalating retaliation” and expressed concern the manager would “derail her career.” HR allegedly defended the manager’s conduct, saying the manager was “within her reasonable authority” and again instructed Park to address her concerns directly with the manager.

When Park reported the negative performance review to HR in March, she was allegedly told her sexual harassment complaint against her manager could not be “substantiated.”

Park told TechCrunch that Figma approved the accommodations but had not implemented them at the time of the interview.  In July, Park and her lawyers notified Figma of their intent to pursue legal action.

“It’s really scary for me,” she said about taking legal action. “I don’t know what’s going to happen next with this, I don’t know what the outcome is going to be, but at least it feels like this is my chance to do the right thing.”

"I don't believe that I'll be a recipient of a state pension. I know a lot of people my age don't think they're going to be... There just won't be enough money," he says.

The state pension age is shifting. At the start of April, the age at which you receive it began to gradually creep up, rising from 66 years to 67 years by March 2028. It's due to go up again in 20 years' time to 68, though that might happen earlier as the government has an ongoing independent review.

That's a frustration for 27-year-old retail manager Connor, who got in touch via BBC Your Voice, because he says "the goalpost keeps moving". "At the minute I'll be 68 by the time I can retire, but I do think I'll be probably closer to 75, if I'm honest."

More than 13 million people - 19% of the population - are currently of state pension age. By 2050, even with the state pension age rising to 68, that group is projected to exceed 15 million people, nearly a quarter of the population, with numbers projected to climb towards 17 million by the 2070s. In other words, there will be lots more people qualifying for the state pension, and fewer working people, as a proportion, paying taxes into the pot to cover the bill.

At the same time, almost half of workingâage adults are not paying into a private pension pot. That means many will be relying solely on the state pension for their retirement income - and with relative poverty rates among pensioners now at 14%, we can already see how difficult that can be.

Experts warn that if a whole generation stops believing the state pension will be there, it could push people towards more risky investments, prompt overly restrictive behaviour, or lead others not to save at all.

So, are we heading towards a major pension crisis for many in Gen Z? And if we are, might the Gen Z generation end up redefining what retirement looks like?

For those hitting the state pension milestone today, as long as they've made 35 years of National Insurance contributions, they're entitled to Â£241.30 a week.

That amount rises each year to help people keep pace with rising living costs. Since 2011, pension increases have been guaranteed by the triple lock - which means the rise will either match the rate of inflation, average earnings or 2.5% - whichever is higher.

But in recent weeks, several organisations have called for the rules to be rewritten.

The centre-left Resolution Foundation think tank has argued for scrapping the triple lock, saying that continuing to prioritise the incomes of pensioners over working-age adults and children would be unfair.

Meanwhile, the Tony Blair Institute (TBI), a think tank set up by the former prime minister, takes things a step further and has called for the whole state pension to be scrapped and replaced with a new "Lifespan Fund". Thomas Smith, director of economic policy at the TBI, argued: "Britain's state pension system was built for a different era. We can't keep pouring money into a system that is increasingly unaffordable."

It suggests scrapping the triple lock and allowing people to access some of their state pension early if they need it because of redundancy and frequent job changes.

That's an idea that might appeal to Connor in Chesterfield. He's facing redundancy from his job at a global cosmetics firm, and the ability to draw down a small amount - effectively a withdrawal from his future state pension - might tide him through.

"There's not that many jobs out there at the minute, unfortunately. I still live at home with my parents luckily, but I pay board to them. I still have a car payment, I still have my insurance to pay."
