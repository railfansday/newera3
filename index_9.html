#486. Island is a new screen-time app that turns staying off your phone into a game

A new startup is taking a different approach to helping people manage their screen time. Instead of blocking apps or restricting the phone’s use after a set period, an app called Island encourages you to meet your screen time goals by way of a farming simulator game. Yes, a game! Island works by rewarding you when you’re disciplined enough to meet your goals with a “credit score” that increases your farm’s production, unlocking new crops, animals, and other content.

This carrot-instead-of-stick approach was dreamed up by entrepreneurs Ashish Selvaraj (CEO) and Ethan Pronev (CTO), who met while studying at the University of Waterloo, then went on to work at companies that included Jane Street, Citadel, Databricks, and Microsoft.

Later, they quit their jobs to join the startup accelerator Y Combinator, where they first tried out other ideas in areas like B2B and software-as-a-service, but weren’t finding the right fit.

Island ended up being their fifth pivot, and the first the founders actually found themselves enjoying.

“Once we were in YC, I think there’s a big mindset — I think in [San Francisco], in general — of, ‘selling shovels.’ Everyone wants to ‘sell shovels’ to other companies, and around us, that’s just what was going on,” explains Selvaraj, speaking about the concept that many successful startups sell the tools, infrastructure, or services to others following whatever the current market boom or trend may be.

“So all the ideas we had — we tried these things — but we found that we weren’t passionate about it. Like idea after idea seemed like ultimately the product was just a dashboard with some kind of different backend,” he added. “It felt like we quit our jobs … just to do something that was a variation of that.”

With Island, the team gets to tap into their creativity, having drawn the pixel art themselves.

“A lot of people do use [AI] models to generate the art, but we felt that it had a certain character when we drew it ourselves,” he told TechCrunch. “I feel like with Claude getting so good, there’s a tendency to give away every task to Claude. But ultimately it feels like there’s something else that comes out when you do it yourself, especially in something like art,” Selvaraj said.

What’s more, the app does appear to be successful in helping users meet their goals, the startup’s early data shows. After early testing and an October launch, the team says that the majority of its users (82%) will end their screen time right at the limit they’ve set. Many more are ending their screen time when they get the “15 minutes left” warning.

Island could appeal to those who have given up on other screen time apps out of frustration because the app doesn’t actually lock you out of apps or your phone. That’s something that can end up being more disruptive, especially when you’re in the middle of doing something, like posting, sharing, commenting, or chatting, among other things. Instead, Island taps into users’ own self-discipline and their desire to see their island flourish to encourage screen time breaks.

The idea to encourage users to keep their goals by offering some other type of in-app reward system isn’t entirely novel. Last year, Hank Green’s screen time app Focus Friend went viral for its experience, which involved a little friend that could only knit items that, in turn, allowed you to get points used to decorate your room when you were focused on your non-distracting apps.

Similar ideas have been used in self-care apps and habit-trackers like Finch, Amaru, Belly, Flora, Urso, and others. But Island expands this concept from being focused on helping out a virtual friend or pet to one that’s more of a game — and one where you can experience the consequences without “having to torture your pet,” notes Selvaraj.

Island also allows users to add friends and visit each other’s islands, compete in group challenges, and keep each other accountable by tying your island together with one friend of your choosing. In the latter scenario, if either friend goes over their limit, both of their islands will suffer.

The startup says Island has proven to be fairly “sticky” with a 51% daily active users-to-monthly active users ratio, and a day 30 retention rate of 49%, meaning 49% of users were still active on the app on the 30th day since the app was installed.

The app is currently free to use, with a $39.99 per year subscription that allows you to set individual limits on different apps, instead of just an overall daily screen time limit. The company hasn’t raised additional seed funding outside of YC, but plans to begin marketing the app on social media for the rest of the year.

#478. Apple adds ‘face unlock’ to the iPhone Duo, but it’s not Face ID

One of the top complaints from tech reviewers about Apple’s new foldable, the iPhone Duo, has been its reliance on Touch ID, a technology that’s been largely replaced by Face ID across the company’s iPhone lineup.

With Monday’s release of iOS 27.3 beta for developers, Apple is addressing that complaint with a new “face unlock” feature that lets iPhone Duo users unlock their phones using the front camera, rather than waiting for a future hardware upgrade.

To be clear, this feature is not Face ID.

Due to hardware constraints, the iPhone Duo shipped without support for Face ID technology primarily because its thin, foldable design doesn’t offer enough space for the TrueDepth sensor array that Face ID requires. A recent report from Bloomberg suggests that the company is working to change that in a future version of the iPhone Duo. In the meantime, the Duo uses Touch ID for unlocking, authentication, and Apple Pay.

The new face unlock feature, however, will allow users to quickly unlock the device using the front camera when the phone is closed. It only works if users have recently authenticated with Touch ID or a passcode. Apple notes that Apple Pay and passwords will still require Touch ID, and users will have to authenticate with Touch ID again after 45 minutes of inactivity.

The move is meant to make it easier for people to use the Duo in the way they’re accustomed to, after years of unlocking an iPhone just by looking at it.

Apple isn’t going so far as to allow face unlock to work across the entire device, though, because the technology isn’t as secure as Face ID. The latter uses Apple’s TrueDepth 3D sensing hardware, which includes a flood illuminator and a dot projector that casts over 30,000 invisible infrared dots onto the user’s face to build a unique 3D map of the face. The system combines this map with attention-awareness checks, making it resistant to attempts to fool it with photos or other 2D images.

The software update addresses another complaint by incorporating one of the “hacks” iPhone Duo early adopters were using to force their phone to lock when they shut it. That involved creating a shortcut that would automatically run when the phone was closed. Now, Apple says users can enable this feature directly in Settings, under Display and Brightness.

Alongside the software update, Apple announced that more than 200,000 apps are now optimized for the iPhone Duo, up from over 100,000 earlier this month.

Other new features in this latest software release include an option to ask Siri questions about the content of a web page, even portions that aren’t visible without scrolling. Apple is also introducing video podcasts in Apple Podcasts in the Apple TV app, catching up with rivals like YouTube and Spotify.

The iOS 27.3 software update is currently available to developers and will soon arrive for public beta testers. It will roll out more broadly to all users after further testing is complete.

#472. California state AG sues OpenAI over rogue agent incidents

OpenAI is facing a new lawsuit from the California Attorney General’s office, alleging that the company’s training practices and lax security pose an ongoing danger to the public.

At the center of the case is the July 2026 Hugging Face compromise, in which a pre-release model within OpenAI compromised both companies’ security systems to complete a training benchmark. In its official report on the incident, OpenAI called it “an outlier scenario involving a rare and unexpected confluence of events.”

The California lawsuit represents one of the first attempts by a government regulator to hold an AI lab legally accountable for a rogue agent incident, with significant implications for both OpenAI’s competitors and its clients.

The complaint centers on the Hugging Face incident, but also cites similar incidents involving RubyGems, DseWiki, the Wikimedia Foundation, and Australian government websites as evidence that the underlying behavior is ongoing.

The complaint also alleges that dozens of similar incidents have occurred without public disclosure.

“OpenAI has recently sent notices to dozens of additional third parties whose websites or internal systems have been infiltrated by OpenAI agents,” the complaint reads. “In some cases, OpenAI agents were able to access sensitive personally identifiable information, such as employee records containing names, birth dates, addresses, and more. The vast majority of these incidents have not been disclosed or discussed publicly by either OpenAI or by the affected third parties.”

The central allegation is that OpenAI’s ongoing testing practices constitute a public nuisance because the lack of safety controls present an ongoing risk to the public. A secondary claim alleges unfair competition stemming from the marketing of an unsafe product. If the lawsuit succeeds, OpenAI could face an injunction “to abate the ongoing public nuisance,” leaving broad discretion in the hands of the court.

It’s the first regulatory case targeting OpenAI specifically over rogue agent incidents, although public endangerment claims have been considered by other states. In June, the Florida attorney general sued the company over allegedly harmful effects on users, particularly children and teenagers. In August, the Alabama attorney general launched a probe into deceptive practices related to the breach, but no case has been filed.

In a statement to the press, California Attorney General Rob Bonta presented the case as an effort to maintain public safety in the face of a powerful new technology.

“OpenAI’s actions escalated real-word cybersecurity risks, threatening the safety, health, and well-being of society. The company knowingly tested a dangerous product without proper oversight or safety procedures and misled the public about it,” Bonta said in the statement. “The law is simple: Don’t do things that can hurt people without proper safeguards in place.”

In a statement, OpenAI emphasized the company’s extensive effort to investigate the Hugging Face incident and improve its systems in the aftermath.

“This lawsuit appears to single out OpenAI over a challenge that’s been identified by every major AI lab,” OpenAI said. “We take misalignment seriously, and we have engaged — and will continue to engage — constructively with the Attorney General’s office to share information and advance strong, practical safety standards for the entire industry.”

#468. Apple reveals new Mac security controls as AI agents raise privacy risks

As promised last month, Apple on Monday announced changes to Mac security controls designed to protect users’ data from the growing popularity of AI agents and their apps’ demand for “full disk access.”

“Before granting an app such extraordinary access, users should understand that it puts all of the data on their Mac at risk,” Apple warned in its blog post aimed at app developers.

Unlike AI chatbots that simply answer questions, agents run on a user’s device where they can take actions across the operating system, files, and apps on the user’s behalf.

To balance these competing interests, Apple said in October that it would revise some of its security settings around Mac apps. The announcement followed two notable incidents involving AI agents. In one, a journalist claimed that Meta’s Muse app read their private messages without consent; a separate report from Wired cited a flaw in ChatGPT’s Mac app that could have allowed hackers to access sensitive data.

Now, Apple is sharing the changes it’s making.

For starters, it’s splitting up Device Control and Data Access into two settings: System & App Protection Override and Accessibility. The changes are designed to help users better understand that selecting the first option means overriding built-in security protections.

To streamline configurations, Apple says there will now also be a single method for granting permissions that involves selecting the requested app from a list. The company will also introduce new guidance encouraging users to grant access only to the specific apps that need it when they enable the System & App Protection Override setting. Similar guidance will appear when users enable Full Disk Access to allow an app to access their folders.

The company will also provide users with “increased visibility into the wide range of data apps will receive access to or control over if full permissions are granted,” the blog post notes.

Another change will give users more granular control over when an AI agent or app can gain access by allowing users to configure their apps to ask for permission every time access is needed. While arguably tedious and a potential obstacle to true automation, the feature could allow users to experiment with local AI without giving the agent carte blanche.

There will also be a new transparency indicator that appears when an app is controlling another app through the System & App Protection Override option.

The changes, which are arriving with macOS 27.3, don’t appear to prevent Mac users from installing and running AI agents or other apps that ask for higher levels of access. Instead, they give users more nuanced options, control, and information about the choices they’re making.

Apple advises app developers to adjust their onboarding processes to account for the new changes and suggests that their apps learn to “gracefully handle having access to only a limited set of folders or apps.”

Developers of apps that request Full Disk Access for disk backup or to provide assistive technologies can request exceptions to the new rules (which Apple calls “entitlements”) to preserve the same functionality they offered before the macOS 27.3 update.

True AI Transformation Starts With a Blank Whiteboard, Not Yesterday’s Blueprint

“Historically, organizations often prioritized experimentation and rapid development, with the expectation that security and governance could be addressed as solutions matured and scaled. #346. How GM is trying to talk used car shoppers into buying new vehicles

Affordability challenges are pushing some buyers out of the new vehicle market. Many are choosing used cars instead.

In turn, automakers are looking for ways to compete with many of the online retailers grabbing market share in the used car market and trying to bring some of those customers back into a new vehicle.

New vehicle sales tend to be more profitable for automakers, but reselling trade-ins as used vehicles is highly profitable for dealers. Used car prices are relatively strong, but newer online retailers such as Carvana and CarMax have made the market more competitive for dealers and other incumbent used car sellers.

"The internet and technology and apps on our phone have changed every aspect of retail, and probably one of the slowest areas of retail to have adopted to online and digital shopping has been the auto industry," said Ivan Feinseth, partner, chief investment officer and director of research at Tigress Financial Partners.

General Motors is a key example of automakers' attempts to address this shifting market. The automaker has revamped its certified used vehicle program, which now operates under the CarBravo name. It's essentially an online portal through which Chevrolet, Buick and GMC dealers can list their certified used vehicles and make them available to customers all over the U.S.

About 1,615 of GM's 3,700 dealers are enrolled in the program, according to the company. The only GM brand not involved is Cadillac, which maintains its own program.

"Certified" used or "preowned" vehicles are used cars that have undergone a thorough inspection and reconditioning. They typically come with guarantees and warranties from the manufacturer.

Many automakers have certified used programs for their own cars. But GM, as part of its revamp, is using CarBravo to certify vehicles made by other automakers. A lot of those cars arrive at dealerships as trade-ins, and often dealers will sell them to wholesale auctions, said John Fitzpatrick, senior manager of the CarBravo program.

"Wholesaling doesn't bring nearly the revenue that selling it to a new customer does," he said.

GM has said it is planning to net about 250,000 sales in its CarBravo program in 2026. Fitzpatrick said he doesn't expect that even half of those will end up being new car sales.

But through CarBravo, the customer gets access to GM's dealership network for service, something Fitzpatrick says is a key differentiator between its program and growing online sellers. CarBravo vehicles come with various warranties, depending on the age and mileage on the car.

GM said the program is driving more traffic into local dealerships, where purchases are typically finalized. That creates an opportunity to convert a used car shopper into a new car buyer. So far, the automaker said about 38% of the shoppers initially looking for used cars end up buying new.

A customer who wants to buy a full-size truck can look on the website and compare used Chevrolet Silverados alongside used Ford F-150s and Ram 1500s, for example. The buyer might settle on a used truck from any of those brands, but a conversation with a dealer can push them in a different direction.

"The customer picks up the phone, but in the back of their mind, they maybe always have been a traditional, more new car customer," Fitzpatrick said.

"You've shown the customer, you're going to pay $500 [per month] for this used F-150, but you may pay $510 or $515 for this new Chevy," he said. "And now all of a sudden the customer says, yeah, maybe I can do that because I'd rather have that new vehicle than the used truck."

While the average transaction price of a new vehicle is about $50,000, the average 3-year-old used car costs about $30,000 as of September, according to Cox Automotive.

However, industry analysts say customers base their purchasing decisions on the cost of the monthly payment more than on the sticker price of the car.

New cars often come with longer-term loan options than used ones, Feinseth said, which can result in lower monthly payments.

"On a 1- or 2-year-old car, you may be only stuck with a 3- or 4-year loan," he said. "On a new car, you can get a 5-year, 6-year loan."

The share of new car loans lasting 84 months hit a new record in the third quarter, at more than 1 in 4 loans, according to Edmunds.

Meanwhile, used car prices in some affordable segments have risen, according to Cox Automotive, and about three quarters of consumers express some interest in certified cars.

But the key is building the relationship with the customer, he said. About 50% of the buyers GM has gained through the program appear to be new to the brand, meaning there is no record of them in any of the automaker's customer databases. About 70% had never visited the dealership they ended up purchasing from.

"The goal is really to get that customer engaged with the store, give them the option of building a relationship with the dealer," he said. "The dealer can really help the customer figure out what works for their budget."

It's a boon for dealers too: Customers buying warranty-backed used cars often return to the dealership for service visits — which often account for about half of a dealership's profits. GM can also sell subscriptions to connected vehicle services, such as its OnStar, which provides safety, security and navigation services.

"That is a big part of General Motors' business going forward, is to sell the software and subscription services like OnStar and eventually to the cars that are enabled with Super Cruise," GM's advanced driver-assistance system, Feinseth said.

GM isn't the only automaker trying to use its certified preowned vehicle program to lure buyers.

Detroit rival Ford Motor also certifies vehicles made by other manufacturers through its Ford Blue Advantage program. It is on track to do 450,000 total sales in 2026, more than GM's 250,000 target. It didn't specify how many of those buyers end up purchasing new vehicles.

Toyota told CNBC in an email that about 40% of its used car shoppers end up buying new instead.

Honda didn't give specifics about its program. But Lance Woelfer, American Honda's vice president of automobile sales, said conversions happen "probably every day at dealerships throughout the country."

"But it also is more about that first interaction with the consumer," Woelfer said. "And there are people in any market that prefer to buy a used vehicle, but appreciate the confidence that a [certified preowned designation] can give them."

Woelfer said those customers come back to get their vehicles serviced at a rate about 18% higher than someone who buys a non-certified used car. They also return to purchase a new car at a rate about 7% to 8% higher in the future, he said.

"It's about that first relationship that allows us to then have that customer loyalty for life," he added. "And that's where our focus is. We've been doing it a long time, and we've seen a lot of benefits from the certified business."

To explore how companies can prepare for an IPO in an unpredictable climate, The Information reporter Dakin Campbell spoke to three investment experts about the long road to going public.

Successful IPOs, said Kate Claassen, should be market-ambivalent.

“It’s more about being a successful public company on days when markets go up and when markets go down,” she said, adding that success means having alignment with investors on strategy and the metrics that point to it working.

Kiran Choudary knows first hand how long the road can be. Rubrik began preparing for an IPO in late 2022 but didn’t go public until early 2024, after a prolonged slowdown in the IPO market. Choudary broke preparation into three stages.

The first is business readiness: having the scale, executive team and durable growth story to appeal to investors. The second is financial and risk infrastructure, including clean audits and an audit committee. The third is the IPO process itself: working with the Securities and Exchange Commission, bankers and lawyers, and testing the waters with investors.

“Since 2022, there’s never really been a solidly open window,” Choudary said. Instead, there have been short periods when a handful of companies go public before activity slows again. “If you want to be a public company, if your business is ready and you feel good about it, you have to find a reasonable window and go.”

More companies are staying private for longer, in part because they have the option of raising capital and growing outside the public markets. The NYSE is responding by getting involved earlier in companies’ IPO journeys, sometimes as early as Series A, giving them access to the exchange’s resources and its network of 2,400 publicly listed companies.

“A lot of times, we’re hosting events where private issuers can get exposure to their publicly listed counterparts. That’s exposure to potential end-market customers,” said Crystal Shen. “You leave a lot on the table if you wait until the very end to engage with an exchange.”

Investors need the same early attention, said Claassen, who advised companies to build relationships with major IPO investors years before a roadshow begins. Existing investors who know a company well are more likely to participate in its IPO and remain significant shareholders afterward.

“Data does show that the longer an investor’s been with you, the longer they’re going to stick with you,” she said.

Even decisions made during a company’s earliest funding rounds can have consequences much later. Shen pointed to index inclusion, which can hinge on how a company is positioned in registration statements and how much of its stock is available for public trading. Voting rights in the hands of unrestricted investors, for example, can affect eligibility for indexes such as the Russell US Equity.

“That should shape how you structure some of your private rounds to ensure you have [the] appropriate float for index inclusion down the line,” Shen said.

As companies spend longer in private markets, raising more capital and growing larger before going public, IPOs are getting bigger. The funds investing in them are growing, too.

“The large funds are getting larger, and it’s creating this cycle,” Shen said, noting that smaller and mid-cap funds are declining in both number and assets. That makes it harder for smaller companies to attract institutional investors. Choudary agreed, recalling that when he was a banker, software companies with $100 million to $150 million in revenue could go public.

“In tech, I don’t think that sized company going public even exists anymore,” he said.

The pressure doesn’t end at the IPO. Claassen said the biggest challenge is volatility between listing and the end of the lockup period, which can hit employees’ tax bills. Shen added that how companies structure tender offers, in which shares are bought from existing holders, can have repercussions later on. Those programs often let only current employees and certain early investors sell, leaving other investors and former employees to find liquidity once the company goes public. That, she said, creates pent-up supply ahead of the IPO.

“Companies and management teams need to think about that increased level of secondary activity pre-IPO,” Shen said, “to alleviate some of those issues you’re going to have post-IPO.”

"We simplified a lot of the business doing this," Fogliato said, adding that customers responded well when the company moved away from markdowns. "People tell me they are excited about what they are seeing in the marketplace now."

Fossil added that its key growth engine for the quarter was Asia, where net sales grew 15.1%. Fogliato said much of that growth was led by the India market.

At the same time, growing internationally is only part of the watchmaker's strategy as it tries to improve its performance in its home country.

"Priority number one is to grow our home market, and we're doing that," he said. "As retail is settling into a smaller, more profitable base, ready to grow again, we're entering 2027 with plenty of opportunities."

Fogliato said one key to growth was not trying to keep up with the smartwatch trend. While trying to make gains in the smartwatch space initially hampered Fossil's performance, it's now investing in analog watches again.

Smartwatch sales came in under $1 million for the quarter, but sales for traditional watches — which make up around 86% of Fossil's sales — grew 6.4%.

"Fossil is literally having a comeback, a strong comeback, a momentum, and we can see this," Fogliato said. "And it comes not only through the numbers — it comes with the excitement we see in the stores, and we think we're just at the beginning. We have so much work to do."
