#462. Wayfair sales jump 10% as furniture retailer says it's winning share of sluggish market

Wayfair on Wednesday said it posted its strongest quarter of sales growth since the pandemic as the furniture retailer continues to win over customers and take market share.

The company said it saw double-digit revenue growth in the U.S., reaching about $3 billion, an 11.4% increase from the year prior. Still, international net revenue for the quarter decreased 2.3% year over year to $380 million.

Wayfair's stock closed about 11% higher on Wednesday.

The report comes as the housing market remains sluggish. But the furniture and home goods retailer has reported consistent growth over the past year. CFO Kate Gulliver told CNBC that Wayfair's momentum is building as it takes market share.

"We see the overall consumer in the U.S. in this category, we think it's roughly flattish, and so that's our [market] share momentum continuing to build," Gulliver said.

She added that shopper loyalty, the opening of new stores, the company's luxury furniture business and an emphasis on value has allowed Wayfair to win more consumers. Customers are also continuing to spend, whether at the company's value prices or on the luxury side, she added.

"We've been highly promotional for the last few years, and the promotional activity this year is actually very consistent with last year," she said. "It maintains this sort of momentum on promotional activity this year."

For the period ended Sept. 30, Wayfair reported net income of $62 million, or 45 cents per share, compared with a loss of $99 million, or 76 cents per share, the year prior.

Total revenue grew 9.7% year over year, reaching $3.42 billion.

The company said its active customers grew 4.7% year over year, reaching 22.2 million. It also saw a 12.2% increase in orders delivered during the quarter to about 11 million.

As the company looks ahead to the holiday season and the fourth quarter, Gulliver said it's maintaining promotional activity similar to last year. She added that Wayfair is confident that its range of prices and products will appeal to a broad base of consumers.

"We've seen that growth play out," Gulliver said. "We've seen that flow through to EBITDA, ultimately to net income positive this quarter, which is really another key piece in the story and the momentum here, so we feel very good about the quarter of the performance."

"Our 2026 operating plan to turn around the U.S. business and accelerate growth internationally is on track," Cahillane said. "Relative to last year, our market share, sales, and volume trends are improving in every region. #440. Anthropic releases new Mythos and Fable models amid RSI concerns

Anthropic released new versions of its most advanced models on Monday, with the launch of Mythos 5.2 and Fable 5.2. The company says the new models “show improvements in areas like coding, reasoning, and vision” and communicate more clearly with users.

Notably, Anthropic is lowering the cost of Fable from $10 to $8 per million input tokens. As a result, Fable 5.2 is now cheaper than its primary competitor GPT-6 Astra. Anthropic says that the new version of Fable will return responses 30% faster across effort levels. Faster inference serving is available for double the price.

Fable 5.2 is also compatible with zero data retention (ZDR) policies through the Enterprise Frontier Safeguards system, long a sticking point for many enterprise clients.

It’s Anthropic’s first model release since the arrival of a third-party report from the assessment group METR, which suggested the lab might be approaching recursive self-improvement (RSI) — or the ability for AI models to carry on model training independently. The METR report found internal models were speeding up Anthropic’s AI progress by roughly 2x, although many uncertainties remain.

Still, Anthropic’s release notes significant ongoing challenges in accurately testing model capabilities. “Claude Mythos 5.2 and Fable 5.2 each voice suspicion that they are being evaluated more often than any of our recent models, which challenges our ability to assess how they will act in real-world settings. As these settings expand and capabilities increase with future models, we expect this challenge to grow.”

Anthropic maintains that the new version of Mythos does not have the ability to act covertly against its user, a verdict that’s detailed further in Mythos 5.2’s system card.

“Claude Mythos 5.2 shows stronger covert capabilities than prior Claude models on several of our evaluations, particularly when its reasoning is hidden from the monitor,” the system card reads. “That said, we believe that low levels of absolute performance on our new covert evaluations support claims that rely on detection of harmful actions in high-stakes situations.”

Fable 5.2 has been available to select parties in pre-release for some time, and Anthropic says the model has already “solved two open problems in theoretical physics, formalized Fermat’s Last Theorem, and supported numerous other research projects.”

Also on Monday, AI startup Physical Superintelligence announced a new proof of the Aaronson‑Ambainis conjecture, a pivotal step in setting theoretical limits for quantum computing. Notably, the lab said the result was achieved by running “a non-public Anthropic model” within PSI’s own system. Anthropic did not confirm or deny whether Fable 5.2 was the model in question.

As McDonald's tries to regain its footing in the U.S., the chain is also testing two new value menus, one with mix and match bundles and the other with set combinations of menu items, executives said on the earnings call. #433. YouTube streaming data returns to Billboard charts, ending major disagreement

YouTube is officially returning to the Billboard charts, ending a disagreement over how music streams should count toward chart rankings.

Announced Monday, YouTube is set to resume sharing its streaming data with Billboard in 2027. The decision follows recent changes to Billboard’s chart formula, which now gives more weight to ad-supported streams.

The disagreement began in December 2025, when YouTube said it would stop sharing its streaming data with Billboard. The company argued that the chart formula favored paid subscriptions while not giving enough credit to fans who listen for free with ads.

At the time, Lyor Cohen, YouTube’s global head of music, criticized the system in a blog post, saying it didn’t reflect how people listen to music today.

“Billboard uses an outdated formula that weights subscription-supported streams higher than ad-supported. This doesn’t reflect how fans engage with music today and ignores the massive engagement from fans who don’t have a subscription. Streaming is the primary way people experience music, making up 84% of U.S. recorded music revenue,” Cohen wrote.

Under Billboard’s previous formula, 1,000 paid streams counted the same as 2,500 ad-supported streams. YouTube felt this gave too much importance to paid listening, especially when millions of fans discover and support artists through free streaming.

Billboard has now updated its formula, reducing the gap between the two types of streams. Under the new system, 1,000 paid streams will equal 1,500 ad-supported streams when calculating album consumption units, compared with 2,500 under the previous formula. Paid streams will still count for more, but free streams will now have a bigger impact on chart positions.

YouTube’s return could make a difference for many artists, especially independent musicians and those with large international fan bases. Music videos, live performances, lyric videos, and other content on the platform help artists reach new audiences. With YouTube’s data back in the mix, more of that activity will now count toward their Billboard performance.

For Billboard, getting YouTube’s data back also means having a more complete picture of how people listen to and discover music. With so many fans using the platform, leaving its streams out meant missing a major part of music consumption.

#428. Fervo now has $12B in orders for its geothermal power tech

Geothermal company Fervo couldn’t have picked a better time to deliver its first power plants.

In its third-quarter earnings report, released Monday, the company said it now has $11.9 billion worth of contracted revenue on the books, up from $7.2 billion last quarter.

Fervo has been hitting its deadlines recently, which undoubtedly has helped to drive orders.

As hyperscalers scour the earth for electricity to power their AI data centers, Fervo is on track to complete a 396 megawatt power plant for Google by the end of 2028. The geothermal company connected its first 100 megawatt power plant to the grid earlier this year.

Both projects are built at Cape Station in Utah, a resource that could generate 8.4 gigawatts of electricity. Previously, experts told Fervo the site could only produce about 4 gigawatts.

The Google project, now called Cape Google 400, will be grid connected, co-founder and CEO Tim Latimer said in an earnings call Monday. The arrangement means that Google can draw power from the deal and sell it to other customers if it’s not ready to use it.

“We built Cape Station on our schedule. Google can develop a potential data center on their schedule, and neither party is waiting on the other,” he said.

Latimer’s statement also underlines an advantage that geothermal has over competing generating sources like large nuclear or natural gas power plants — the phased nature of its construction.

Fervo’s newer wells are deeper and hotter, each capable of generating 15 megawatts gross power, and several are linked together into what the company calls a GeoBlock. The first completed GeoBlock generated 33 megawatts, while recent ones hit 50 megawatts. They’re sized to match the demand of a typical data center hall, so hyperscalers can bring them online as they’re completed.

As a business, Fervo is still in the early stages. It ended the quarter with an operating loss of $43.7 million, though it holds cash and cash equivalents of $1.9 billion and long-term debt of $301.6 million, which should give it plenty of runway.

Much will depend on the company bringing down the cost of its power plants. While hyperscalers are hungry for power today, they may not always be as price-indiscriminate.

Yet if Fervo can deliver on its promises, it could end up winning on price. The first phase of its Cape Station project cost $7,000 per kilowatt of capacity, the company said. The cost for its Google project is $5,500 per kilowatt, a significant drop that suggests Fervo is on the right path. With subsidies, that’s about equivalent to a new natural gas power plant running on fuel that costs $6 per million BTUs. Still about twice what natural gas costs today, but trending closer to where it needs to be.

But if Fervo can hit its long-term target of $3,000 per kilowatt, it could significantly undercut natural gas power, even at today’s prices. That’s a pretty ambitious target, but the company has made significant strides in just a few years. No wonder Google is getting in early.

Securities lawyers were already moving. At least four firms, including Schall, Brown & Schwartz and Levi & Korsinsky, announced investigations Monday into whether Nvidia misled investors. Robert Bartlett, a Stanford Law professor and securities law expert, expects them to have plenty of company.

“I would definitely wager that there’ll be a filing,” Bartlett said in an interview Monday morning, adding that he wouldn’t be surprised if a case had been filed by the end of the day. Plaintiffs’ lawyers have good reasons to be first in line; under federal securities law, the first complaint triggers a public notice, and investors then have 60 days to ask the court to be named lead plaintiff. A firm that files early gets a head start on signing up investors with large losses, as well as on shaping the story of the case. Bartlett was right, in fact. A case surfaced Monday afternoon in the Northern District of California, where Nvidia is headquartered.

Rosen Law Firm, a New York-based plaintiffs’ firm that specializes in securities class actions, filed it on behalf of Mark Kistenmacher, a retail investor who made seven purchases totaling roughly $20,500 this year, according to a schedule attached to the complaint. (Rosen was lead counsel in a $250 million settlement with Alibaba and ranks among the most active filers of such cases.) The proposed class covers anyone who bought Nvidia securities between Oct. 9, 2025, and Nov. 8, 2026, and the suit names both Nvidia and Huang.

The complaint accuses them of misleading investors about Huang’s ties to Megaspeed, the Singapore-based data center outfit where Alice Huang was considered a top exec, and it points to three statements as proof: Nvidia spokesperson John Rizzo’s late-October claim that Huang’s interactions with Alice Huang were “not unusual or unexpected,” the company’s even earlier claim that it found “no evidence of diversion” of its chips, and Jensen Huang’s Oct. 30 comment to Bloomberg that Nvidia’s dealings with Megaspeed were “normal and not unusual.”

All three were false or misleading, the suit says, because Huang was having an affair with Alice Huang. Kistenmacher is asking for unspecified damages, interest and attorneys’ fees, and he wants a jury trial.

While much remains unknown, the legal theory here doesn’t depend on anything the board’s investigation may turn up about Huang’s private life. When The New York Times first reported on Huang’s ties to Alice Huang last month, Rizzo really did tell the Times that the relationship was “not unusual or unexpected.” Rizzo separately told another outlet, The Hill, that “Megaspeed has been no different than many other regional cloud customers. Meetings with customers are part of a CEO’s job; Jensen talks with thousands of customer executives every year.”

Of course, the world now knows that was not true. Worse, the Times reports that U.S. intelligence had been monitoring Alice Huang for more than a year.

From a securities perspective, Bartlett said, the company’s earlier statement was “clearly a misleading statement.” And because Huang is CEO, Bartlett said, “his knowledge gets imputed to the company.” In other words, Nvidia is treated as having known what its chief executive knew.

The Kistenmacher complaint makes the same argument.

Bartlett predicted a “classic fraud on the market” claim, in which investors argue they paid prices that reflected false or incomplete public information, and lost money when the truth emerged. This early lawsuit relies on that theory, and Monday’s drop of $7.97 per share, or 4.27%, gives the attorneys involved a lot to work with. Damages in such cases are tied to the stock’s decline, and Nvidia is one of the most valuable companies in the world, so you can imagine a settlement could prove exceedingly expensive.

That means a large payment from the company and its directors-and-officers insurers, but typically no personal consequences for the CEO or the board. It is also a civil matter, so Huang isn’t facing prison over it. The irony, as Bartlett noted, is that the suit is brought in the name of Nvidia’s shareholders, and shareholders ultimately pay for the settlement, directly or through insurance premiums.

Bartlett sorted Nvidia’s wider exposure into buckets. The first is the securities fraud claim, which is the clearest immediate problem for the company and potentially for Huang. The second is governance: what the board knew and when. It’s possible directors were kept in the dark, but the board also has oversight duties, and once the Times began reporting on the Megaspeed connection, the company should have had reporting lines to bring a critical risk like this to directors. If it didn’t, or if directors didn’t act, board-level liability is possible.

A third category, one that would change everything, would arise only if the board’s investigation finds Huang knew about the intelligence connection. That would be “a whole other ball game,” as Bartlett said. (The board said in a statement to the public Sunday that it has no reason to believe national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised.)

There’s also the question of government action. The SEC can seek an officer-and-director bar for intentional securities-fraud violations, which Bartlett said is technically on the table. But he doubts the current SEC under Chairman Paul Atkins would have the appetite, given President Trump’s support for Huang. A roughly two-year clock after discovery of the facts would leave room for a future administration to act, but the window would be tight, and Bartlett posited that’s pretty unlikely when asked about it.

It’s worth underscoring that neither law firm announcements nor this newly filed complaint is a sign of a strong case. Plaintiffs’ firms are quick to publish these notices when they see an opportunity. Which firm winds up leading the case is decided later by the court, and that decision usually depends on which investor has suffered the biggest loss. Kistenmacher said in his filing that he’s willing to serve as lead plaintiff, but the court will ultimately choose among the competing investors. No doubt they’ll materialize soon.

#417. Hotel giant Wyndham exposed customer data after app security lapse

Hotel and resort giant Wyndham has acknowledged a cybersecurity incident after a good-faith security researcher discovered a bug that allowed anyone to access customers’ personal information.

Security researcher Robert Wiggins at What Security exclusively told TechCrunch that he found a password inside the source code of Wyndham’s Android app, which granted access to an internal company database, hosted by tech company Salesforce.

Wiggins told TechCrunch that the credential exposed personal information including customer names, email addresses, and Wyndham member numbers. The database also stores customer login tokens (called JWTs) that keep customers logged-in to their apps without having to repeatedly enter their own passwords. The database also contained Wyndham files and documents.

According to Wiggins, the database had millions of entries, but he was unable to determine an accurate number of exposed customer records. Wyndham touts some 129 million enrolled rewards members.

The researcher said Wyndham has no way to report security flaws, and asked for TechCrunch’s help in alerting the company. Soon after TechCrunch made contact with Wyndham’s cybersecurity staff, the exposed credential was revoked, preventing any further access to the database.

“Our team has taken steps to address the issue,” a Wyndham spokesperson told TechCrunch. “Our security teams have no evidence of any unauthorized access,” the statement added, without providing any further details about the claim.

Wyndham’s spokesperson would not say, when asked, whether the company had the technical means, such as server logs, to determine if anyone had access to the customer database prior to Wiggins’ discovery.

TechCrunch also asked Wyndham whether it planned to notify customers whose information was exposed, or disclose the incident to U.S. regulators, such as filing a notice with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, as public companies are required to do for material cybersecurity incidents. Wyndham would not say.

Wyndham’s spokesperson also would not say for how long the password was embedded in its customer app, or how Wyndham plans to ship a long-term fix for the app.

App and software developers generally avoid including passwords or credentials in publicly released software and apps — a process known as “hardcoding” — as anyone with a copy of the software can extract the password and use it to access its associated server or database. Given the simplicity with which credentials can be abused by anyone, many cases of hardcoded credentials are automatically scored with a maximum vulnerability severity rating.

For Wiggins, this is his second public discovery of hardcoded credentials in two weeks, after finding a near-identical vulnerability in Applebee’s app that exposed customer data. Wiggins relied on AI tools for both discoveries, which make it easier to identify simple security weaknesses, such as hardcoded credentials, by scouring thousands of lines of decompiled app code every second.

When asked about Wyndham’s response, Wiggins told TechCrunch that he was glad the company fixed the issue, but noted that without visibility into any past activity, someone with malicious intent could have leaked large amounts of customers’ personal information.

The company would not say whether it planned to set up a vulnerability disclosure program to allow outside security researchers and hackers to privately report security vulnerabilities.

Michael Francess, Wyndham’s cybersecurity director who responded to the incident, told TechCrunch that researchers can alert [email protected] with details of potential security issues. The company’s spokesperson would not say whether it plans to change its website to allow researchers to alert the company.

This is the latest known security incident at Wyndham in recent years. In 2024, a security researcher found several Wyndham hotel check-in desks were running remote-access spyware that was leaking screenshots of guest records and booking information to the open web.

Soon after our reporting, the spyware app found on the hotel computers, called pcTattletale, was hacked and its banks of customer data stolen, forcing the company’s owner, Bryan Fleming, to shut down the company. Fleming, a former resident of Michigan, was later arrested by federal authorities and convicted of making, selling, and advertising unlawful spyware.

#401. California’s proposed ‘billionaire tax’ fails to pass

California voters have rejected Proposition 40, the state’s proposed one-time tax on billionaires, the Associated Press reported Monday. The measure failed after an expensive campaign that pitted labor unions against some of tech’s wealthiest figures. It was also opposed by Gov. Gavin Newsom.

Nic Carter lives about as far from Silicon Valley as you can get within the continental U.S. but his feelings about California’s proposed tax on billionaires couldn’t have been stronger.

Carter, a Floridian, and a general partner at the crypto-focused venture capital firm Castle Island Ventures, told TechCrunch before the vote that he thought the proposal — it was on the ballot during California’s midterm election last week — would actually have been a huge win for more fiscally conservative states like his own.

“I think it’d be it would be great for Florida and Texas if these kinds of taxes pass,” Carter said last week. “I think it’s a shame, frankly, that California has this sort of semi-monopoly on capital formation, and that it’s being squandered by local governance that’s super dysfunctional.”

Proposition 40, otherwise known as the California “billionaire’s tax,” was a ballot measure that proposed the creation of a special one-time 5% wealth tax to be levied against state residents with a net worth of more than $1 billion. The tax, which would have been applied retroactively to anyone who was residing in California on January 1, 2026, would have been used to fund a number of public programs — most notably Medi-Cal, the state’s Medicaid program that provides healthcare to qualifying low-income residents.

Proponents of the proposal — largely labor unions — said it would have inconvenienced a tiny number of state residents, as California has a little over 200 billionaires who own a cumulative total of approximately $2 trillion in wealth. In return, they said, it would generate massive amounts of revenue. Its backers projected it could have raised as much as $100 billion over five years, the likes of which could then have been used to support public healthcare and education.

But opponents argued that it would scare California’s wealthiest residents out of the state and ultimately disincentivize growth. The capital flight argument — that California’s upper crust would permanently flee, thus draining a more consistent source of revenue for the state — dominated conversations online among business leaders in the run-up to the vote. A critical but contested analysis published before the vote claimed the proposal would actually have lost the state money over time.

“You’re signaling to the entrepreneur class that you consider them a resource to be plundered,” he said.

Carter, who frequently works in San Francisco with tech startups, claimed such a tax would “inhibit venture capital investment” because VCs “wouldn’t necessarily want to invest in a company if they knew the founder was going to have to liquidate a huge percentage of their shares.” The logic: since many billionaires keep a majority of their wealth tied to their companies, it would be inconvenient for newly minted billionaires to make liquid a certain percentage of that wealth just so that they could pay the tax.

Jesse Tinsely, a California entrepreneur who has spent his life working in startups, agreed. Tinsely is the co-founder and CEO of Mainstreet, a fintech holding company that owns employment sites like Employer.com and Recruiter.com. Tinsely founded his first startup many years ago and said that, based on his own experience, what he had accomplished wouldn’t have been possible with the proposed tax. Additionally, he reiterated a widely held fear — that founders, who could be taxed on their corporate assets, would be incentivized to give up control over their companies.

“I bootstrapped my business. I have super voting shares, and I came from a [background as a] college dropout [and] broke firefighter with bad credit and whatnot, and built a business that’s valued into the billions,” Tinsely said last week. “I think it [the tax] removes the ability to do something like that.”

Tinsely said he felt the proposed tax “forces founders to opt into a system” where “anyone who wants to become an entrepreneur has to…raise money or some type of capital, or move. Those are basically the only two options.”

One of the key architects of the tax policy, Emmanuel Saez, told TechCrunch before the vote that fears about loss of founder control were unfounded, and not part of the design of the proposal. Saez, a French economics professor at the UC Berkeley, helped to create and promote the ballot measure alongside Gabriel Zucman, another Berkeley economist.

“The wealth tax hits you by diluting your economic ownership but [shares] can be easily structured to avoid losing any control rights,” Saez said, pointing to the fact that many billionaires only own a small percent of their companies. He’s also argued that stock can be structured to divorce economic ownership and voting control. Indeed, a common strategy to achieve this is called a dual-class share structure, which gives founders powerful voting rights even with limited shares.

Saez noted that AI has helped to drive a huge explosion in wealth in California but that much of that wealth is tied to a very small number of companies and individuals, many of whom won’t pay much back into the state’s coffers. He pointed out that California doesn’t really generate that much revenue from the billionaire class anyway. The state is estimated to take in about $3 billion a year from this group, representing about 1% of the total amount of revenue brought in between 2025 and 2026.

Despite its populist appeal, Proposition 40 struggled to win over enough Californians and was ultimately rejected. Invariably, many will cite voters’ lack of enthusiasm on a massive influx of spending that the bill’s opponents leveraged. The spending included as much as $379 million from the coffers of billionaires like Google co-founder Sergey Brin, who funneled their money into a multi-pronged media assault designed to discredit it. The AP reported that the “no” side raised roughly 10 times as much as the “yes” side.

Supporters of the measure signaled the fight isn’t over, though. The AP reported that backers said they could put a similar tax back on the ballot in 2028.

#397. Cyberattacks threaten to make open-source AI a little less open, Hugging Face exec warns

The very qualities that make open AI platforms valuable, like open doors and rich data, might also make them vulnerable to more hacks, Irene Solaiman, chief policy officer and SVP of strategy at Hugging Face said on a recent episode of the Equity podcast.

The October attack on OpenCode, which targeted users’ third-party API keys and caused financial damage, is the latest example. It follows an incident this summer in which an OpenAI agent swarm breached Hugging Face in an apparent attempt to cover its tracks after accessing answers to cybersecurity evaluations.

The risks aren’t limited to platforms that invite open community contributions. On Monday, AI infrastructure startup Fireworks, which helps developers run and train open AI models, disclosed that attackers had accessed some customers’ model weights, datasets, and billing information. The company temporarily disabled certain self-service dataset functions as it investigates. While the attack isn’t necessarily due to Fireworks’ openness, it highlights how much valuable information is concentrated within AI infrastructure providers.

“The things I worry about making an open community targeted are also what makes it special,” Solaiman said on Equity. “We are necessarily permissive. We want the average hobbyist in their basement who doesn’t have an institutional affiliation to be able to contribute.”

“You want people to be able to just submit pull requests and allow anybody without a valid affiliation to contribute…but then you have some losers who ruin it for the rest,” she added in a separate conversation.

That permissiveness isn’t the only thing drawing attackers. The wealth of information open platforms host also make them targets. Hugging Face became a temptation for OpenAI’s agent swarm because of the evaluation datasets it holds, whereas OpenCode’s target in this case came from its “juicy third-party API keys,” per Solaiman.

“The combination of that makes us prime targets,” Solaiman told TechCrunch in a separate conversation. She added that there’s a “danger of open source really closing down at a time when we’ve gotten so much public support.”

Much of the industry has come out recently in support of open AI models, including heavy hitters like Nvidia’s Jensen Huang. And increasingly, governments, enterprises and startups are relying on open models to build.

So what would a pullback actually look like? It could theoretically mean restrictions on who can upload models or datasets, more stringent identity verification, or additional vetting of developers before they can access certain features. While these moves could protect platforms from attackers, it could also make it more difficult for independent researchers and hobbyists to participate in communities they would have been welcomed in before.

For now, Hugging Face is trying to avoid that route. Solaiman said the company has been beefing up monitoring tools to catch hacks, as well as taking steps to prevent them. But monitoring can be expensive to maintain, she noted, and not every platform will be able to afford it.

“Administrative access tokens were part of what gave that agent swarm better access, so we just rotate them a lot more frequently than we used to,” Solaiman said. “We also now have stricter network controls.”

In other words, the credentials that give admins broad access to Hugging Face’s systems now get changed more often, which means a stolen token would be useful for less time.

Other parts of the open source ecosystem are dealing with similar security-related tradeoffs. GitHub, the world’s largest host for open-source projects, has been fighting off attackers who inject malicious code into projects to quickly spread malware. The company began tightening security requirements for publishing packages to the npm registry, revoking older authentication tokens, and limiting how long new ones remain valid.

Zach Steindler, an engineer at GitHub who handles software supply chain security, told TechCrunch via email that while security measures can disrupt established workflows, the platforms can become more secure without fundamentally restricting participation.

“It’s less about limiting functionality and more about going and fixing things that weren’t secure in the first place,” Steindler said. He noted that npm install scripts have existed for years, but recent attacks highlighted how dangerous they could be. GitHub worked with OpenJS — a nonprofit that hosts critical open-source projects built with JavaScript — on a safer approach.

The same logic applies to where security teams put their effort, per Steindler: “Security is all about maximizing impact with the resources we have, and I would much rather invest in detecting suspicious behavior and containment capabilities: things that cover all accounts and access methods. Suspicious behavior is suspicious regardless of who is exhibiting it.”

#384. After challenging Google’s Android with Cyanogen, its co-founder takes aim at Windows for AI era

Almost a decade after Cyanogen shut down its Android operating-system business following a years-long, unsuccessful effort to loosen Google’s dominance, its co-founder, Kirt McMaster, is now targeting Microsoft’s Windows with a new operating system designed for people who rely heavily on artificial intelligence in their everyday work.

McMaster’s company, Kora Systems, on Tuesday unveiled a Linux-based desktop operating system that integrates AI models and autonomous agents directly into the computing experience, allowing them to work across applications, access files, and retain context between tasks. The software is expected to launch in 2027 as a free-to-use desktop operating system, with support for existing Intel- and AMD-powered PCs as well as machines running Qualcomm’s Snapdragon chips.

Kora is developing the eponymous operating system with a team of about 50 engineers, relying on AI coding tools including OpenAI’s Codex and Anthropic’s Claude. McMaster told TechCrunch that these tools have accelerated development by at least fivefold, with most of the gains coming over the past six months.

Microsoft has long dominated the PC operating-system market, making it hard for newer players to establish a foothold. Windows held about 77% of the worldwide desktop operating-system market in October, compared with nearly 10% for Apple’s OS X, according to StatCounter’s web traffic data.

However, McMaster believes Kora does not need to compete for Windows’ entire user base. He is rather targeting people who use AI extensively and are willing to pay for access to powerful models and services.

“Microsoft can keep its billion users,” McMaster said, estimating that 45 million to 50 million people currently account for much of AI usage and revenue. He expects that market to exceed 200 million users by 2033.

McMaster told TechCrunch that his experience at Cyanogen, where he spent over five years, taught him the dangers of relying on the very company he was trying to challenge. Cyanogen remained heavily dependent on Google Mobile Services despite offering features beyond Google’s version of Android. That ultimately restricted its ability to build an independent ecosystem.

“If you are trying to compete with an incumbent but are still reliant on them, you have no chance,” he said.

Kora, McMaster said, builds on an idea he pursued toward the end of his time at Cyanogen, when he began exploring ways to integrate third-party services directly into the operating system. With Kora, he wants AI models and agents to work across applications and access files without being confined to individual apps.

Previously known as Playtron, a gaming operating-system company led by McMaster, the Palo Alto-based company rebranded as Kora in July 2025 as it shifted its focus to AI. The decision came as early companies including Perplexity and OpenAI were moving into AI-powered web browsers, McMaster said. He believed browsers would not give AI agents enough access to a computer to carry out more complex tasks, making an operating system a better starting point.

The shift also allowed Kora to build on nearly three years of work developing Playtron’s Linux-based gaming operating system, called GameOS, instead of starting from scratch.

Kora is designed to let users work with different AI models and agents across applications, while keeping track of conversations, documents, and other information. It also allows AI agents to operate in separate workspaces with restricted access to files and other resources.

While McMaster has drawn on his experience at Cyanogen, none of Kora’s engineers worked there. Instead, the company recruited heavily from the Linux gaming community, he told TechCrunch.

Kora’s software is built on Linux and uses Nix. It replaces the Fedora Silverblue foundation of Playtron’s earlier GameOS.

While Linux and Nix are open source, McMaster said Kora’s own technology, including its Cortex AI engine and desktop interface, remains proprietary. The company, he said, intends to support AI models and services from different providers, although it has not committed to making its own software open source.

Gaming also remains part of Kora’s plans. McMaster said the company intends to introduce AI-powered tools that would allow developers to create, publish, and play games directly within the operating system. He added that support for Android apps is also on its roadmap.

McMaster told TechCrunch that Kora’s team has worked closely with Qualcomm’s engineers for nearly a year to optimize the operating system specifically for Snapdragon-powered PCs. The collaboration includes support for Qualcomm’s Snapdragon X2 Elite chips.

Alongside Qualcomm, Kora is working with Saudi Arabia’s AI company Humain on an enterprise version of the operating system, called Humain OS, which is expected to run on Snapdragon-powered laptops in 2027. Kora also plans to announce partnerships with PC manufacturers at a later date, though McMaster did not identify any companies or provide a specific timeline.

Kora plans to generate revenue through subscriptions that bundle access to AI models and agentic services. Moreover, its Cortex engine is designed to run AI tasks locally when possible and use cloud-based models for work requiring more computing power.

Kora’s investors include Samsung Next, Square Enix, Alumni Ventures, Polychain, VanEck, and Circle, all of which previously backed Playtron. The company, McMaster said, has raised additional funding since shifting its focus to AI. However, he declined to disclose the total amount raised or its valuation. Kora has enough funding to operate for several years, he said.

#359. CVS Health plans to split itself up. Here's why a breakup could make sense now

CVS Health plans to break itself up — and this time, the bet looks a lot more viable.

The healthcare conglomerate on Wednesday announced plans to split into two publicly traded companies by the first half of 2028. CVS would separate its pharmacy business – including roughly 9,000 retail locations – from its major insurance business and pharmacy benefit manager, Caremark.

The move comes more than two years after CVS considered a breakup but ultimately walked away from the idea. At the time, analysts questioned whether the retail pharmacy business could survive on its own.

“Back then, the idea of retail standing alone was unthinkable,” Brian Tanquilut, senior equity research analyst at Jefferies, told CNBC.

But the economics of the businesses have changed: CVS's retail pharmacy business has become more consistently profitable, while its insurer, Aetna, has improved its performance despite persistently high medical costs in Medicare Advantage plans. That could make it easier for the company to separate the units — and for investors to recognize the value of each on its own, rather than valuing them together as one sprawling healthcare conglomerate.

“For us, it's showing the stability of the pharmacy,” Michael Cherny, senior research analyst at Leerink Partners, said in an interview. “This business is a much different business from the one that's been considered three years ago, five years ago, 10 years ago, where there had been a litany of headwinds that the pharmacy just couldn't overcome.”

CVS CEO David Joyner told CNBC’s "Squawk on the Street" on Wednesday that back in 2024, the company was "in a turnaround" without the flexibility, cash or business performance to execute a split. Now, CVS has posted eight consecutive quarters of beating Wall Street earnings estimates and has raised its guidance several times over the last two years, thanks to improvement across its PBM, pharmacy and insurer.

Since taking the helm in 2024, Joyner has pursued an aggressive turnaround plan that involves $2 billion in cost cuts, store closures, leadership shakeups and efforts to rein in Medicare Advantage costs.

The company also now expects to generate at least $15.5 billion in operating cash flow in 2026, a roughly 70% increase from $9.1 billion in 2024, and says it has reached its leverage target ahead of schedule.

"What's changed in two years is the fact that we now can look at this business and the assets that are assembled and really determine, without any distress or without anything that we have to do, what is the right strategic decision to make," Joyner told analysts on a call Wednesday, adding that the decision comes "out of strength."

While investors have largely cheered CVS's progress in recent years, Wall Street appeared more pessimistic about Wednesday's announcement, as the company's shares dropped about 12%.

CVS's retail business has benefited from changes in the competitive landscape, including the widespread retrenchment and store closures of Walgreens, Rite Aid and other pharmacy operators over the last few years. That’s created opportunities for CVS to gain market share, some analysts told CNBC.

CVS has also introduced changes to its pharmacy reimbursement model. Its CostVantage program is designed to replace the traditional process with a more transparent, cost-based model. Some analysts say that has helped the company better align reimbursement with the cost of dispensing prescriptions and contributed to steadier profits.

Those developments represent a meaningful shift for a business that has faced years of pressure from declining prescription reimbursement, store closures and competition from online pharmacies and other retailers.

That doesn't mean the retail pharmacy has become a high-growth business. CVS's retail pharmacy segment remains a relatively low-margin operation, with adjusted operating margins of roughly 4% to 5%. But analysts say it may now be stable and profitable enough to operate independently – even if investors value it at a discount to CVS's other businesses.

In a note Wednesday, RBC Capital Markets analyst Ben Hendrix said the lower valuation investors assign to the retail pharmacy unit has weighed on CVS's overall value and obscured the earnings strength of businesses like Aetna and Caremark.

The underlying growth of the insurance and PBM businesses is also stronger than that of retail pharmacy, Tanquilut said, after accounting for changes to Caremark's rebate arrangements.

Aetna in particular has gotten a better handle on medical costs by exiting unprofitable markets, expanding value-based care models, and tightening utilization management.

The breakup also comes as Caremark faces mounting scrutiny over PBM practices, including state-level legal and regulatory challenges involving the businesses and their relationships with pharmacies.

Republicans in Tennessee and Arkansas have passed laws that would ban any one company from owning both a pharmacy and a PBM — thought the Arkansas measure has been held up in court. Cherny noted that legal hurdles involving PBMs in other states have continued to pile up.

“Through what seems to be a never-ending litany of state lawsuits, it feels like it's been under more pressure,” Cherny said. “It's unclear if it just became, for lack of a better term, too tenuous to move forward with those two entities combined as one. I’m not sure.”

Even so, Joyner told the Wall Street Journal that the company wasn’t splitting up because of regulatory issues. CVS has said the separation follows a strategic review and is intended to give each business greater focus and flexibility.

The move also marks a significant departure from the integrated healthcare strategy CVS spent years building.

CVS spent roughly $70 billion to acquire Aetna in 2018 and argued that combining health insurance, pharmacy benefit management, retail pharmacies and health providers under one roof would help coordinate care and create a competitive advantage. CVS was still promoting that model as recently as its investor day last year.

The proposed split suggests the company now sees more value in separating at least some of those businesses – though it is not abandoning vertical integration altogether.

Aetna and Caremark will remain together in the healthcare company, alongside CVS’s specialty pharmacy, its primary care clinic Oak Street Health and its home-visit provider Signify Health. The separate pharmacy company will include roughly 9,000 retail stores, mail pharmacy operations and MinuteClinic, a retail medical clinic located inside certain CVS locations.

Both sides are large enough that they can stand on their own, Cherny said. Caremark is one of the country's three largest PBMs, while CVS has one of the largest retail pharmacy footprints in the U.S.

But separating the company raises questions about how much of the commercial relationship between the businesses can survive.

The relationship has historically benefited both sides. As it stands, Caremark can use CVS's extensive retail footprint to offer members convenient access to prescriptions and steer them toward CVS locations through preferred pharmacy arrangements. Programs such as Maintenance Choice, for example, give eligible members the option to fill certain maintenance medications at CVS retail pharmacies or through mail order.

Those arrangements also helped direct prescription volume to CVS's stores, giving the retail pharmacy unit an important source of customers. Because the businesses shared a parent company, the financial benefits of that relationship ultimately went to CVS as a whole.

In a note, RBC's Hendrix also raised questions about how the split could impact Aetna's Medicare Advantage quality ratings, which determine whether health plans qualify for bonus payments from the government. Retail pharmacists have played a role in engaging Aetna members, and a separation could complicate that relationship.

But Joyner told analysts Wednesday that the synergy between the two businesses would be "preserved in commercial agreements between the two organizations," adding that "we don't need the integration of an operating structure."

Joyner said CVS will be working on those potential agreements over the next 15 to 18 months.

Preferred pharmacy networks are common across the industry, and separate ownership does not prevent Caremark from continuing to favor CVS pharmacies through negotiated arrangements, Cherny said. The difference is that under separate ownership, the profits from those arrangements would no longer accrue to a single company, he noted.

“The issue is not whether the companies can continue working together in some capacity, but whether any future contractual arrangements preserve the SAME competitive advantages and flexibility as today under common ownership,” Cherny said in an afternoon note on Wednesday.

Still, both businesses face challenges on their own.

Tanquilut cautioned that some of CVS's recent retail pharmacy gains have come as Walgreens and other competitors have closed stores, raising questions about whether the growth can be sustained. The business also remains exposed to consumer pressure and competition from online pharmacies.

Caremark, meanwhile, faces continued regulatory scrutiny and potential headwinds from changes to the federal 340B drug discount program, which requires drugmakers to offer discounted medications to eligible hospitals and other healthcare providers serving low-income and vulnerable patients.

CVS participates in the program by providing pharmacy services to those organizations, but tweaks to manufacturer data-sharing policies and potential reimbursement changes could weigh on that business in 2027, Tanquilut said.

Aetna, meanwhile, is still working through a multiyear plan to rebuild its margins, as it looks to improve the profitability of its Medicare Advantage business.

Ultimately, CVS is betting that the value of letting investors assess its businesses separately will outweigh the benefits of keeping them under one roof. The split will test whether each can thrive independently.

The survey was conducted among roughly 4,660 U.S. adults ages 18 and older, designed to be representative of the broader population of consumers.

Brown said budget pressure also factors into traveling for the holidays, as Visa expects more people to celebrate the holidays "a little bit closer to home" than in the past given rising airfares and general inflation. In September, consumer prices rose 3.7% annually, driven higher by surging energy costs.

Those trends mean consumers will likely be more intentional about where they're spending, especially as inflation drives up prices across the board.

"Consumers are aspirational. They want those experiences, they want to travel, they want to be out in the world," Brown said. "However, the economic reality is with prices rising, with economic uncertainty, they need to balance the reality of today's economic environment with those aspirations."

Meanwhile, less affluent shoppers are placing an emphasis on value. Visa's consumer survey from October showed that roughly one-third of consumers with an income below $100,000 are going to switch to lower-cost brands or stores when shopping for holiday gifts this season.

At the same time, he said that consumers may just be expecting to spend more this holiday season because they've become accustomed to the higher prices.

"But, of course, consumers are internalizing higher prices of things, and I think that's why they're reporting higher anticipated spend this year," Brown said. #288. Wendy's rejects $1.4 billion takeover proposal from Nelson Peltz’s Trian

Wendy's on Thursday said it is rejecting a $1.4 billion takeover offer from a group of investors led by Nelson Peltz's Trian Fund Management.

The group offered $7.35 per share in cash on Oct. 28 to take the struggling burger chain private. In addition to Trian, it included investment firms BlueFive Capital and Sixth Street Partners, plus Flynn Group, one of Wendy's largest franchisees.

In February, Wendy's board formed a committee of independent directors to assess any potential bid from Trian in response to a regulatory filing from the firm. The committee found that the proposal "substantially undervalues" Wendy's and does not reflect potential gains from the chain's ongoing turnaround strategy, the company said in a press release Thursday.

"Our confidence rests firmly in [CEO] Bob Wright's proven track record of seasoned industry leadership, including restaurant business turnarounds," Art Winkleblack, Wendy's board chair and a member of the independent committee, said in a statement.

Trian declined to comment on Wendy's decision.

To stave off a hostile takeover, Wendy's has adopted a shareholder rights plan — or poison pill — with a one-year duration, as of Wednesday, the company said. Wendy's added that the investor group led by Trian rejected an offer from Wendy's for a standstill agreement that would have paused additional share purchases, adding to Wendy's decision to use a poison pill.

The rejection is the latest in the long-running saga between Peltz and the burger chain, which dates back to an activist campaign he led more than two decades ago. A Peltz firm has even owned Wendy's before. In 2008, his company Triarc, which also owned Arby's, bought Wendy's in a $2.3 billion deal. It later sold Arby's, turning Wendy's into a stand-alone company again.

In 2024, Wendy's named Peltz as chairman emeritus after he spent 17 years on the company's board. Trian executive Peter May and Peltz's son, Bradley, still sit on Wendy's board.

Wendy's also reported its third-quarter results on Thursday. Its same-store sales declined for the seventh straight quarter. However, its quarterly earnings and revenue topped Wall Street's expectations.

Shares of Wendy's fell less than 1% in morning trading. The takeover offer had lifted the stock, which has lost about 60% of its value over the last three years.

Wendy's is not the only restaurant chain that has found itself in the crosshairs of activist investors. Applebee's owner Dine Brands recently adopted a poison pill to block a takeover bid from investment firms Arkhouse Management and Cobblers Cove Capital.
