#465. A 'canary in the coal mine': Measles resurgence exposes weaknesses in U.S. public health, experts say

For nearly two years, the U.S. has had to swallow an uncomfortable reality: A highly infectious disease it once eliminated is spreading again.

More than 4,400 measles cases and at least three deaths have been reported nationwide this year, marking the country's worst outbreak in more than three decades. The spike followed nearly 2,300 cases in 2025. But many public health experts argue that the return of measles isn't the only concerning development — it's also what the resurgence signals about America that's causing alarm.

Measles is often described as one of the most contagious viruses, and it is uniquely unforgiving of weaknesses in public health defenses. A safe and highly effective vaccine has existed for decades.

But the virus's spread exposes declining vaccination rates, eroding trust in health institutions and growing challenges in persuading people to protect themselves, their children and their communities, experts said.

Together, those vulnerabilities have made measles a revealing test of the country's public health defenses. For some experts, the resurgence of a disease the U.S. eliminated more than two decades ago is a warning sign of its ability to contain future threats, from other vaccine-preventable diseases to emerging pathogens.

"Measles is the canary in the coal mine for so many different reasons," said Jennifer Nuzzo, an epidemiology professor and director of the Pandemic Center at Brown University School of Public Health. "It's the canary in the coal mine for other vaccine-preventable illnesses, where we could see a return of things that used to harm kids, but also for very acute, no-notice dangerous infectious disease emergencies."

The U.S. is on track to lose its official measles elimination status during an upcoming review by the Pan American Health Organization, which will meet in Washington, D.C., in mid-November.

"It says a lot about a country if it is unable to control measles," said Nuzzo, who is one of 10 experts CNBC spoke with about the ongoing outbreak and its implications.

The U.S. has the tools to prevent measles from spreading. But pockets of low vaccination coverage are leaving the country vulnerable.

The measles, mumps and rubella, or MMR, vaccine is about 97% effective against measles after two doses, while a single dose is 93% effective, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Yet data aggregated by the CDC shows that vaccination coverage for that shot among U.S. kindergarteners fell to 92.4% in the 2025-2026 school year, which is below the 95% threshold public health officials generally recommend to prevent sustained transmission.

That translates to 280,000 kindergarteners who did not complete their MMR vaccination series, according to the CDC. Childhood vaccination exemptions in the U.S. also reached a record high 4.2% for incoming kindergartners during the 2025-2026 school year, up from 3.6% the previous year, the agency says.

"The bottom line is that not enough people are vaccinating their kids," said Brown's Nuzzo.

While some cases stem from international travel, much of the recent spread has been driven by local transmission in "too many" communities where vaccination coverage falls below 95%, she added.

Among states across the U.S. collecting MMR vaccine data, 67% of counties and jurisdictions now have immunization rates below 95%, according to a September investigation by NBC News.

Recent U.S. outbreaks have often occurred in "closely knit communities" where people have frequent contact and low vaccination coverage, said Dr. William Moss, an epidemiology professor at Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health.

But declining vaccination rates do not mean most Americans have turned against immunization. Polling suggests broad support for childhood vaccines. A July KFF survey found that many Americans sit in a more uncertain middle ground about the shots, leaving room for conflicting information to influence their decisions.

"This is not an edge-case issue," Nuzzo said, adding that the vast majority of parents still vaccinate their children.

Even if outbreaks are concentrated in a minority of communities, it can create a "dangerous situation" far from those areas, she said. Infants too young to be vaccinated and people who cannot receive immunizations for medical reasons can be particularly vulnerable when the virus spreads into communities with high vaccination rates.

Those risks come as the Trump administration reshapes federal vaccine policy.

President Donald Trump signed an executive order in August suggesting that the combined MMR vaccine be split into separate shots, while repeating false claims about its safety. Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has also overhauled a key federal advisory committee responsible for recommending childhood immunizations, though the changes are frozen amid ongoing litigation.

Several experts told CNBC that such moves risk further eroding confidence in vaccines and weakening efforts to protect children.

Public health infrastructure was also "really decimated by Covid," and the CDC in particular lost many experienced scientists and physicians "who had spent their lives working on vaccine-preventable diseases," said Scott Weaver, a virologist who is the research director of Galveston National Laboratory.

He added that the CDC, as of September, is also no longer immediately incorporating state-reported "measles-associated" deaths into its national published tally, making it harder to track outbreaks in real time. The agency lists three measles-related deaths in 2026, but Pennsylvania has reported at least five deaths.

"We have folks in federal health leadership right now that are throwing gasoline on the fire, and that is not helping the situation," said Jess Steier, a public health scientist and founder and CEO of Unbiased Science, an organization that aims to make complex scientific concepts understandable.

If the U.S. fails to materially improve vaccination rates, the measles resurgence could extend beyond ongoing outbreaks.

At "current" state-level vaccination rates, measles could become endemic again in the U.S., resulting in an estimated 851,300 cases over 25 years, according to a large-scale epidemiological model published in JAMA in April 2025 by researchers at Stanford and other institutions. Under that scenario, the model predicts the measles cases could lead to 170,200 hospitalizations and 2,550 deaths over that period.

The model, which simulated infectious disease spread in the U.S. at various childhood vaccination levels, relies on immunization data from 2004 to 2023 for that baseline scenario. The model assumes that diseases are introduced by someone who travels abroad and brings them back, most commonly a U.S. citizen who isn't vaccinated.

While many people are currently immune through vaccination, outbreaks can increase in size and frequency if immunization rates decline over a longer period.

Michael Osterholm, an epidemiologist and the director of the Center for Infectious Disease Research and Policy at the University of Minnesota, said he views the measles situation in Canada as a direct warning for the U.S.

Canada lost its official measles elimination status in November after a multi-province outbreak that generated more than 5,400 cases across 10 jurisdictions in 2025. Canada has a population of more than 40 million people.

"We have a population of 350 million. If you extrapolate, if we had the very same experience that Canada had over the next year or two, that would be up to 50,000 cases of measles in the U.S.," Osterholm said. "I think that's a potential future we have to be mindful of."

An even larger measles outbreak isn't the only potential problem.

Vaccination coverage for other childhood diseases has similarly declined. The share of U.S. kindergartners vaccinated against polio fell to 92.4% in the 2025-2026 school year, while coverage for diphtheria, tetanus and acellular pertussis, or DTaP, dropped to 92%, according to CDC data.

The U.S. has already seen a sharp rise in pertussis, or whooping cough. U.S. reported cases reached roughly 43,300 in 2024 and nearly 28,000 in 2025, up substantially from pandemic-era levels, according to the CDC. Brown's Nuzzo said the resurgence reflects, in part, fewer people getting vaccinated and the fact that protection from the pertussis vaccine wanes over time.

The simulation model published in JAMA also examined how falling childhood immunization rates could affect the future spread of several diseases previously eliminated from the U.S. through vaccines. Under current vaccination levels, the researchers found that diseases including rubella and polio were unlikely to become endemic in the U.S.

But in a hypothetical scenario in which childhood vaccination rates fell by 50%, the model projected 9.9 million cases of rubella, 4.3 million cases of polio and 197 cases of diphtheria over 25 years.

Rubella can cause devastating complications when an infection during pregnancy passes to a developing fetus, while polio can cause paralysis and death, said Dr. Nathan Lo, assistant professor of infectious diseases at Stanford Medicine.

The projections are not predictions that these outbreaks will occur, but they illustrate how much risks could grow if vaccination coverage deteriorates.

"What vaccines often buy you is not just the protection, but also less uncertainty," said Mathew Kiang, an assistant professor of epidemiology and population health at Stanford Medicine who also worked on the simulation model. "I think even more frightening is just the really wide uncertainty that we just wouldn't be sure of what could happen."

The measles resurgence is also raising questions about how effective the U.S. can be at detecting and responding to emerging public health threats before they spread.

"Public health is all about prevention," said Dr. Manisha Juthani, president of the Association of State and Territorial Health Officials and commissioner of the Connecticut Department of Public Health. She said the country must maintain its ability to detect threats, invest in surveillance, and recognize that infectious diseases do not respect national borders.

Those capabilities are crucial for curbing emerging pathogens, especially when scientists initially know little about how a new disease spreads, how severe it is or how to treat it. A pathogen could spread before vaccines or treatments are available, making early detection, clear public communication and coordination especially important.

Other experts argue that weaknesses in those capabilities could also have implications for the country's defenses against deliberate biological attacks.

In an April 2025 op-ed, Steph Batalis, a research fellow at Georgetown University's Center for Security and Emerging Technology, argued that the measles outbreak exposed a broader vulnerability in the U.S.' biodefense strategy and, specifically, a weakened ability to deter potential biological attacks.

She argued that the country's struggle to contain a known, vaccine-preventable disease "might only embolden those who see our public health vulnerabilities as an opportunity."

But Osterholm, who works extensively on bioterrorism, said low MMR immunization rates and rising measles cases do not necessarily indicate that Americans would refuse a vaccine or antibiotic during a biological emergency. The shots and drugs used to counter biological threats can differ from those routinely administered to the public, he said, and the prospect of a severe outbreak could prompt urgency among the public to seek protection.

That point highlights an important limitation: The measles resurgence by itself does not establish whether the U.S. could withstand entirely different or novel public health threats.

But as a disease the U.S. knows how to prevent spreads, experts say the stakes of addressing those vulnerabilities extend to whatever might come next.

#460. Yvonne Bajela is bringing $17M in fresh capital to Europe’s startup scene

Yvonne Bajela knew it was time to launch her own fund. She spent more than a decade as a VC, most recently at LocalGlobe and Impact X Capital Partners, where she made bets on now-unicorn companies like the fintech Wise, the insurance company Marshmallow, and robotaxi Mooves.

“After more than a decade backing founders who bet everything on their ideas, it felt like time to make a bet of my own,” she said. “I wanted the independence to act on those convictions and build something that reflected my own approach to investing.”

The firm, which has already backed seven companies, will focus on early-stage investing across the U.K. and Europe. The average check size will be around £350,000, she said, with plans to back at least 20 companies over the next three years.

Perhaps unsurprisingly, she said this AI boom helped her realize that this was the right time to launch her own fund. Not only has it changed the economics of launching a company, she said, but it has created new opportunities for early-stage investors like herself. “The ability to build has been democratized,” she said. “But the ability to build something enduring remains rare.”

As TechCrunch has previously reported, the U.K.’s startup scene is having a moment. King’s Cross, for example, is one of the world’s top AI hubs, while companies like Revolut, Synthesia, ElevenLabs, and the Bezos-backed CuspAI are redefining what it means to scale and attract success in the U.K. market. For someone like Bajela, this moment means there is an opportunity to snap up investments in the next generation of top British talent early. “I think more globally significant businesses will emerge from a wider range of starting points,” she said.

However, this is also a tough market for emerging fund managers. In the U.S., it has been described as a barbell market where capital is concentrated at the top and among firms led by those who already have solid track records. Things may be slightly rosier in the U.K., as PitchBook data through the end of September shows that emerging fund managers in this region raised $7.4 billion, already surpassing the $6.2 billion they raised in all of 2025.

Still, Bajela said fundraising was a grind. The hardest rejections weren’t necessarily the outright nos. “They were the ones that came after months of conversations and significant time invested, only for the answer to ultimately be no,” she said. “Time is one of the most valuable resources a founder has, and a prolonged process without clarity has a real cost.”

Still, she said the fund nabbed five institutional backers, including the British Business Bank and Basecamp, the fund-of-funds of the investment firm Phoenix Court, which is also home to LocalGlobe. Evertrue was part of the £400 million British Business Bank’s Investor Pathways capital initiative, which saw it distribute £90 million to 10 first-time fund managers. Bajela said Evertrue wanted to raise around $13 million but saw a lot of investor interest. She decided to turn down additional investors rather than expand the fund size.

“Fund size is a strategic decision, not a measure of success in itself,” she said. “I wanted enough capital to build meaningful positions in my highest-conviction investments without creating pressure to deploy capital simply because it was available.”

Around 80% of Evertrue’s LPs are U.K. based, with the rest scattered across Europe, Asia, and the U.S. She said her international LP base represents the relationships she’s built “over more than a decade investing across markets.” It also reflects the fund’s global ambition, she said, especially since so many British founders keep their eyes on a U.S. expansion; more than half her portfolio already operates in the U.S.

Evertrue will invest mainly across AI and fintech, she said, adding that she’s “particularly interested in people with a unique insight into a problem, an unusual ability to execute, and the ambition to build something much bigger than their initial product.”

“I also pay attention to the distance a founder has traveled, what they’ve overcome, and how that has shaped their perspective,” she continued.

She’s lived in Japan as a senior investment manager at Mitsui and started her career as an analyst at Goldman Sachs. Throughout her career, she said she’s seen how “widely distributed ambition and talent are.”

“I want Evertrue to be known for the quality of the founders I back and the businesses they go on to build.”

There’s a lot of mystery around the finances of major AI labs, and as Anthropic inches towards it IPO, we’re starting to get some answers. In particular, the company is finally disclosing more about the economics of compute, and how much of Anthropic’s skyrocketing revenue is being lost to compute costs.

In a new filing on Monday, Anthropic released data that showed those margins on compute actually getting worse. The filing was an update to the S-1 released last week — but where that filing’s financial disclosures only covered through June 30 of this year, the new data extended to disclose results through an extra quarter, to September 30. Broadly, Anthropic had a good quarter: revenue reached $17 billion, up from $11.5 billion in the previous quarter, suggesting durable growth. But the operating loss grew at the same time, thanks in large part to growing compute costs.

Looking at the compute margins makes the picture even worse. In the new quarter, compute took up 58% of the company’s revenue, up from 48% the quarter before. At a moment when the company could win over IPO investors by showing margins improving, they’re getting worse.

A chart in the filing shows this even more clearly, tracking the company’s revenue for every dollar spent on compute. Since the start of 2025, it’s been steadily growing, peaking at just over 2x this past quarter, then falling back down to 1.72x.

Many indications imply that this shift in compute margins is more of a blip than a long-term problem, but it still tells us important things about the business of running a major lab. Trying to keep up with demand, Anthropic’s compute bill shot up to $10 billion for the quarter, including, as the filing notes, “$5.0 billion of compute under short-term commitments, which currently costs significantly more than comparable compute procured under our multi-year commitments.” Basically, demand was growing so fast that Anthropic had to scramble for compute, which meant paying higher rates.

Some of those deals have already played out in public. Anthropic has been paying SpaceX $1.25 billion a month for compute, alongside multibillion-dollar deals with Nscale, Akamai, and Volta. In the parlance of the industry, these are neoclouds rather than hyperscalers — smaller outfits that offer more flexible compute deals at higher rates.

Anthropic’s margins got worse in Q3 because of deals like these, and there’s no sign they’ll be able to do without them anytime soon. As the new filing puts it, “We expect compute and infrastructure expenses to increase in absolute dollars and remain our largest operating expense for the foreseeable future as additional committed compute becomes available to us under multi-year arrangements with CSPs, and as we continue to invest in additional compute.”

Anthropic is still trying to lower its compute costs. In particular, the company talks quite a bit about its efforts to build its own compute base under a project it calls “Anthropic Direct.” But with demand growing so fast, those efforts aren’t going to be able to keep pace, and the neoclouds will continue to be the best option they have.

In a sense, these are champagne problems. As long as revenue is growing at nearly 50% a quarter, few investors will worry about high compute costs — and the moment demand slows down, investors may be comforted by a compute picture that has gotten a lot sunnier. But it’s a reminder of how much AI labs still depend on the trillion-dollar infrastructure buildout — and how vulnerable they are to hiccups in that process. It’s easy to imagine the data center buildout slowing down over the next year if bond markets get skittish or local opposition gets more intense. There’s not much Anthropic can do to address either of those risks, and either one would make a messy compute picture a whole lot messier.

In the meantime, the future of Anthropic and other labs will be at the mercy of compute prices.

In the lead-up to a presidential election that would irrefutably transform American culture, Hillary Clinton took the stage at a rally in Virginia as she campaigned to become the first female president.

“We’re going to have a lot of jobs — jobs from building infrastructure to coding. Creating new apps,” she said. “I don’t know who created Pokémon GO, but I’m trying to figure out how we get them to have Pokémon Go to the polls.”

Ten years later, it is once again Election Day in the United States. Now, most app developers aren’t even writing their own code anymore, thanks to AI, and Pokémon GO has been sold to a company wholly owned by Saudi Arabia’s sovereign wealth fund. The only thing about that political moment that hasn’t changed is that “Pokémon Go to the polls” memes still crop up on every Election Day.

“It was very bold and confident, and also just sounded so terribly out of touch and corny,” Cesar Ruiz de Castilla, an elected ward committee member in Philadelphia, told TechCrunch. “I don’t know if the mainstream media cared that much about it, but everyone was making a meme about it and talking about it online.”

Since then, digital culture has become increasingly inextricable from the fate of American politics. Donald Trump tweeted his way through his first term, then later started his own social media platform; presidential campaigns hire meme managers; the White House invites podcasters and content creators to press briefings; Elon Musk even spun up his own advisory group under Trump that named itself after the cutesy doge meme and then slashed international aid programs.

“The 2016 election was such a monumental moment in internet history, and we were all there for it — our politics have become more, not just divided, but segmented online,” Organizer Memes, a political influencer who teaches Gen Z activists how to reach their peers online via memes, told TechCrunch. “It’s a relic of an optimistic, bygone political era.”

Now, people who weren’t even old enough to vote in 2016 reflect on the year as a cultural turning point, even calling it “the last good year.” It’s a sentiment that isn’t so much political as it is rooted in nostalgia for an era of internet culture before AI slop, when we used social media to connect with friends, rather than doomscrolling through an algorithmic feed of people we don’t even follow.

At the start of the new year, this yearning for the old internet became a viral sentiment as millions of people shared throwback photos from 2016 on Instagram.

Spotify reported that user-generated playlists featuring 2016 in their titles spiked 790% in the first three weeks of the year, and the company changed its Instagram bio at the time to say it’s “romanticizing 2016 again.”

Never mind that, at the time, the internet perceived 2016 as a cursed year, even before the election — months earlier, one Slate columnist wondered how 2016 compared to 1348, the year the Black Death took hold in Europe.

“[Pokémon Go to the polls] is in that era of the singular internet, when Facebook and Twitter and Tumblr were all kind of bouncing content back-and-forth and not just this complete anarchic mess of 25 platforms that we now have and don’t use,” Organizer Memes said.

Organizer Memes also notes that some cultural touchstones that felt more significant then — like the obsession with Harambe the gorilla — haven’t had as much staying power as “Pokémon Go to the polls.”

Perhaps this is because we’re bound to remember “Pokémon Go to the polls” every few years on Election Day, and because it appeals to both sides of the aisle. On the right, it’s a way to make fun of Democrats, and on the left, it’s a shared groan of embarrassment.

“It’s like if all the kids in a class are making a joke, and then the teacher gets in on the joke, and it stops being funny,” Mack Finkel, who canvassed at the polls on Tuesday on behalf of a healthcare workers’ union, told TechCrunch. “I think there’s a key essence of millennial cringe happening here, and it’s the same reason that all my friends are rewatching [HBO’s] ‘Girls.’”

Clinton’s decade-old, corny comment can be seen as a precursor to the “Kamala is brat” meme. While the former vice president’s record as a prosecutor initially made her unpopular among some left-leaning young voters, they rallied around her once she became the only alternative to Trump. Gen Z rallied around Harris by creating flashy, absurdist “fan cam”-style edits of the candidate’s best quips in the aesthetic of Charli XCX’s album “Brat,” which soundtracked the summer of 2024.

“Previously the views I saw about Harris were making fun of her being a cop, like her history as attorney general in California with truancy laws,” Jess Rauchberg, an assistant professor of communications at the University of Leeds, told TechCrunch at the time. “Now we see that same style of memeification, but it’s an endorsement.”

It’s hard to say why “Pokémon Go to the polls” jokes still make us laugh — whether we’re mocking Clinton or expressing solidarity with others who keep showing up to vote, even as each passing year leaves us feeling more cynical.

“Pokémon Go to the polls” is a way of urging others to make the time to vote and take Election Day seriously, while still acknowledging disappointment with aging lawmakers who don’t know what it’s like to come of age in the social media era. It’s shrouded in enough irony to project a sense of jaded detachment, yet remains a desperate, earnest plea to our friends: Please, just go vote.

A satellite built by the German startup Orbital Matter 3D printed a half-meter long beam out of a custom plastic material, in what executives called a first for a free-flying spacecraft in orbit, and a step toward constructing large structures in space.

The company’s small satellite, Replicator 2, was launched on a SpaceX rideshare mission this summer, with four of the company’s 3D printers onboard. Each printer can create a rigid, cylindrical beam by extruding coils of resin that are cured by an ultraviolet light, which the company analogizes to cement and water.

This technology matters because future spacecraft will need to be larger in order to generate more electrical power and radiate a more heat into the vacuum of space, particularly if they’re going to serve as orbital data centers. But they’ll still need to fit into the tight confines of the fairing on top of the rocket that carries them into orbit. If spacecraft are able to construct their own structures in orbit, the power and heat-dissipation challenges may get a lot easier to solve.

“Replicator 2 is the first proof that we can simply build the array in space, at whatever size the mission demands,” said Jakub Stojek, CEO and co-founder of Orbital Matter. The company is partnering with the European Space Agency and Thales Alenia, a leading European satellite builder, with which it collaborated on Replicator 2.

Today’s large structures in space are origami projects — large solar arrays or telescope mirrors are folded up and then deployed on orbit. That’s a tricky engineering project, and so the idea of making and assembling those structures in space is appealing. NASA has funded some efforts at the idea, notably a mission with the company Redwire that 3D printed structural beams in a vacuum chamber in 2023 but never got off the ground.

Two of Orbital Matter’s printers are designed to deploy a flexible solar array, but an anomaly led one of the printers to fail. Still, the boom that was created successfully deployed the solar array, and is an important proof of concept. Previous 3D-printing efforts in microgravity were limited to the pressurized environment onboard the International Space Station.

“That’s why we put so many printers on this single satellite. We preferred to learn in orbit and adapt the software as needed rather than waste years testing every possibility on the ground,” Orbital Matters CTO Robert Ihnatisin told TechCrunch.

The boom’s length is limited by the amount of resin carried onboard the satellite, and the company’s engineers believe that 100 meter booms are feasible. For comparison, SpaceX’s Starlink V2 satellites — among the most powerful mass-produced satellites in orbit — uses solar arrays that are 20 meters long.

Orbital Matter was co-founded in 2022 by Ihnatsin, who previously worked at 3D-printing company FormLabs, and Stojek, and raised a $3 million seed round.

Now, Orbital Matter is working on another demonstration mission, scheduled for launch in 2028, that will test a larger version of its printer on a bigger satellite that the company hopes will deploy a solar array that can generate 5 kw of power. That will be a “stepping stone” toward larger deployments of 100+ KW per wing by 2030.

The company is also collaborating with satellite builders that are considering incorporating its PADS (printer-assisted deployment system) into their spacecraft to extend solar panels, antennas, or other components.

Anthropic is working on an open-source system to verify which AI model produced a given output, according to a blog post published Tuesday. The system, called FlashProve, is built to provide what researchers call “provable inference” – a cryptographic proof, essentially a mathematical guarantee, that a given output came from a specific model. It’s meant to let users confirm that the answer they received really came from the model they think they’re using.

Similar to cryptographic email signatures that let recipients confirm a message really came from its claimed sender, FlashProve would be robust against impostors attempting to disguise one model as another, even when the impostor is the lab that built and runs the model. In particular, the researchers note that the system could be used to prevent an AI company from secretly swapping in a cheaper model to cut costs, or a more expensive model to inflate its performance results.

FlashProve could also be used to verify claims by third parties that a specific task was done by an advanced model. “A developer might claim that their software library was scanned for vulnerabilities by a frontier cybersecurity model, or a company might claim that its business plan was given the go-ahead by an appropriate model,” the researchers write. “How can a user verify these claims?”

To work, the system requires AI labs to publish a “fingerprint” of each model they release that can later be matched to specific outputs and verified cryptographically. Crucially, the fingerprints don’t reveal a model’s weights, the internal numbers that define how it behaves and which closed-model makers like Anthropic and OpenAI keep private. That means labs could publish the fingerprints without giving up their most valuable asset, enabling the system to work with commercial models, too. The system is also designed to be lightweight, so verification could be performed on a local device.

As part of the announcement, researchers released a detailed technical specification and working code for various elements of the system, which is publicly available under an open-source license on GitHub. Research in the system is ongoing, and there are no plans to deploy it at Anthropic or any other lab.

“This research post is meant to share intermediate progress, and not a completed research agenda,” researchers explain in the announcement post. “We are sharing this explicitly with the goal that others look at what we have done, take it, and improve it.”
