#435. ‘Pop-up’ super PACs pour $637 million into 2026 midterm races

A super PAC called Our Heritage Matters registered with the Federal Election Commission on Oct. 15.

Five days later, it began spending to boost third-party candidates and attack Republicans. By Oct. 29, the group had spent about $1.2 million across 10 House and Senate races, according to a CNBC analysis of FEC filings.

What voters still cannot see is who supplied the money.

Because Our Heritage Matters formed after a key campaign finance reporting deadline on Oct. 15, its first regular report identifying its donors is not due until Dec. 3, a month after Election Day.

The group is an extreme example of a broader surge reshaping the midterm election: late-forming super PACs, including the latest-forming "pop-up PACs," pouring money into races before voters know where it came from.

Federal law requires super PACs and political committees to report large independent expenditures within 24 or 48 hours depending on how close to Election Day the spending occurs, showing how much they spent and which candidates they supported or opposed. Their donors, however, are disclosed less frequently through periodic campaign finance reports.

And in 2026, the practice of creating new, last-minute PACs has exploded. Both parties are using the tactic, with Republican-linked PACs spending about twice what those connected with Democrats have disbursed.

Twenty-two super PACs that registered with the FEC during the final 10 weeks before Election Day have each spent at least $1 million on congressional races, according to CNBC's analysis. Together, they had reported roughly $637 million in independent expenditures by Oct. 29 — about one-sixth of all independent spending reported in House and Senate races this election cycle.

The number of million-dollar late-forming super PACs has more than quadrupled since 2022, while their spending has grown nearly 35-fold.

At the same point in the 2022 midterms, just five comparable groups had spent about $18 million combined, according to CNBC's analysis. In 2018, three had spent about $12 million. The total has even eclipsed the past two presidential elections, when comparable late-forming super PACs spent less than $50 million combined on congressional races in both 2020 and 2024, according to CNBC's analysis.

"The reason for this is almost entirely to avoid disclosure, even if just temporarily," said Michael Kang, a Northwestern Pritzker School of Law professor who studies campaign finance. "Many times, if the donors were known, then it would undercut their cause."

CNBC's analysis includes super PACs formed in the final 10 weeks before Election Day that spent at least $1 million on House or Senate races by Oct. 29. Spending includes regular FEC filings and 24- and 48-hour reports, with duplicates removed.

One of the defining features of late-forming super PACs is speed.

Among the 22 groups in CNBC's analysis, 18 began spending within 30 days of launching, and almost all started spending before filing their first regular campaign finance report showing where their money came from.

“It’s happening more often, and they’re spending more money doing it because it works, and they’re getting away with it,” said Saurav Ghosh, director of federal campaign finance reform at the Campaign Legal Center. The group is a nonpartisan nonprofit founded by a former Republican FEC chairman.

Despite their late start, the groups accounted for more than 22% of independent expenditures in congressional races during CNBC's 2026 window, up from less than 2% at the same point in each of the previous two midterms.

Many are new only on paper. CNBC found financial or organizational ties between nine of the 22 groups and established political networks.

Lower Costs Now, for example, formed on Sept. 10 and disclosed an affiliation with the Future Forward PAC, the main outside group that backed former Vice President Kamala Harris' 2024 presidential campaign. Nineteen days later, Lower Costs Now began spending in Senate races.

But voters did not get a fuller picture of its financing until Oct. 15, when its first regular financial report showed that Senate Majority PAC, the main super PAC working to elect Senate Democrats, had given the group $23 million, according to FEC filings.

By Thursday, Lower Costs Now had reported about $75.5 million in independent expenditures across eight Senate contests, including Iowa, Texas, Kansas and Ohio, according to CNBC's analysis.

No Going Back PAC formed Sept. 1 and began spending three days later. Public reporting soon linked it to MAGA Inc., President Donald Trump's marquee super PAC. But its formal financial report did not arrive until Oct. 20 — more than six weeks after the spending began.

By then, No Going Back had already spent roughly $187.8 million, according to FEC filings, which revealed that MAGA Inc. had supplied the group with $200 million. By Thursday, the super PAC had spent roughly $242.2 million, according to FEC filings.

“They are a vehicle designed to emerge from nowhere and spend a bunch of money on an election” before voters can learn “who’s supplying that money and what their motivations might be,” Ghosh said.

Many of the new groups are also narrowly targeted.

Fourteen of the 22 million-dollar super PACs identified by CNBC spent all of their money in a single congressional contest.

Take The Maine Standard. It formed Sept. 11 and began spending five days later. Since then, it has spent $8.2 million, all of it opposing Democrat Troy Jackson in Maine's Senate race, according to FEC filings.

The Cornhusker Majority followed almost the same pattern. It formed Sept. 10, began spending Sept. 16 and has spent about $7.1 million in Nebraska's Senate race, almost all of it opposing independent Dan Osborn, according to FEC filings. Osborn is running against Republican incumbent Sen. Pete Ricketts in a tight race.

The connection between the two groups did not appear in their regular financial reports until Oct. 15: Both were financed by Senate Leadership Fund, the GOP's main Senate super PAC.

On the Democratic side, Texas Forever launched Sept. 24 and began spending five days later, according to FEC filings. By Thursday, it had spent about $27.5 million to help Democratic Texas Senate nominee James Talarico, according to the FEC. Unlike some other pop-up groups, however, its initial FEC filing identified the Senate Majority PAC as an affiliate.

"A locally branded PAC doesn't necessarily hide where the money comes from, but it fragments the picture. Instead of seeing one national organization spending tens of millions of dollars across the country, voters see a series of different groups operating race by race," said Hilary Braseth, executive director of OpenSecrets, a nonpartisan nonprofit that tracks such spending.

But even identifying the national group behind a pop-up PAC can still leave another layer of the money trail hidden.

Texas PAC formed Aug. 28 and soon began spending to help Republican Ken Paxton in Texas' Senate race. The money flowed through the SLF, but Elon Musk's role was not public until Oct. 12, more than a month after the spending began.

On Oct. 15, FEC filings showed Musk had given the SLF $158 million in two contributions, with the group transferring matching amounts to Texas PAC the day after each donation.

By Oct. 29, Texas PAC had spent about $181.6 million in the race, according to FEC filings, making it one of the largest late-forming super PACs CNBC identified.

Together, Texas PAC and No Going Back accounted for roughly two-thirds of all spending by the 22 million-dollar groups. But the surge was much broader than those two giants: Even excluding them, the remaining groups spent nearly 12 times as much as comparable late-forming super PACs did at the same point in 2022.

"The growth is huge this year," Kang said. "Maybe you don’t want to be known this election, or want to hide away for a period while you might be unpopular, like AI is right now, but you kind of want to build a reputation for the players inside the system."

#414. For JPMorgan, Amazon, fighting AI bots like Meta Muse may not be a winning long-term strategy

The biggest names in banking and retail are starting to fight the tech titans, and one another, over a new kind of customer: the AI personal agent.

JPMorgan Chase, Charles Schwab, Amazon, Neiman Marcus and Kohl's have moved to block personal agents like Meta Muse just as consumers start sending them out to find better savings rates, shop for deals and cancel subscriptions.

The era of agentic AI has arrived, but there is uncertainty about what the landscape will look like when virtual storefronts become flooded with AI agents or when a business tries to block them.

Reports last week of AI agents going rogue in customer accounts — and prominent politicians including Sen. Mark Warner and Sen. Bernie Sanders calling for bot legislation in response to the headlines — come on the heels of JPMorgan Chase CEO Jamie Dimon saying the bank currently blocks external AI agents from operating on behalf of retail customers, citing security risks.

Some other retailers are leaning in. Walmart announced last week it is opening the virtual doors to AI agents that customers prefer to use. "We price products, not people, so the prices that an AI agent sees are the same prices that a human would see on Walmart.com, with applicable offers and fees shown before purchase," the company said in a blog post announcing the decision.

A recent audit of 51 large U.S. retailers' published access rules by identity-verification company Billions Network and affiliated company AmanTrue, found only 11 have taken a deliberate position on AI agents. Five, including Amazon, Kohl's and Neiman Marcus, explicitly restrict them, while 37 block shopping agents largely by accident, through older bot rules written for scrapers. None has a way for a verified agent to complete a purchase.

Based on Billions Network's analysis, blocking an agent can occur in multiple ways: blocking a specific bot from a third-party by its rules (e.g. as Chewy does with an Amazon bot), and employing more general blocks on any third-party bots attempting to access login, registration and checkout functions. Its analysis of published access rules comes with the caveat that those rules are not proof of technical enforcement.

Evin McMullen, Billions Network and AmanTrue co-founder and CEO, said at this point, "Most of the retailers in our audit have not made a decision about AI agents at all."

Michael Abbott, global banking lead at the consulting firm Accenture, thinks that businesses blocking AI agents will find it difficult to win with that approach over the long term.

"If customers explicitly authorize an AI agent to act on their behalf, that's ultimately an extension of customer choice. The more important question isn't whether institutions can block agents, but whether they can create a secure and trusted way for them to engage," Abbott said.

Abbott said that Accenture research found that 65% of consumers are open to using a GPT-like financial assistant through a Gen AI platform or digital wallet, and 71% would welcome an AI assistant embedded within their primary bank's mobile app.

"We've seen this movie before … online banking, mobile banking and digital wallets. Customers tend to gravitate toward experiences that reduce friction and save time. The institutions that win are usually the ones that find a way to participate safely rather than resist the change," Abbott said.

The real opportunity for banks is to become the trusted provider of the data, intelligence and execution that those agents rely on.

"As interactions increasingly happen beyond bank-owned channels, banks still need to be present at those critical decision moments, even if they don't own the interface," Abbott said.

When it comes to distinguishing legitimate security concerns from companies protecting profits by making it harder to move money or comparison-shop, Abbott said both things can be true at the same time.

"There are very real concerns around fraud, identity verification, liability and consumer protection. And customers expect their banks to safeguard their assets and data — those responsibilities don't disappear simply because an AI agent is involved," Abbott said, adding that at the same time, consumers have historically benefited from technologies that increase transparency and make it easier to compare options.

"Our research found that 82% want to approve every AI action and 79% want the ability to immediately pause or stop AI activity. That tells us consumers are looking for convenience, but not at the expense of control," Abbott said.

Mike Danford, owner and chief revenue officer of Adverio, which runs Amazon, Walmart and Target marketplace operations for e-commerce brands, said that some of the security fears businesses, including banks, cite are less about security and more about whose sandbox a customer plays in.

"Amazon blocked Muse and other outside agents, but its own Buy for Me feature uses Amazon's agent on other brands' sites. Amazon doesn't appear worried about the agent. It's worried about who holds the data," Danford said. "Amazon now lets shoppers set rules that auto-purchase when a price threshold is met, and that's the version of agent buying consumers seem to accept, because the shopper still approves the decision within predefined parameters," he added.

"We think it's fairly straightforward that third-party applications that offer to make purchases on behalf of customers from other businesses should operate openly and respect service provider decisions about whether or not to participate," an Amazon spokesperson said. "Agentic third-party applications such as Muse have the same obligations, and we've requested that Meta remove Amazon from the experience," the spokesperson added.

Danford says banks are similar, just with a fee instead of a wall.

"JPMorgan now charges aggregators like Plaid for pulling account data, so the toll lands on the apps a consumer uses and may trickle down in subscription fees," he said.

Plaid said at the time of the September 2025 deal that it would not impact any of its existing pricing agreements with customers. Plaid does not charge consumers, though apps and services that use Plaid determine their own customer fees and subscription prices.

Michael Jabbara, head of payment ecosystem risk and control at the payments network Visa, declined to speak to any single bank's motives for blocking agentic AI, but he said the fraud risks from agents are "very real and very tangible."

Consumers can be fooled by fake agents built by fraudsters to steal their credentials and payment details, he said, and a legitimate agent can act in ways the customer never intended, like buying a sofa the shopper had only asked it to find.

Agents can also behave unpredictably: Jabbara described a user who asked his agent to book a gym slot, and when the slot was taken, the agent hacked the gym's scheduling software, deleted the existing appointment and put the user's name in its place. The concerns, he said, come down to knowing "who the agent is, who the agent is acting on behalf of," and whether it has "the appropriate permissions."

"Agents are not just exchanging data. Agents can also take an action on behalf of a user," he said.

Like other experts, Jabbara says past shifts from online to mobile shopping are instructive, but for another reason: "None of those innovations really scaled until people were able to build that foundational security layer that enabled trust for that scale to happen."

Another worry is volume. Millions of agents doing legitimate work for customers could swamp websites and customer-service systems, a phenomenon some call "agentic flooding."

Amid the sudden rise of personal agents like Muse, Wharton School AI expert Ethan Mollick posted, "We will soon see what will be, in effect, a perfectly legal distributed denial of service attack on customer service departments authorized by millions of consumers when it becomes widely known that Muse/Dot/etc can make unlimited requests for old refunds, negotiate deals, etc."

Abbott calls it a possibility, not a crisis. "I see it more as an infrastructure challenge than an existential threat," he said. "Most digital systems today were designed for human interactions. Agent-driven interactions could occur at a completely different scale and frequency. One person might have multiple agents continuously monitoring rates, researching products or evaluating alternatives."

Nevertheless, he says the answer isn't to block them. "It's to build the next generation of infrastructure that can authenticate trusted agents, verify permissions, manage identity and support machine-to-machine interactions efficiently," he said.

Which, he added, is nothing new for banks.

"Customers used to interact with their banks a couple of times a month and now with mobile it is nearly daily. AI agents are just the next evolution. The organizations that prepare for that reality will be better positioned than those that try to prevent it," he said.

While many financial sites already have defenses against overuse, to protect against the types of mechanized denial-of-service attacks that have long been possible, agents "never sleep and never lose focus," said Ed Felten, a Princeton professor emeritus of computer science who served as chief technologist at the Federal Trade Commission and deputy U.S. chief technology officer in the Obama administration. And the more agents use a site, the more the costs could rise for companies like banks.

But Felten doubts the blocking will last. "When a button is clicked in the user's browser, it will be hard for a remote site to tell whether this was done by a human hand or directed by an AI agent on the user's computer," he said. "If there is a battle over AI access, it will slow the adoption of AI agents. But in the end, agents will win out because they will be so useful to users, so that sites that are too effective in blocking agents will lose business," he added.

As the market forces sites to accept their customers' agents, common expectations will emerge about what is reasonable behavior by agents, experts said.

But Joseph Chalom, CEO of SharpLink, one of the largest corporate holders of the cryptocurrency ether, and a former BlackRock executive, expects the gates to stay shut for a while.

"These large incumbents effectively control the access points and will seek to keep their gates closed to fend off this innovative threat. Disruptors have fought the same battle for access for decades across travel, telephony, and financial data consolidators," Chalom said, adding that in each case, the incumbent has cited security concerns, but in fact they also face significant business model interruption.

"For example, an AI agent working for users can easily rebook flight tickets at a lower cost, eating into the margins of airlines. And more significantly, an AI agent can move idle cash from checking and savings accounts earning little interest to a money market account earning 10x the interest rate," Chalom said, noting that these types of "switching costs" are being reduced to near zero as agents have full financial literacy and attention span.

"For this reason, I don't expect any of them to be in a hurry to lower the drawbridge," Chalom said.

In the near term, Abbott says, some institutions will welcome agents while others stay restrictive as they sort out authentication, liability and governance. Longer term, he says, the data shows that companies should expect people to hand the administrative work of financial decisions to their agents. "Defense can prevent you from losing in the short term but to win banks will need an offensive strategy," Abbott said.

Proprietary bots will need to be good enough to not only keep customers but keep them from wanting to use other agents, and that is how CEOs like Amazon's Andy Jassy are talking about it. "We believe Alexa for Shopping is going to be the primary shopping agent customers use to shop Amazon. Having a shopping assistant constantly by your side as you browse, get inspired, have questions on product detail pages, and that you can simply ask to buy something based on your prior purchases that already have your history, your shipping address, and payment info, is uniquely helpful and convenient for customers," he said during last week's latest quarterly earnings call.

Ultimately, brands are about to be tested in ways that haven't been possible before. Human customers become attached to brands for reasons beyond price: a nostalgic cereal that evokes warm fuzzy childhood memories, an airline soaked in the nostalgia of family vacations, or a bank with a favorite teller. An AI agent doesn't care about any of that.

"Brand loyalty and customer relationships are getting tested. An AI agent has no feeling about a logo or a ten-year relationship. It has a mandate and cares purely about outcomes," said Nikhil Behl, president of software at FICO, the analytics company best known for credit scores. "The levers that used to create stickiness get replaced by levers an agent can actually read, such as consistent approvals, transparent pricing, fast resolution, and terms that hold up through comparison," Behl said.

#406. Why AI is boosting Meta's ad business while hurting Google search

Google and Facebook have dominated the internet for the past two decades, turning billions of eyeballs into trillions in advertising dollars over that stretch. But with the ChatGPT era altering how consumers engage online, the companies are diverging.

In the third quarter, Meta's ad business grew more than twice as quickly as Google's Search and other unit, backing up Mark Zuckerberg's claim that his company's investments in artificial intelligence are leading to better targeting tools for brands to promote goods and services across Facebook and Instagram.

Google's problem is that chatbots are becoming an alternative to search, leading consumers to click far fewer blue links and hurting Google's ad revenue. Anthropic's Claude chatbot and Meta's recently launched Muse assistant are adding to the mix. Meanwhile, users are spending more time scrolling on Instagram, giving Meta more advertising opportunities.

"It's a double-edged sword, because as AI revolutionizes discovery, people might come across tools they like better than Google," Nate Elliott, an analyst covering AI at Emarketer, told CNBC in an email. "If ChatGPT or Claude or Muse is better at helping them find what they're looking for, people's search habits might change for the first time in a generation. That's potentially catastrophic for Google's ad business."

Meta's ad revenue rose 26% in the quarter to $63.1 billion, up from $ 50.1 billion a year earlier, compared with 12% growth for Google's search division, which brought in $63.4 billion. That 14-point growth gap is the widest in Meta's favor since 2021, and it puts the two businesses neck and neck by size, though Google got an additional $11 billion in third-quarter revenue from YouTube.

Following its disappointing third-quarter earnings report on Wednesday, Alphabet had its worst day ever on the stock market, plunging more than 12% and wiping out about $500 billion in market cap. Meta isn't exactly a Wall Street darling at the moment either, as the stock fell 14% in October for its worst month since October 2022. That followed a 27% surge in September, which was its best month since November of that year.

Investors' reactions reflected market jitters surrounding the companies' AI buildouts, which have pushed both businesses into negative free cash flow territory. Questions are swirling about whether their historic spending on AI infrastructure will generate enough revenue.

Meanwhile, both Meta and Google are aggressively trying to diversify their revenue streams. Meta, which still relies on ads for nearly all its revenue, is pouring money into AI models and agents to build a subscription business. Google already has a great and rapidly expanding cloud computing division that just reported over 100% growth to $31 billion in quarterly revenue, driven by demand for its infrastructure and its custom AI chips called tensor processing units, or TPUs.

The boon in online advertising, and the hefty margins that come along with it, catapulted the two companies into leadership positions and brought their market caps into the trillions.

Late 2022 brought the launch of OpenAI's ChatGPT and ushered in a new era of generative AI technology. While Meta initially struggled to find a consistent AI strategy and fell far behind OpenAI and Anthropic in developing frontier models, the company has successfully infused AI into its ad products, giving brands advanced tools to reach users more efficiently.

A year ago, the company unveiled the Meta AI business assistant, which advertisers can plug into the Ads Manager system to help build campaigns and improve performance. Meta CFO Susan Li said on last week's earnings call that advertisers adopting the system's recommendations "drove almost 5% more conversions" and "increased their advertising investment by 9% compared with similar non-adopters."

Li gave the example of syrup producer Simply Organic Maple, and said the company, in using the assistant, saw a 57% increase in purchases and a 36% decrease in cost per purchase. She added that in the third quarter, almost 10 million advertisers used at least one of its AI tools.

Google has a similar opportunity with AI in ad products across its properties, including YouTube.

Philipp Schindler, Google's chief business officer, said on the earnings call that the company is deploying its Gemini models "across our entire ads infrastructure," adding tools for advertisers and improving ad quality.

However, Google's search engine is competing directly with ChatGPT and Claude by surfacing more results through AI Overviews and AI Mode, where users primarily get summaries rather than links to other sites. Search queries hit a record high in the quarter, Google said, but revenue growth is slowing.

"That's going to bring Search cannibalization fears right back to the forefront, made worse by META's ad strength this quarter and the lingering Muse/agent threat," analysts at UBS wrote in a note after Google's results.

Both companies are so bullish on AI's potential that they're pumping hundreds of billions of dollars into the infrastructure needed to build new models and services. Google projects up to $205 billion in capital expenditures this year, while Meta expects to spend up to $145 billion.

Advertising remains key, but both are investing in a future that could look very different. Google is much further along on diversification, as ads now account for less than two-thirds of its revenue, while Meta still counts on ads for 98% of sales.

Still, analysts at Wedbush see Google's search monetization as critical to the investment thesis.

"GOOGL shares are unlikely to work until the search narrative changes," the analysts wrote in a report on Thursday. "And it will likely require new ad products and proof points that it can monetize in the evolving AI era as it did in the past."

#400. The midterm election could reshape the muni bond market. Here’s what investors need to know

The outcome of Tuesday's election is expected to have broad implications across the market, including municipal bonds.

The spotlight has been on the battle for congressional control and whether Democrats will wrest the House of Representatives, the Senate or both from Republicans. But for the muni market, what happens at the state level could have bigger repercussions — at least in the near term.

There is an array of initiatives on ballots around the nation that could change tax laws and add to bond supply. Taxes matter to muni investors since the income received is free of federal tax, as well as state tax if the holder lives in the state where the bond is issued.

Depending on the location, ballot measures are up for approval that would either provide tax breaks, or raise taxes on the wealthy.

"It's a complete bifurcation between red and blue states," said Dan Close, head of municipals at Nuveen. "Blue states are trying to put in place graduated income tax structures, trying to capture more revenue from high-income earners. Red states are promoting affordability by trying to cut property taxes."

Red states are those that predominantly vote Republican, while blue states swing toward Democrats.

For instance, California has a proposition known as the "billionaire tax," which would impose a one-time levy equal to 5% of the wealth of certain taxpayers with at least $1 billion in assets. The state also has a measure on the ballot that would make a temporary tax hike permanent on individuals earning over $371,000, adjusted annually for inflation. The tax is currently set to expire in 2031.

"The impact to the muni bond market will be to make the exemption more valuable," Close said of the possible passage of tax increases. Investors will likely seek shelter in the muni market, which would send bond prices higher, he noted.

It is a different story in Florida, where voters are set to decide whether to increase the Sunshine State's homestead exemption for non-school district property taxes. Qualified homeowners currently have a $25,000 property tax exemption, as well as another for $25,000 that doesn't apply to school property taxes. The new proposal raises the latter exemption to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 by 2028. New residents have a five-year waiting period before receiving the benefit.

If passed, some local governments will have to find other revenue sources or increase the overall tax rate to replace what they've lost, said Tom Kozlik, head of public policy and municipal strategy at HilltopSecurities.

"It's not necessarily going to result in downgrades for sure, but there would have to be some balancing with regard to overall revenues," he said.

Not all municipalities will be hurt equally, which means credit research is important, said Close.

"Like everything in municipal finance, it is really on a case-by-case basis that you could have some credit deterioration from [a] rollback of property taxes," he said.

Other voters on Tuesday will be asked to approve more state or local government debt. That could weigh on a market that has already seen heightened supply.

Some $473.8 billion has already been issued year to date, through September, according to the Securities Industry and Financial Markets Association. That's a 7.8% increase over the same period a year ago. In 2025, total muni issuance reached $587.3 billion, 14.3% higher than 2024, Sifma said.

Cooper Howard, director of fixed income research and strategy at the Schwab Center for Financial Research, believes supply is the biggest issue coming out of the election.

"That amount of issuance has been one of the reasons why you have to see that strong amount of demand continue to keep pace with it for total returns to keep up," Howard said.

Fortunately, new supply doesn't necessarily come quickly into the market. "There's a window over a couple years that they can issue," he noted.

The ultimate makeup of the next Congress could also have consequences for muni investors as lawmakers sort out their priorities. Most notable is what lawmakers will do to tackle the country's affordability problem, Kozlik at Hilltop said.

"There could be some tax policy initiatives and even affordability-related initiatives that manifest themselves in the first quarter of next year," he said. "That could impact housing. It could impact higher education. It could impact public power financing."

Investors considering moves before the election may want to look at those states with tax increases on the ballot, said Close at Nuveen.

For instance, Nuveen recently began offering a separately managed account dedicated to Washington after getting an enormous number of inquiries from financial advisors. The state recently enacted a 9.9% tax on individuals with taxable annual income above $1 million, set to go into effect in 2028. But the issue is now being put to voters, who can decide whether to repeal the new law.

"At the margin, if you do think that some of these measures are going to pass, knowing that bonds usually move up with an increase in taxes, there might be some opportunities to buy ahead of the election for blue states," Close said.

Howard believes munis are attractive right now, particularly for those who are in a tax bracket of 24% or above. Fundamentals are good and the bonds largely have good credit quality, he said. "About 70% of the broad muni index is either triple-A or double-A rated."

Kozlik is also bullish, calling yields "really attractive." He suggests investors concentrate on AA-rated or higher bonds.

"The muni space is part of the portfolio that most investors want," he said. "It helps them sleep well at night."

#346. Merck tops quarterly estimates, hikes profit outlook as new Keytruda form shows strength

Merck on Thursday topped third-quarter estimates and hiked its adjusted profit outlook, as its immunotherapy Keytruda, including a newer injectable form of the drug, showed stronger-than-expected sales.

The pharmaceutical giant now expects adjusted earnings to be between $2.77 and $2.82 per share. That adjusted profit outlook is up from a previous range of $2.66 to $2.76 per share. The guidance now includes a one-time charge of $400 million, or 12 cents per share, related to its licensing agreement with the Chinese cancer drugmaker SciBrunch.

Merck also narrowed the low end of its 2026 revenue guidance, now expecting sales between $66.8 billion and $67.3 billion. That compares to a previous outlook of $66.3 billion to $67.3 billion.

The company has been on a deal spree as it races to offset generic competition for Keytruda after key patents begin expiring in 2028.

Merck is also positioning the new form of the drug, Keytruda Qlex, as a cornerstone of its growth strategy. The goal is to transition patients from the original intravenous version to Qlex, considered a more convenient under-the-skin injection. This strategy allows the company to defend its market share and sustain Keytruda's blockbuster sales beyond the original version's loss of exclusivity.

Keytruda raked in $8.60 billion in sales for the quarter, beating the $7.73 billion that analysts were expecting, according to StreetAccount. That includes $830 million in sales for Qlex – the highest seen in a quarter since it launched in 2025.

Analysts were expecting $649 million in sales, StreetAccount estimates said. The blowout quarter for the shot suggests it could soon eventually be a multibillion-dollar product annually.

Here's what Merck reported for the third quarter compared with what Wall Street was expecting, based on a survey of analysts by LSEG:

The company posted a net income of $5.07 billion, or $2.04 per share, for the quarter. That compares with net income of $5.79 billion, or $2.32 per share, for the year-earlier period.

Excluding acquisition and restructuring costs, Merck posted earnings of $2.49 per share for the third quarter.

Merck raked in $18.09 billion in revenue for the quarter, up 5% from the same period a year earlier.

Beyond Keytruda, Merck's newer products also showed strong growth.

Winrevair, which is used to treat a rare, deadly lung condition, generated $694 million in sales for the quarter, up 93% from the same period a year earlier. Analysts were expecting sales of $645 million.

Meanwhile, the Gardasil vaccine, which prevents cancers from HPV, a very common sexually transmitted infection, drew about $2 billion in sales due to higher demand in the U.S. That tops the $1.76 billion in sales that analysts were expecting.

That strong revenue comes after Merck last year grappled with elevated Gardasil inventory and weak demand for the vaccine in China.

Meanwhile, Merck's animal health business posted $1.68 billion in sales for the third quarter, which came in slightly below analysts' estimates.

#330. These Senate races may help determine the fate of Social Security

Senators elected this November must answer a crucial question before their six-year term ends: What will they do to fix Social Security?

The program's trustees project that the trust fund Social Security relies on to help pay retirement benefits may be depleted in the fourth quarter of 2032. Without action from Congress before then, those benefits could be cut by 22%.

Lawmakers may enact reforms to prevent those automatic cuts. Or they may kick the can down the road.

Most voters — 81% — say they are more likely to support a candidate who has a plan to avoid automatic benefit cuts to Social Security over a candidate who promises not to touch the program, according to a recent survey of voters in states with competitive Senate races by the Peter G. Peterson Foundation, a nonpartisan organization.

Even so, many Senate candidates' campaign platforms lack specifics on plans for the program's future.

While 35 Senate seats are up for grabs in November, including in special elections in Florida and Ohio, CNBC identified nine races where the candidates have mentioned their plans for the program's future. MS NOW says all nine races are closely contested battlegrounds, and it rates the possible outcomes.

Here is a closer look at those nine contests and what the candidates in those races have said about Social Security reform.

Alaska voters will choose between incumbent Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan and former Democratic Rep. Mary Peltola, who served in the House from 2022 to 2025.

During his latest term, Sullivan voted yes on the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which provided a new temporary senior tax deduction of up to $6,000 per eligible individual. Sullivan also voted for the Social Security Fairness Act, which ended reductions to certain government pensioners' Social Security checks. While both laws helped beneficiaries, they increased the program's costs.

Most older Alaska voters — 90% — say they are more likely to support a candidate who will ensure they receive the Social Security benefits they've earned, according to a September AARP survey.

During the 2014 Alaska Senate campaign, Sullivan said he would consider options to cut Social Security costs, such as raising the retirement age or means-testing benefits — a point his opponent raised during the 2026 race.

Sullivan's campaign did not respond to CNBC's requests for comment. During an Oct. 15 debate, he said benefits for retirees and near-retirees should be protected and that program reforms must be bipartisan.

Peltola's platform calls for eliminating taxes on Social Security benefits, according to her campaign website. Peltola also co-sponsored the Social Security Fairness Act.

"Mary will stand up to anyone to fight for Alaska seniors and protect the benefits Alaskans have earned," a spokesperson for Peltola's campaign said in an email statement to CNBC.

MS NOW rates the Alaska Senate race a toss-up.

In the Hawkeye State, sitting Republican Sen. Joni Ernst is retiring, and the open seat's winner will be decided in a contest between Democratic state Rep. Josh Turek and U.S. Rep. Ashley Hinson, a Republican.

"One of the best ways to keep Social Security solvent is we have to remove the cap," Turek said at a Sept. 9 Des Moines town hall, referencing the maximum annual earnings subject to Social Security payroll taxes.

Raising or eliminating the cap would mean high earners pay more into the program. In 2027, that threshold, adjusted annually, will rise to $192,300 from $184,500 in 2026. Proposals to raise the cap would generally leave that first threshold in place, and then reapply Social Security payroll taxes at a proposed earnings level, such as $250,000 or $400,000.

In a recent AARP interview, Hinson said, "Anyone who's going to propose cuts to Social Security is going to get a 'hell, no' from me." In 2024, Hinson voted on the House Appropriations Committee to cut Social Security Administration funding by up to $452 million.

In 2020, Hinson noted she was "open" to raising the Social Security retirement age but has since said she opposes that change. In the House, she voted for OBBBA and the Social Security Fairness Act.

When the candidates were asked during an Oct. 13 debate about Social Security fixes, both said they would support a bipartisan commission.

A Social Security field office in Hinson's district in Decorah, Iowa, recently closed for six months. Some reports indicated low staffing, and political opponents and advocacy groups blamed Hinson's support for staffing cuts backed by DOGE, President Donald Trump's so-called Department of Government Efficiency. In a June 10 statement, Hinson called the six-month closure "unacceptable" and said she advocated for the office to reopen.

Turek "will protect Social Security by lifting the cap to make billionaires pay their fair share," a campaign spokesperson said in an email statement to CNBC.com.

Hinson's office did not respond to CNBC's requests for comment.

MS NOW rates the Iowa Senate race as "leans Republican."

Maine's incumbent Sen. Susan Collins, a Republican, will face Democrat and former Maine Senate President Troy Jackson on the ballot.

During an Oct. 8 debate, Collins said the payroll tax cap should be "adjusted," signaling support for a largely Democratic proposal that has recently gained bipartisan backing. She also said Congress should create a commission of independent financial experts to advise on how to address the program's funding shortfall.

A September AARP survey found 81% of Maine voters age 50-plus say Social Security is an important issue for deciding their vote. Moreover, 93% said they are more likely to support a candidate who will make sure workers receive the Social Security benefits they've earned.

At the debate, Jackson said the threshold should be raised.

In a recent Bangor Daily News op-ed, Jackson said he wants to increase retirees' Social Security benefits. He also said he opposes privatizing the program and other benefit cuts.

In the op-ed, Jackson also said he will "never support" raising the retirement age. Collins said in a 2023 News Center Maine interview, in response to potential reforms being discussed at that time, that she does not support raising the age to 70.

Collins co-authored the Social Security Fairness Act. She voted against OBBBA in its final vote, citing its Medicaid cuts. However, she did vote "yes" on an earlier motion to further the bill.

Collins' campaign did not respond to CNBC's request for comment.

"Troy will work to scrap the cap and require the wealthy to pay Social Security taxes on their whole income and expand benefits — not raise the retirement age," a Jackson campaign spokesperson said in an emailed statement.

MS NOW rates the Maine Senate race a toss-up.

Michigan voters face a choice between Democrat Abdul El-Sayed and Republican Mike Rogers for Senate. El-Sayed most recently served as Wayne County public health director, and Rogers served as a U.S. representative for Michigan from 2001 to 2015.

El-Sayed said during an Oct. 8 television debate that the Social Security payroll tax cap should be eliminated. "It's only fair that the rich should pay their fair share," he said.

He also said during the debate that taxes on Social Security benefits should be eliminated.

Rogers said during the debate that he supports a "bipartisan solution" through a committee to address the program's funding issues. "Put them in a room, lock the doors and say, 'Don't come out until we have a real solution that can get bipartisan support,'" he said.

An August AARP poll found that 93% of Michigan voters ages 50 and older said they are more likely to support a candidate who will ensure workers receive the Social Security benefits they've earned.

Rogers has not proposed raising the retirement age or privatizing the program during this campaign. However, he has said in the past that he would support raising the retirement age, according to PolitiFact. As a congressman, Rogers previously supported proposals to allow some Social Security payroll taxes to go to private investment accounts, according to the fact-checking website.

A campaign spokesperson said via email that Rogers understands Social Security is "a lifeline for seniors who've spent their entire lives working hard and investing into this state ... Mike will ensure that we invest in them."

El-Sayed's campaign did not respond to CNBC.com's request for comment.

MS NOW rates the Michigan race as a "toss-up."

Nebraska voters may choose between Republican Sen. Pete Ricketts and independent candidate Dan Osborn, a labor union leader backed by state Democrats.

Ricketts has said Social Security benefits should be "completely tax-free."

As Nebraska governor, Ricketts signed a 2022 law to phase out state taxes on benefits. As senator, he introduced a similar federal measure, which remains with the finance committee. He also voted for OBBBA. While eliminating taxes on benefits helps recipients, it reduces revenue for the program.

"Nebraskans reaching retirement age deserve to receive the benefits they have earned," Ricketts said in an emailed statement. "I am committed to protecting our seniors' benefits and working to improve the Social Security program for all Americans."

To extend Social Security's solvency, Osborn has called for raising or eliminating the payroll tax cap, according to his campaign website. "The solution is very simple — remove the Social Security cap, so everyone pays their fair share," he said in an email statement to CNBC.

Osborn is against Social Security benefit cuts, including raising the retirement age, and privatization of the program, according to his campaign website.

MS NOW rates the Nebraska Senate race "likely Republican."

New Hampshire voters in November may choose between two veterans of Congress: Democrat Chris Pappas and Republican John Sununu. Incumbent Democratic Sen. Jeanne Shaheen is not running for reelection.

Pappas, who has served as a U.S. representative since 2019, will "never vote to cut benefits," according to his campaign website. At an August event, he said high earners should pay their "fair share" into the program.

"I also think that seniors deserve a raise," Pappas said at an Oct. 14 AARP candidate forum. Social Security's cost-of-living adjustments have not kept up with the "true cost of living," he said.

Pappas voted for the Social Security Fairness Act. However, he voted against OBBBA, citing its cuts to social safety net programs, including Medicaid.

Sununu has served terms in the Senate and the House of Representatives. During his tenure, Sununu supported bills that would have diverted a portion of payroll taxes to individual investment accounts instead of the Social Security trust funds under a broader push by then-President George W. Bush.

Pappas has said during the campaign that Sununu led efforts to privatize the program — a statement that fact-checking organization PolitiFact said is "mostly true," noting that Sununu's proposals would have been voluntary, limited by age or both. Had the proposal passed, the program would be solvent today, Sununu said in an August WMUR interview.

"I never voted to change or reduce or cut benefits," Sununu said at AARP's candidate forum.

Sununu's campaign did not respond to CNBC's request for comment. Pappas' campaign referred CNBC to his comments on Social Security during the recent AARP forum.

MS NOW rates New Hampshire's Senate contest as "leans Democrat."

North Carolina's Senate candidates — former Gov. Roy Cooper, a Democrat, and former Republican National Committee Chair Michael Whatley — have raised the issue of Social Security during their campaigns.

Cooper told AARP in September that some members of Congress are "undermining" Social Security by giving the wealthy tax breaks. He told AARP that the program needs to be protected and strengthened: "In the U.S. Senate, I'll work with anyone I can to do just that."

A September AARP poll found 83% of North Carolina voters ages 50 and over say Social Security is an important issue for deciding their vote. Meanwhile, 93% say they are more likely to support a candidate who ensures workers get the benefits they've earned.

Whatley told AARP that his top priority will "always be to preserve and protect" Social Security and that he will not support any proposals that "take away anything from what is owed" to people who have paid into the program.

Whatley also said Congress must work in a "bipartisan way" to improve the program's solvency.

Whatley's campaign did not respond to CNBC's requests for comment.

In an emailed statement, a Cooper campaign spokesperson said: "Roy Cooper will work to protect and strengthen Social Security benefits for current and future generations."

MS NOW rates the North Carolina Senate race as "leans Democrat."

Former Democratic Sen. Sherrod Brown of Ohio is pursuing a comeback bid, running against Republican Sen. Jon Husted. The special election will decide who will serve the remaining two years of the term formerly held by Vice President JD Vance.

As senator, Brown advocated for expanding and protecting Social Security benefits. He co-authored the Social Security Fairness Act.

A June AARP poll found that 83% of Ohio voters ages 50 and older said candidates' positions on Social Security will affect how they vote.

More recently, in an October AARP voter guide video, Brown said he opposes privatizing the program.

"We need to make sure that billionaires are paying their fair share by raising the tax cap so we can extend the life of the program," Brown told AARP.

In AARP's video, Husted said Social Security will require "genuine bipartisan solutions."

Husted voted for OBBBA. He also introduced a constitutional amendment to balance the federal budget, a move critics say may prompt large cuts to Social Security.

Asked about the candidates' plans for Social Security, neither Brown's nor Husted's campaigns gave CNBC more detail.

MS NOW rates the Ohio Senate race as a toss-up.

#323. AI is coming to construction sites. But does it make workers safer?

When Shelley Copsey was appointed by the Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan, one of the world's biggest infrastructure investors, her job was to find ways to use technology to solve problems across its portfolio.

She quickly learned that there were persistent problems facing front-line workers.

Four years later, Copsey is CEO of FYLD, a London-headquartered software company that uses artificial intelligence to support workers in construction, utilities and infrastructure.

It comes as AI plays an increasingly important role in some of the most physically demanding jobs, from maintaining roads and railways to working on construction sites.

The aim is to reduce both paperwork — and accidents.

According to Copsey, when fieldwork goes wrong, attention usually turns to budgets or cash flow.

"We always go to the back office and we're like: oh, if we could help them forecast the job better, maybe this wouldn't be a problem," she told CNBC. "I think that what nobody ever did was just sit and say: well, what goes on in those field workers' lives and how do we make the life of the front line easier?"

FYLD uses AI to predict which permits a project will need, helping workers avoid delays. Previously, workers often arrived at a site without all of the information they needed, Copsey said. The system can also help crews plan around the weather, for example, rescheduling heavy lifting from a day when strong winds are forecast.

Workers record short videos describing their day's tasks and safety concerns. AI analyzes the information to help workers and managers anticipate potential problems.

An additional benefit is a reduction in paperwork, according to Copsey. Field workers can spend around 20% to 25% of their time on site on administrative work, she said, whereas now, photos, video and job information can be used to generate draft reports, reducing a task that previously took around half an hour to two or three minutes.

Infrastructure services provider Amey announced in March that it would roll FYLD out to 2,500 field workers as it looks to improve safety and give supervisors real-time visibility of work on the ground. Copsey said FYLD case studies have reported reductions in incidents and injuries of as much as 48%.

AI is also being used to reduce the need for physical infrastructure inspections.

Nathan Marsh, London-based senior vice president for Europe, the Middle East and Africa at U.S. software company Bentley Systems, told CNBC that the company's road maintenance platform, Blyncsy, analyzes images captured by drivers to spot problems, including damaged guardrails, overgrown vegetation and debris.

"Applying AI to existing imagery provides complete asset coverage in a fraction of the time a conventional survey would take," Marsh said.

Steven Elsham, U.K.-based CEO of Dutch maintenance software company Ultimo, told CNBC its AI tools can flag safety incidents that might otherwise go unreported.

The system scans work orders and shift logs for signs of incidents, then drafts a report for a person to review. Elsham gave the example of a warehouse door with damage that suggested it had been struck by a forklift.

"People should still report things themselves," he said. "This is there for what gets missed."

Waleed Umer, an assistant professor at Northumbria University, England, told CNBC that many studies so far have focused on what models can detect, rather than whether they prevent harm.

Construction sites change constantly, making it difficult for cameras to capture every hazard, he said. He also warned that false alarms can lead workers to ignore warnings, while overreliance on AI can create a false sense of security.

Billy Hare, a professor of construction management at Glasgow Caledonian University, Scotland, told CNBC that research projects and pilot studies have primarily focused on productivity gains, often assuming that less exposure to hazards means lower risks.

Bentley Systems, for example, said one rail project it supported cut physical site visits by 50% and manual surveys by 70% using a digital twin, a virtual model of a physical asset.

It's reasonable to assume that less time spent in hazardous environments could reduce risks, Hare said, but establishing whether technology actually prevents accidents needs further investigation.

According to the U.K.'s Health and Safety Executive (HSE), the number of construction workers killed in work-related incidents in Great Britain fell to 25 in 2025/26, down from 35 the previous year and below the five-year average of 37.

"The U.K. has seen the lowest construction fatality figure ever recorded this year, but this may be due to economic slowdown," Hare said, which makes it harder to tell whether technology has made a measurable difference.

Human oversight may not be enough on its own,  Northumbria University's Umer said, especially if AI produces more information than supervisors can realistically review. In that case, recommendations could be approved without proper scrutiny.

"Treat AI as an extra pair of eyes, not a replacement for supervision," Umer said. "And be clear about what it can't see."

For Ultimo's Elsham, the bigger question is whether AI makes difficult jobs more manageable rather than squeezing more work out of the people doing them. That starts with how performance is measured, he said: companies need to decide what any time saved is for, and which parts of the job AI should support.

"Safety teams are already covering more hazards with the same headcount," he said. "If the only plan for the freed-up hours is more tickets per head, workers will see straight through it, and they'd be right."

Companies should instead measure what matters on the floor, he argued, including safety, first-time fixes and unplanned breakdowns, rather than just output.

"A well-run business benefits everyone in it, and the people doing the hardest jobs should feel it first," Elsham said.
