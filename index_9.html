#475. Nike lays out financial goals ahead of first investor day in 9 years

Nike said Tuesday ahead of its first investor day in nine years that it was reaffirming its 2027 financial guidance.

The retailer also laid out a financial framework and said that by the end of fiscal 2031 it's targeting approximately $5 billion in incremental revenue growth from fiscal year 2027, operating margins of 12% to 14% and adjusted earnings per share of $3.00 to $3.50. The company said higher-quality revenue, gross margin expansion and productivity improvements would help it reach its goals.

Nike's 2027 financial guidance includes expectations for revenue to decline in the high-single-digit range, an adjusted operating margin of approximately 6%, and adjusted EPS of $1.15 to $1.35.

The company also introduced a strategy it's calling Full Stride Ahead, which the company said aims "to get closer to athletes and focus resources on the greatest opportunities for long-term growth."

Nike CEO Elliott Hill told CNBC's Sara Eisen that the new initiative is about "being closer to the athletes we serve."

"What's different today is the depth and breadth we're taking from an innovation perspective," Hill said.

He added that Nike is emphasizing sportswear at a time when sports have never been more relevant around the world. Hill also said Nike is working to regain its cultural relevance, after a perceived dropoff contributed to its slowdown both in its home market and in China.

Nike said it would focus on performance sports and sportswear opportunities "with the greatest potential for long-term growth," and said its five priority sports would be running, training, global football, basketball and outdoor.

Nike is holding an investor day at its Oregon headquarters Tuesday as the company aims to prove to investors that its turnaround strategy is yielding success after a yearslong slump.

Last month, the company reported mixed fiscal first-quarter results as it announced a restructuring plan, including layoffs that it said would start next year. It's the company's third round of layoffs to be announced this year.

Nike's net income and revenue both fell during the most recent period compared with a year ago, and the company said it expects revenue to decline by a high single-digit percentage in fiscal 2027.

The new strategy, dubbed Pace, focuses on the company's supply chain and reorganizing into three geographies. Nike said the turnaround plan is expected to deliver roughly $2.5 billion in savings through fiscal 2031.

Nike has been seeing significant sustained declines in its China market, where revenue last quarter dropped 26%. Experts have said the company is losing relevance in the country, while Nike has said it's actively working to win over younger consumers.

At the same time, Nike announced last quarter that it's creating a new global campus in India as part of its new growth strategy, hoping the country will help boost its performance. The company said it was remaining focused on China even as it begins to build out its India presence.

Nike COO Venkatesh Alagirisamy told CNBC the company sees an opportunity to triple its business in the country over the next five years.

He added that Nike's broader strategy will position it for success in India.

"If you look at the three components, brand building, landing those innovations into marketplaces in an increasingly locally connected fashion and then doing all of those things in a much faster pace really aligns well with the aspirations for India," he said.

CEO Elliott Hill's turnaround strategy includes improving different parts of the business at different rates based on priority. While it's seen some signs of progress in the North America business, Hill has said that performance isn't enough to offset the pressures on the company elsewhere, including internationally.

The Nike consumer has also been under increased macroeconomic pressure as geopolitical tensions mount and inflation rises.

Shares of Nike have fallen 50% over the past two years.

#465. Republicans outspent Democrats for first time in over a decade in record 2026 midterm election political ad blitz

Political advertisers spent a record $12.06 billion during the 2025-26 elections, making it the first time a midterm cycle outspent a presidential cycle, according to a report from advertising intelligence company AdImpact released Tuesday.

Total spending exceeded AdImpact's June projection by more than $460 million.

The elections also marked the first time in over a decade that Republicans have outspent Democrats on advertisements, according to AdImpact.

Despite Republicans leading spending, John Link, senior vice president of data at AdImpact, said that "Dems still led or were generally even with overall airings and impressions."

The record spending extended across the board, with every type of race reaching a new high. Senate spending hit $3.39 billion, while spending on gubernatorial elections reached $2.61 billion, spending on races in the House of Representatives grew to $2.44 billion and state legislative races totaled $602 million, all records, according to AdImpact.

The 2026 midterm cycle was largely powered by megadonors, with SpaceX and Tesla CEO Elon Musk becoming the largest contributor by funneling more than $366 million into campaigns.

The biggest donors in this year's election disproportionately favored Republicans, according to a CNBC analysis of Federal Election Commission records. Fourteen out of the 20 largest donor groups supported GOP candidates, according to that analysis.

Republicans spent $5.51 billion on political advertisements, compared with $5.11 billion spent by Democrats, according to AdImpact.

While Republican spending increased by 27% from 2024, spending by Democrats fell by 4%.

Fundraisers told CNBC in interviews that Democratic donors' hesitancy largely stems from memories of Kamala Harris' defeat in 2024, unease about the rise of democratic socialism and distrust in party leadership.

Though they were outspent, Democrats made sweeping gains against Republicans in races nationwide, taking control of both the House and the Senate and flipping seats in some of the most competitive places, including Texas and Kansas.

It is not the first time spending did not align with the winners. In 2024, President Donald Trump defeated former Vice President Harris despite Democrats outspending Republicans.

While party majorities in Congress have switched hands, the most popular medium for political ads has not changed.

Broadcast television represented the largest category for spending in this year's election, accounting for 49%, or $5.85 billion, of political ad spending, according to AdImpact. Broadcast's share of political ad dollars is up slightly from 48% in 2024.

Broadcast TV's strength comes despite facing pressure from falling viewership and increasing competition from streaming.

Industry experts have told CNBC that political advertisers continue to spend on broadcast because it reaches local markets and active voting populations. The Associated Press also reported that airtime was so expensive during the midterm elections that some candidates, including Republican Ken Paxton in Texas, bought ads during national sporting events, figuring that reaching enough constituents through a national broadcast might actually be less expensive than buying across the state's local television markets.

The fastest-growing categories in this year's cycle were connected TV, or internet-connected devices like Apple TV or Roku, with $2.66 billion, and digital, with $1.91 billion. Both mediums grew double digits from the 2024 presidential election. Spending on cable and radio declined 7% and 13%, respectively, from 2024.

#462. Todoist confirms 6% of customers’ details were stolen in data breach

Task management app Todoist said on Tuesday that a hacker gained access to its production systems and stole customer data, including email IDs, password hashes, and account creation dates. The company said around 6% of its customers were affected and blamed stolen credentials for the breach.

Todoist said the attacker used stolen credentials to access the company’s version control system and source code between November 22 and 23. The hacker then changed employee access controls and reached the app’s cloud infrastructure via an employee’s work account.

The hacker downloaded credentials of early Todoist users whose passwords were stored using an older hashing method, but didn’t change any source code, the company said. Todoist said it has blocked the attack path and reset passwords as a mitigation step, as well as disabled the Todoist for Outlook email add-on.

For a small number of people, about 0.001% of its userbase, the hacker also searched through tasks, comments, and attachment names to look for credentials for other services, like API keys and access tokens.

The hacker also pulled Todoist API tokens and Slack integration tokens, and looked for emails, addresses, and attachment file names through the Todoist for Outlook extension if it was connected to accounts and had permission to read emails.

The company said it has sent a notice to those affected and asked them to reset their credentials and change their login details in other places with the same password. The company is asking users to also update API tokens, reconnect integrations, and revoke or replace any access keys, recovery phrases, or credentials they had stored in Todoist comments.

Todoist said it has also reset its internal access structure and how remote command execution works in its cloud environment to prevent such incidents in the future.

Todoist’s founder and CEO Amir Salihefendić apologized to users about the incident on X, and said the company will take a closer look at why it happened.

The to-do app’s data breach is the latest in a burgeoning list of data breaches in which hackers use stolen credentials to access data and systems at enterprises. So far this year, we’ve seen several companies fall victim to this form of attack, including Uber and Klue.

"The commission concluded that endemic measles transmission has now been reestablished in Bolivia, Mexico and the United States, and recommended that their measles elimination status be withdrawn — a recommendation which I have accepted," Dr. Jarbas Barbosa, PAHO director, said during a news briefing on Tuesday.

It's a devastating step backward for American public health, as declining childhood vaccination rates and vaccine hesitancy fuel outbreaks across the country that have sickened thousands of people and led to a tragic string of preventable deaths. Among the deaths where vaccination status was reported, all were unvaccinated.

More than 5,400 measles cases had been reported nationwide in 2026 as of mid-November, marking the country's worst outbreak in more than three decades. States have confirmed 10 deaths associated with the disease so far in 2026: seven in Pennsylvania, one in New York and two in Wisconsin.

The U.S. in particular has ramped up vaccination efforts to close immunity gaps, particularly in areas with low immunization coverage experiencing outbreaks, said Dr. Daniel Salas, executive manager of PAHO's Special Program for Comprehensive Immunization. #417. Jensen Huang broke Nvidia’s code of conduct, and his employees may not forget it

When Jensen Huang gave his first all-hands address since returning as CEO, there was an odd emphasis on Nvidia’s code of conduct. He admitted to violating the code, then emphasized that no one was exempt from it. But that “does not mean, the moment you violate a code of conduct, we walk you out the door,” Huang said, according to Wired. “Facts, circumstances, a lot of assessments” still matter. In other words, the code applies to everyone, but breaking it doesn’t automatically cost anyone their job, including, apparently, the CEO.

It’s an awkward stance for a company whose CEO has admitted to breaking its rules. A central part of modern human resources, a code of conduct can seem like a quasi-legal rulebook that lays out unacceptable behavior. But experts say most codes are better understood as a reflection of corporate culture. When an infraction goes unpunished, the main risk isn’t legal but cultural — that employees will feel embarrassed or even angry about the company they work for. That’s the risk Huang now faces at Nvidia.

Matt Plass, chief market strategy officer at LRN, an ethics and compliance training outfit, recently surveyed more than 2,000 workers across different industries for a study on workplace codes of conduct. As Plass sees it, the biggest risk is that employees will become ashamed of their workplace.

“People coming into the workforce now, ethics is incredibly important to them,” he told TechCrunch. “They’re really mindful of the ethical standards of their company. They’re really embarrassed and angry when senior leadership lets them down.”

The consequences of violating the rules aren’t always tangible, but they’re definitely real. Plass gave one example of a company that banned corporate travel to cut costs — only to have its CEO turn up in a VIP box at the Olympics.

“For over a decade afterwards, ‘the Olympics’ was used within that company as an example of the worst thing an employee could do. People would say, ‘Yeah, I shouldn’t have done that, but it’s not like I went to the Olympics.’”

Plass says companies that remove an executive typically do so after an investigation, then try to win back employees. “In most organizations they make a change at the top based on what’s happened,” he said, “and then they will have a hearts-and-minds initiative, to go out to their employee base and their customers and their investors and everybody else, and say, we get it. We’re sorry. We’ve changed. That’s essentially what they do.”

Of course, that playbook is harder to follow at a company like Nvidia, where Huang left without a clear succession plan, and there are serious doubts about whether the company could continue to thrive without him. In cases like that, Plass acknowledges there’s no easy answer.

“I would draw a distinction there between the kind of companies where the CEO is a kind of godhead figure … where the person and the company are almost impossible to separate,” he said. “There it’s much, much harder to deal with.”

In that context, Huang’s stutter-step over the code of conduct is easier to understand. Judging by the employee campaign for his reinstatement, Huang’s return hasn’t created a morale problem so far. But as the coming months put more pressure on Huang and his colleagues, sidestepping the code could end up costing Nvidia something harder to measure — its employees’ pride in the company.

#400. Grand Theft Auto VI proves why efficiency isn’t a virtue

It should have been impossible for “Grand Theft Auto VI” (GTA VI) to live up to the hype. Fans waited 13 years for the newest installment in the crime-adventure franchise, and Rockstar Games exceeded even those inconceivable expectations when the game finally came out last week.

GTA VI launched as the highest-rated game of all time on Metacritic and made $3.79 billion on release day to mark what could be the biggest entertainment event ever – more massive than “Avengers: Endgame” ($2.9 billion) or Taylor Swift’s Eras tour ($2 billion).

The 13-year wait certainly fueled the frenzy, and sales, around GTA VI, but some fans craved GTA VI because of what it represents in the emerging era of AI.

Even the biggest workaholics among us – startup founders – scheduled time off to play the game, including Spiry co-founder Dima Beseda, who told TechCrunch, “I reshuffled my workload to free up time around the weekends and even booked vitamin IV drips to keep my energy up.”

Yet some players felt a sense of anticipatory nostalgia as they wiped away a layer of dust on their Xbox to play the game they’d been looking forward to since they were kids.

“We do have a view that GTA is likely to be the last game of its kind to be made this way,” Ben Feder, managing partner at TIRTA Ventures and former CEO of Rockstar parent company Take-Two Interactive, told TechCrunch.

That’s because GTA VI was created entirely without generative AI.

“We’re kind of at this technological moment where this way of developing has sort of reached its natural frontier,” Feder said. “I don’t think you can say that games like this will always be around.”

As millions of people immersed themselves in Vice City, the tech industry witnessed another long-awaited moment: Anthropic became the first major AI lab to go public. At Nasdaq HQ, Anthropic executives gathered to celebrate the Claude maker’s $81.3 billion IPO. At virtually the same moment that Anthropic broke the record for the largest IPO ever, GTA VI was also breaking records with the highest-rated and best-selling game launch of all time.

But when the Anthropic team rang the bell on Thursday, the sound also signaled the end of an era in video game development, and perhaps even in the arts at large.

The AI industry operates with efficiency as its north star. The promise of this technology is that we will be able to automate so much work that we can expand the capabilities of humanity, avoiding needless time spent tinkering with ultimately fruitless ideas. Instead, a prompt goes into an LLM, and a response is spat out.

This technological revolution exploded into the world with some soul-crushing side effects: children are inundated with mind-numbing slop on YouTube, lawyers cite nonexistent cases in court, and tech companies like Meta lay off tens of thousands of people as part of a corporate-branded “Year of Efficiency.” But against the backdrop of the AI industry’s first major IPO, Rockstar has unveiled a game that about 5,000 people labored over for more than a decade.

The final product is breathtaking, looking so lifelike that it can be hard to distinguish gameplay footage from real life. These sprawling open-world games are usually made possible by procedurally generating new landscapes and objects, but Rockstar has taken the time to painstakingly craft every detail of the world, even developing 30,000 different nonplayable characters with unique appearances and personalities.

The video game industry is already grappling with the threat of AI. Google’s Project Genie, which launched earlier this year, allows users to create and wander around 3D worlds generated from prompts to an LLM. The stock prices of several video game companies faltered around the time of Project Genie’s release, perhaps because of investors’ uncertainty about the future relevance of video game developers.

“The notion that somehow new tools would allow an individual to push a button and generate a hit, market a hit, and bring it to many millions of consumers around the world, it’s a laughable notion,” Take-Two Interactive CEO Strauss Zelnick said at the time. “It’s just never been the case with entertainment.”

Zelnick’s fellow tech CEOs see different future, though. On a Meta earnings call this year, CEO Mark Zuckerberg promised that, “soon, you’ll have an AI that understands you and [can] even generate great personalized content for you.”

Elon Musk even went as far as proposing that a game like GTA VI could be made with AI.

“You won’t even have to ask,” Musk wrote on X. “AI will figure what video game you’d like best.”

This is the paradox of “Grandthropic” week. GTA VI is so impressively vast and captivating because its developers could make mistakes, experiment, and rework ideas, even if that meant delaying the game (twice). GTA VI was incubated with the exact inefficiencies that investors hope AI companies like Anthropic can help eradicate.

“Plenty of fantastic art — video games and elsewhere — almost by definition has to be created slowly, and there’s something to having that kind of inefficiency around it,” Feder said. “Creative output requires deletion, right? Getting to kind of a masterpiece oftentimes requires an enormous amount of deletion. And when you’re talking about a game of the kind of complexity we’re talking about, that kind of deletion ends up being very expensive, and it is by definition not efficient.”

GTA VI reeks of its own humanity – every small detail is loaded with intention, but it’s this impressive precision that keeps the world abuzz, triggering the kind of monocultural moment that has become increasingly rare in a media landscape that revolves around highly personalized social media algorithms.

The game forces the player to slow down, which is either frustrating or genius, depending on your sense of humor. People waited more than three presidential terms for the next installment in a high-octane action franchise, only to download a 212 GB behemoth of a game in which your earliest missions involve driving around doing errands and putting up drywall to make your girlfriend happy. Don’t worry — there are still car chases and shootouts — but in a moment when AI feels suffocating, GTA VI is such an artistic achievement that it is reaching audiences that don’t even like violent video games.

The ultimate fantasy of GTA VI is no longer to role-play as a criminal — the fantasy is just taking your time to relish the mundane and explore an expansive world that developers were given the time and space to perfect.

#396. A month with the iPhone Duo has changed how I use my phone

For years, the definition of a premium smartphone depended on its cameras, processors, and how light it felt when you stowed it in your pocket. The iPhone Duo challenges that concept by asking consumers to pay more for a fundamentally different smartphone experience, even if it means sacrificing what they’ve long thought of as high-end features.

In a little under a month of using the Duo, I’ve found that the trade-offs I was initially concerned about matter far less to me than I had expected. What’s been more interesting is how the Duo’s foldable form factor has changed my everyday habits, including how I interact with apps, which apps I use, and how I organize and personalize the phone itself.

I also easily forgot a number of my initial concerns about switching to the Duo. The apps, for the most part, are now optimized for the Duo; I’ve reacquainted myself with Touch ID; and I don’t miss the Pro Max camera on most days.

That’s not to say the Duo doesn’t have its shortcomings. I still miss that telephoto lens sometimes, and I’m still longing for Face ID — though not as much since Apple shipped a face unlock feature in the latest update. But after a month of using the Duo, I’ve found these compromises have had less of an impact on my day-to-day experience compared with the benefits that the new form factor brings.

I was surprised by how much I’ve grown to appreciate the Duo’s multitasking capabilities. Initially, I didn’t think I’d regularly use it to view two apps side by side, given the still fairly limited screen space even while the phone is unfolded, especially compared with an iPad. But I’ve found myself snapping apps to both halves of the screen more often than I had imagined.

For starters, Apple has made this side-by-side mode incredibly intuitive and easy to use — you simply drag the app over to the left or right side of the screen and drop it into the target box that appears.

I have regularly taken advantage of this feature to do all sorts of things.

I really enjoy keeping X open as I read through the news, which allows me to switch back and forth between the article I’m reading and real-time reactions to it. Sometimes I’ll keep email or Slack open on the side while I’m working on something else so I don’t miss an important message or notification.

And much like the overstimulated members of Gen Z (which I am not), I sometimes like scrolling social media while engaging with some other media off to the side.

Case in point: I passively watched Netflix’s “Love is Blind” reunion while reading the gossipy tweets and Reddit posts about the main characters on the other side of the screen. This sort of TV watching behavior used to be called second-screen viewing, but now it’s morphing into something new. Everything is happening on one screen, so should we call it “Duo screen viewing,” perhaps? It makes me long for even more interactive TV experiences where commentary and content are combined.

I’ve noticed another shift in my behavior since adopting the Duo as my main device. When I’m out and about, the Duo feels more like an (arguably heavier) iPhone mini. For those who still miss smaller iPhones, the Duo is the next best thing. Despite its weight, the phone feels small. You can cover it with one hand; you can type with a thumb; you can reach the top and bottom easily; and you can even fit it into your front and back pockets. The Pro Max now feels unbelievably strange in my hand — how was I ever comfortable wrangling this giant rectangle?

The Duo’s small screen works well when you’re on the go, especially for short interactions like responding to a quick text or pulling up directions to a destination. It’s not as great for mindless scrolling, which I appreciate, as it forces me to engage with the real world instead of killing every moment of boredom or unease by opening up apps.

But when you’re home or somewhere with time to kill, you can unfold the screen and enjoy your apps super-sized. It’s the best of both worlds.

I find that I instinctively unfold the phone when I’m preparing to watch videos, because the larger screen is best for that. It’s also unfolded if I’m shopping, as it’s more convenient for looking at products, zooming in to examine the details, or complete checkout forms.

Generally, I also unfold the Duo when I’m actively using apps. This setup has also allowed me to rethink my Home Screen layout: With the Duo closed, I prefer to see just widgets on the small screen with key information like weather, time, battery life, and so on. Meanwhile, the only app icons on the Home Screen are those in the dock.

In the weeks since its launch, most of the apps that weren’t yet optimized for the Duo have been upgraded to support its foldable form factor. In mid-November, Apple said there were over 200,000 Duo-ready apps, and in my experience, that certainly feels true. A majority of my so-called “daily drivers” — with the glaring exception of key Google apps like Gmail and Drive — have dropped the black band on the side of the screen that was frustrating early testers of the phone.

Most apps now take advantage of the available space, whether that’s on the Duo’s small front screen or the wider, unfolded one.

I’ve also found it interesting to see the different interpretations developers have had for how to best optimize their app for Duo.

Some have used the form factor as a way to bring in some sort of novelty: there’s a note-taking app that opens and closes like a notebook, with pages you can write on with the Apple Pencil; a retro Walkman-like music player; a tiny typewriter; a gaming handheld; a game where you close the phone to make grilled cheese.

As much as I love the whimsy of this group of apps, I appreciate how my everyday apps feel better to use on the unfolded screen. Spotify, at last, feels less cluttered when it has more screen real estate. A number of note-taking, list-making, and calendar apps also feel somehow more usable when spread out.

The Netflix app is actually fantastic with its support for multiple viewing modes, which include playing video on the closed outer display, the “laptop” orientation with its big player controls, and the “tent” mode.

Other developers have similarly reimagined how their app would work if it had originally been designed for the Duo’s small and larger screens.

One good example of this is Sofa, a handy utility for organizing the books, movies, TV shows, podcasts, albums, and games you want to check out. When the Duo is closed, its navigation is tucked off to the right side in a vertical bar, while the main screen focuses on the items you’ve saved. But when the Duo is unfolded, the number of items displayed on the screen also expands into a two-column layout, with more of your items featured on the screen and your lists on the left side. It doesn’t feel like two different apps, but like you’ve opened up the app itself and given it more space to spread out.

In terms of your daily social media scroll, apps like TikTok and Instagram have been updated to be perfectly usable on the Duo, whether the phone is open or closed. When closed, TikTok now stretches the video to cover the screen, instead of showing the ugly black bar. Scrolling short-form videos on the Duo, while unfolded like a slightly smaller iPad mini, has become my preferred way to watch.

Here’s another surprise: I’ve spent less time with my Kindle, and am instead reading books on my Duo. Unfolded, the Duo is a decent enough replacement for an e-book reader, albeit it can be a bit more stimulating as it’s not an e-ink device. However, the matte inner screen isn’t quite as bright and distracting as your typical glossy OLED display. There’s less glare and softer reflections, but there’s also something about the display that just feels calmer than a smartphone, allowing you to more fully engage with a book’s text.

Unfolded and held vertically, reading on the Duo feels a lot like reading on the standard Kindle, at least in terms of hand-feel. And putting your phone into the “Do Not Disturb” focus mode can help you not be distracted by incoming notifications and messages while you read.

You can also unfold the device while keeping it in landscape orientation, with the screens bent slightly inward like a real book. For even more fun, you can turn on Kindle’s page-turn animation, which makes it seem as if you’re turning the page in a physical book. And I love how you can “performatively read” using an app like Book2Life or Actually Read — they display the book’s cover on the Duo’s outer screen as you read the text on the inner screen.

E-book readers will likely find they can carry one less device when they’re out and about, because the Duo can stand in for whatever Kindle or other reader you typically keep in your purse or bag. You can save the Kindle for other times, whether that’s at home, on long travel days, or when you don’t want to worry about sand getting into your $2,000 phone.

On the big-screened iPhone 17 Pro Max I used to carry, I tended to keep a PopSocket of some sort on the back because the phone was both heavy and large, which made it hard to hold for longer periods of time. The Duo is similarly heavy, but its shape makes it far easier to hold in one hand without the extra help.

Folded, you can do the usual one-handed hold where the bottom edge of the phone rests on your pinky, your other three fingers cradle the back, and your thumb is free to navigate the screen.

Unfolded, the Duo’s like using a small Kindle or iPad mini, where two hands are required. You also don’t need a PopSocket for balancing the phone on the side, because it can be propped up like a tent or opened like a tiny laptop. Or, if you prefer, Apple and others offer Duo cases with a built-in stand. (Apple’s case comes in navy and taupe, though, so if you’re not boring, you’ll probably want to shop from third-party vendors).

If you think you still might need a PopSocket at times, or you just aren’t ready to give it up, the brand now makes a slimmer version: its Low-Pro Grip. There are other competitors, like the OhSnap grips, which aren’t a bad alternative either.

There’s something about the Duo that seems to have kicked off a new iPhone personalization trend, at least judging by what I’ve been seeing on social media.

Unlike a regular iPhone, where every phone case is simply a shell that offers a degree of protection based on the materials used, the Duo case lineup provides far more variety.

You can buy cases with and without built-in screen protectors, and some use adhesive to better stick to the outer screen, while others don’t. Some cases let you customize the band around the outer screen with a different color than the case that protects the rest of the phone.

Then you have cases that cover the hinge, while others leave it exposed. There are cases that offer holes where you can attach straps, wristlets, and charms. Some of those have attachment points at the bottom and others on the side.

Essentially, there are a lot of options for customizing how you want to carry the Duo and how you want to express your own style.

I’m also now starting to see whimsical cases that come with attached pens. (Thank goodness — most of the pen cases so far have been kind of plain!)

I also believe there’s something about the Duo that particularly appeals to women. Maybe because it’s small, or maybe it’s because you can attach a strap to its side and carry it like a little purse. Or maybe because, like a purse, you can deck it out with chains and charms — a choice that’s grown increasingly popular as young people have embraced whimsical, retro tech as a way to express their individuality and sense of fashion.

The Duo easily lends itself to this trend, perhaps even more than a regular iPhone. It becomes an accessory you want on display, not one buried in a bag.

"We don't have all of this data that we're used to having with compounds like this," he said. "We have the opportunity to get it right, and I think we see that as our responsibility with a company of our size, our skill, and resources to really lead."

Vinjamoori added that people who have serious illnesses, such as cardiovascular disease, liver disease and kidney disease, will not be candidates for the compounds for now.

Meanwhile, Andrew Huberman, a well known neuroscientist, podcaster and Stanford University School of Medicine professor who recently joined Hims' board of directors, in a statement called compounds "real medical treatments, not just a wellness trend." He said Hims provides safe access through "trusted sourcing and physician oversight."

But health experts raised concerns about the broader mainstream marketing of peptides in the U.S. and accessing them on telehealth platforms.

"With peptides, I think you have to view them the same way you would view any drug that hasn't been rigorously tested," said Joshua Kritzer, a chemistry professor at Tufts University. "Without actual testing, all you have is anecdotal information, and that's just not enough information to put a substance in your body."

Kritzer said the lack of large clinical trials means researchers can't rule out potentially serious, rare complications from peptides — the kinds of risks that have led regulators to reject or pull otherwise effective drugs from the market after they underwent comprehensive testing in humans.

Without that testing, it's also unclear how telehealth firms, physicians and pharmacies can make decisions about what doses patients should take, said Paul Knoepfler, a cell biology and human anatomy professor at UC Davis Health. While some people rely on anecdotal data from real peptide users, "the dosing for these products really has not been established," he added.

Knoepfler said one risk is that injecting too much of a synthetic peptide into patients could trigger "weird immune responses that are really bad for one's health" because the body might recognize it as foreign.

"With the peptides, it's very loosey-goosey," Knoepfler told CNBC. "How does one even know what would be the effective amount for whatever you're hoping it will do, and what amount would have more risks?"

But he said the more contact patients can have with physicians around taking a compounded peptide, the better. Providers, including those tied to telehealth firms, should make sure patients are well-informed about the possible risks and unknowns of a given product and consider their preexisting conditions, cancer history and other medications, Knoepfler said.

That appears to be consistent with the approach Hims & Hers is taking. The company says patients who use the peptide and longevity products can also use its Care+ offering, which pairs treatment with ongoing support. That includes twice-a-year lab testing to track their progress and the ability to message providers trained in peptide therapy around the clock with any questions.

Still, Kritzer argued that having a doctor who only knows a patient from online, essentially mail-order platforms or websites "is not enough supervision to put you on a prescription medication," especially an unapproved one.

Hims' ability to legally sell some of these peptides stems from a major shift in federal oversight by the Health and Human Services Department. It issued a policy in October that says the Food and Drug Administration will not take enforcement action against licensed pharmacies, federal facilities or physicians that compound and dispense six unapproved peptides while formal rules are reviewed.

In practice, that means these pharmacies and other firms can now offer them, even though the peptides aren't yet on the FDA's official list of ingredients compounding pharmacies are allowed to make. It does not translate to formal approval of the specific peptides.

The policy followed a 2023 decision by the Biden administration where the FDA restricted compounding pharmacies from producing popular peptide formulations due to safety, impurity and immune-reaction concerns.

HHS Secretary ​Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has supported broader access to peptides, calling himself a "big fan" of the treatments and arguing that restrictions were driving Americans to potentially unsafe products from overseas. Kennedy has previously described using BPC-157 to treat a muscle injury.

The announcement also comes after Hims in February 2025 acquired a California-based peptide manufacturing facility equipped with advanced peptide synthesizers.

The evidence for peptide efficacy varies by compound.

A 2026 systematic review screening 113 studies, including both small trials on humans along with rodent research, found zero eligible clinical outcome trials proving that administering IV or intramuscular NAD+ improves anti-aging, energy, or wellness endpoints in humans.

There is some evidence for specific uses of certain peptides, such as potential weight loss. A review of 37 L-carnitine trials found participants lost about 2.7 pounds on average, though that analysis does not demonstrate that a compounded injection works the same way.

Meanwhile, vitamin B-12 injections are most commonly used to help people with a diagnosed deficiency rather than preventative care. U.S. survey data shows only 3.6% of adults are B-12 deficient.

Glutathione's evidence is thin, too. IV injections of it are widely marketed for skin whitening, yet not FDA-approved for this purpose.

A small study published in the Journal of Pakistan Association of Dermatologists evaluating IV glutathione showed that the treatment is "not very effective" for skin-lightening, and that it loses efficacy with time.

The FDA also said in August that it received reports of at least 30 patients who experienced adverse events after they received compounded IV glutathione alone or in combination with other ingredients from different compounding pharmacies.

All of the glutathione tied to the reports were from the same wholesale distributor and was labeled as dietary supplement grade, which is not an appropriate ingredient to make injectable drugs. Two of the Texas pharmacies conducted recalls related to elevated endotoxin levels in their glutathione injectable drugs.

These studies and reports don't speak to how Hims' exact combination will perform, but they do highlight the current limits on data studying the treatments and the questions surrounding them.
