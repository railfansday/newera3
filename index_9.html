#482. All 7 Anthropic founders will each cash out about $250M in IPO

As Anthropic barrels toward an enormous IPO that could value it at $1.4 trillion, the company has disclosed a tidbit in its new S-1 filing about how many shares each of its seven co-founders plan to sell.

Each will sell 3,378,378 shares. Anthropic has floated an unusually broad initial price range of $70 to $78 a share. At the midrange of $74, each founder would take in just shy of $250 million.

At the low end, $70, each would have to settle for pocketing just over $236 million in cash; at the high end, $78, each would take in just under $264 million.

However, in an IPO as eagerly anticipated as this one, companies can often price shares above the initial range.

The S-1 is already hinting at high demand. Board member Reed Hastings has indicated he wants to buy $300 million worth of shares through his donor-advised fund. Board member Christopher Liddell also wants to buy $3 million worth of shares.

The seven co-founders are Dario Amodei, CEO; Daniela Amodei, president and chair of the board; Tom Brown, chief compute officer; Jack Clark, head of public benefit; Jared Kaplan, chief science officer (pictured above); Sam McCandlish, chief architect; and Christopher Olah, interpretability research lead.

After the sale, each of them will own about 3.2% of the total outstanding Class A shares, the S-1 says, or roughly 22% collectively. That’s not their share of the company overall. Class A is just one of Anthropic’s share classes, and other holders, including Amazon and Google, hold nonvoting Class N shares, so the founders’ stake in the company’s total equity will be considerably smaller. Even still, they, as a group, remain in control of the company. Each will also receive one share of Class F stock, which carries supervoting rights. Collectively, the founders will control 51.1% of the votes, but no single founder will hold significantly more shares or votes than the others.

As is now industry lore, the seven left OpenAI to start Anthropic in 2021 after disagreements with OpenAI’s leadership over how AI should be built. Siblings Dario and Daniela Amodei, who held senior research and policy roles at OpenAI, were joined by five fellow OpenAI colleagues in forming a company that has said, very publicly and from the start, that it’s more focused on AI safety than its rivals, including the founders’ former employer.

#479. X-energy gets green light to build its first small modular nuclear reactors

Nuclear startup X-energy announced Monday that it had been given the go-ahead from federal officials to build its first four commercial reactors.

The Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) approved the construction permit application for four of X-energy’s Xe-100 reactors at a Dow chemical plant in Texas. Each reactor will be capable of generating 80 megawatts of electricity and 200 megawatts of heat. X-energy says the approval is the first for an advanced nuclear reactor at an industrial site in the United States.

The U.S. nuclear industry has pinned its hopes on small modular reactors — or SMRs — like those being developed by X-energy. By shrinking the reactor, proponents say it will speed construction and reduce costs, though those advantages have yet to be proven. No SMR is currently in operation in the U.S., and X-energy says Dow’s reactors won’t be operational until the early 2030s.

X-energy claims that its reactor design, which flows helium gas over uranium-containing fuel pellets, is safer than many existing reactors, but the Union of Concerned Scientists questioned the claim and the speed with which X-energy received the permit.

“The NRC only accomplished this feat by pushing consideration of critical safety questions to a later date—issues the NRC should’ve fully resolved before green lighting construction,” Edwin Lyman, director of nuclear power safety for the organization, said in a statement.

The nuclear industry has benefited from strong interest from hyperscalers and data center developers, who are desperate for any and all sources of electricity. Amazon’s Climate Pledge Fund led X-energy’s $500 million Series C-1, and the big tech company has also committed to buying 5 gigawatts of capacity from the nuclear startup.

X-energy rode that wave of excitement earlier this year when its April initial public offering raised $1 billion. But since then, investors’ optimism has tempered.

The hesitancy isn’t surprising: Nuclear power plants are expensive to build and can take years to complete. Eventually, X-energy hopes to rein in both time and cost, but its first reactors are likely to be pricey.

The new permit approval gave X-energy a small jolt yesterday, though it was only enough to recover last week’s losses. The company’s shares are trading at less than half their initial offering price.

#474. Proximal Ventures raises debut fund to back startups bringing AI to drug development

While established venture firms continue to attract capital, first-time fund managers are facing one of the toughest fundraising environments in years. But that didn’t stop Sahir Raoof, a former SignalFire investor and executive at two healthcare startups, from raising $27.5 million for his first solo VC fund.

Raoof’s firm, Proximal Ventures, is betting that AI can transform the tools, infrastructure, and services that support drug development.

Pharmaceutical companies have historically outsourced various stages of drug development to specialized service providers, from synthesizing thousands of molecular compounds and figuring out how to manufacture drugs at scale to analyzing clinical trial data.

“For a long time, the assumption was that pharma is not a great buyer of software, but a really good buyer of services,” Raoof told TechCrunch.

Raoof believes AI could automate much of that work, creating an opportunity for startups to capture billions of dollars in pharmaceutical industry spending. By using AI, these companies could deliver the same services at a lower cost and in significantly less time, he said.

Some of the most valuable applications of AI so far have been in service-oriented industries like law and finance, Raoof said. He sees similar opportunities in pharmaceutical services.

Proximal plans to invest in pre-seed and seed-stage startups, writing checks from $500,000 to $1.5 million. The firm has already backed seven startups, including ChronicleBio, co-founded by former OpenAI executive Fidji Simo, which uses AI to analyze biological and medical data to better understand chronic diseases and identify potential treatments.

Before spending two years as a principal at SignalFire, Raoof led business development at clinical trial recruitment startup Iterative Health and previously oversaw partnerships at Flatiron Health, an oncology data company acquired by Roche for $1.9 billion in 2018.

“If you’re building a company that’s selling into the life sciences, one of the hardest things to do is starting that initial commercial flywheel,” Raoof said.

His sales chops, deep understanding of the healthcare ecosystem, and extensive industry relationships gave limited partners, the investors in VC funds, the confidence that he could spot strong startups and help them land customers. Proximal’s backers include VC firms SignalFire and Kleiner Perkins, Bertelsmann Healthcare Investments, and individual investors such as Dimension Capital co-founders Nan Li and Zavain Dar.

Amy Abernethy, co-founder of Highlander Health and former principal deputy commissioner of the FDA, is also an investor. She first met Raoof when they worked together at Flatiron Health, where she served as chief medical officer. She told TechCrunch that his ability to build relationships and spot industry bottlenecks made a lasting impression. Abernethy is convinced that his abilities, along with the understanding of what the healthcare market needs, will make him a successful investor.

It is these qualities that have helped Proximal invest in startups alongside some of the better-known, much larger funds, she added.

#470. OpenAI, Anthropic face new oversight as New York’s AI safety law takes effect

New York is gearing up to enforce one of the country’s most significant AI safety laws. The state opened a registration portal Tuesday, with which large frontier AI developers like Anthropic and OpenAI will be required to register by January 1, 2027.

The RAISE Act, New York’s safety and transparency bill, kicks in at the start of the year. It mandates that large frontier developers — companies with more than $500 million in annual revenue — have to publish safety frameworks on their websites, report critical safety incidents within 72 hours, and issue public model transparency reports.

The newly formed Office of Digital Innovation, Governance, Integrity, and Trust (DIGIT) will implement the RAISE Act and oversee large frontier AI labs. Penalties can be as high as $1 million for one violation and $3 million for subsequent violations.

The state also can bring enforcement actions against companies that fail to register with the new portal, though a spokesperson declined to provide specifics.

“Tech moguls tell us that their vision for AI is inevitable,” Governor Kathy Hochul said Tuesday during a press briefing. “They tell us get on board or get left behind… That any collateral damage is worth it as long as we win the race to the AI cliff.”

On Tuesday, Hochul also launched the New York Center for AI Responsibility and Research based at State University of New York’s Binghamton University. The center will independently evaluate the safety, security, and operation of advanced AI systems and work with DIGIT to review companies’ safety frameworks. Hochul first announced the center in January with $55 million in donations and capital.

“We’re building a national model for smart, sensible AI regulation, a model that puts communities above corporations and people above powerful technology,” Hochul said.

The RAISE Act isn’t the only safety law New York has passed. Last year, the state began enforcing a law that requires AI companion operators to regularly let users know they’re talking to a bot, and to make reasonable efforts to detect suicidal ideation and self-harm.

In October, Hochul signed a separate chatbot safety law for minors, restricting behaviors like deceptive human-like interactions, emotionally manipulative or sycophantic responses, and encouragement of behaviors like suicide or disordered eating. That law takes effect June 1, 2027.

Other states have also passed similar AI safety and transparency bills to the RAISE Act. California’s SB 53, which took effect in January, mandates that frontier AI labs have in place and publish safety frameworks, enact whistleblower protections, and report critical safety incidents within 15 days. Illinois’ SB 315, which will go into effect at the start of 2027, has similar requirements, plus includes independent safety compliance audits.

#466. OpenAI acquires Odessia, travel startup created by Sonder founder

OpenAI has acquired an AI travel agent called Odessia, according to Odessia’s founder, Francis Davidson. Davidson says in a post announcing the deal that he’s now working for OpenAI, helping the AI lab with the future of travel.

Terms of the deal were not disclosed, but this looks like an acqui-hire. Davidson’s announcement focused largely on how he built his AI travel agent Odessia by directing teams of AI agents. The year-old Odessia raised a $6 million pre-seed round led by Sequoia and launched its AI travel agent around five months ago. Davidson says that it had helped early adopters plan millions of dollars’ worth of travel, but the tool has already been shut down.

An OpenAI spokesperson confirmed the hire to TechCrunch. “We’re thrilled to welcome Francis to OpenAI. Francis is a proven founder who has built multiple businesses to help people travel, and we’re excited he’s chosen to build with us.”

However the AI lab has not announced new travel-specific products or plans. It does, however, count such companies as Bookings.com, Expedia, and KAYAK as customers or partners. Travel is also one of the main areas that people are using personal AI assistants to help with. And, back in July 2025, when OpenAI launched its ChatGPT agent, it mentioned travel as one of its potential uses.

OpenAI’s spokesperson didn’t have more to share on its plans for travel-related products, except to say that travel is one of the areas that chatbot users frequently ask about. “1.2 billion people turn to ChatGPT each week for help with everyday tasks — from understanding their health, figuring out what to make for dinner, building a budget, and even planning a trip. We see a real opportunity to make these experiences even more useful,” the spokesperson said.

Acquiring Odessia is interesting for another reason. Davidson is well known in the travel industry after he founded a travel startup called Sonder as a college student in 2014. It was sort of like Airbnb, but it focused on short-term leasing of upscale apartments in major cities worldwide. The pandemic badly hurt it, and as it climbed out of that rut, it went public via a SPAC but didn’t raise as much money as it needed, Davidson later said in a post announcing his resignation as CEO in June 2025.

When a key partnership with Marriott fell through, the company went into bankruptcy and wound down. The bankruptcy trustee sold Sonder’s name, trademarks, and website domain in 2026 to TravelAI, and that website operates as a curated guide to the kind of travel rental properties that Sonder was once known for. Prior to its downfall, Davidson had built Sonder into a unicorn with $143 million in revenues. While Sonder was not, ultimately, a success story, Davidson is the kind of battle-scarred founder that Silicon Valley often values.

So we’ll have to wait and see what OpenAI has in store now with travel, now that he’s joined the company.

#462. X launches its own clipping feature to spur influencer engagement

Over the past few years, X has released a number of features to make itself a friendlier hub for influencers. The latest effort to attract social media talent? Its own clipping tool.

Clipping is the pivotal social media technique by which longer videos are spliced into TikTok-attention-span length “clips” and then strategically posted to engage as many eyeballs (and thus derive as much ad revenue) as possible.

On Monday, X announced the tool, which, from what we can tell, appears to be little more than a straightforward video-trimming feature that lets users edit videos and post them to X.

The site says that every clip includes a “Clip from” label that links back to the original video from which it was derived, allowing X users to see more about the creator and the creator’s content.

“Grab your favorite moment from any video, add your take, and post it without leaving X,” the company posted.

The platform added that both creators and clippers can “earn from views on the clip through Original Content Rewards,” X’s rewards program. The program is only available for eligible creators and pays out based on specific levels of audience engagement.

In many cases, influencers pay special firms to handle clipping for them. More and more large companies are also turning to such services to run marketing campaigns, and the technique has been used in political campaigns as well.

#360. CVS Health plans to split itself up. Here's why a breakup could make sense now

CVS Health plans to break itself up — and this time, the bet looks a lot more viable.

The healthcare conglomerate on Wednesday announced plans to split into two publicly traded companies by the first half of 2028. CVS would separate its pharmacy business – including roughly 9,000 retail locations – from its major insurance business and pharmacy benefit manager, Caremark.

The move comes more than two years after CVS considered a breakup but ultimately walked away from the idea. At the time, analysts questioned whether the retail pharmacy business could survive on its own.

“Back then, the idea of retail standing alone was unthinkable,” Brian Tanquilut, senior equity research analyst at Jefferies, told CNBC.

But the economics of the businesses have changed: CVS's retail pharmacy business has become more consistently profitable, while its insurer, Aetna, has improved its performance despite persistently high medical costs in Medicare Advantage plans. That could make it easier for the company to separate the units — and for investors to recognize the value of each on its own, rather than valuing them together as one sprawling healthcare conglomerate.

“For us, it's showing the stability of the pharmacy,” Michael Cherny, senior research analyst at Leerink Partners, said in an interview. “This business is a much different business from the one that's been considered three years ago, five years ago, 10 years ago, where there had been a litany of headwinds that the pharmacy just couldn't overcome.”

CVS CEO David Joyner told CNBC’s "Squawk on the Street" on Wednesday that back in 2024, the company was "in a turnaround" without the flexibility, cash or business performance to execute a split. Now, CVS has posted eight consecutive quarters of beating Wall Street earnings estimates and has raised its guidance several times over the last two years, thanks to improvement across its PBM, pharmacy and insurer.

Since taking the helm in 2024, Joyner has pursued an aggressive turnaround plan that involves $2 billion in cost cuts, store closures, leadership shakeups and efforts to rein in Medicare Advantage costs.

The company also now expects to generate at least $15.5 billion in operating cash flow in 2026, a roughly 70% increase from $9.1 billion in 2024, and says it has reached its leverage target ahead of schedule.

"What's changed in two years is the fact that we now can look at this business and the assets that are assembled and really determine, without any distress or without anything that we have to do, what is the right strategic decision to make," Joyner told analysts on a call Wednesday, adding that the decision comes "out of strength."

While investors have largely cheered CVS's progress in recent years, Wall Street appeared more pessimistic about Wednesday's announcement, as the company's shares dropped about 12%.

CVS's retail business has benefited from changes in the competitive landscape, including the widespread retrenchment and store closures of Walgreens, Rite Aid and other pharmacy operators over the last few years. That’s created opportunities for CVS to gain market share, some analysts told CNBC.

CVS has also introduced changes to its pharmacy reimbursement model. Its CostVantage program is designed to replace the traditional process with a more transparent, cost-based model. Some analysts say that has helped the company better align reimbursement with the cost of dispensing prescriptions and contributed to steadier profits.

Those developments represent a meaningful shift for a business that has faced years of pressure from declining prescription reimbursement, store closures and competition from online pharmacies and other retailers.

That doesn't mean the retail pharmacy has become a high-growth business. CVS's retail pharmacy segment remains a relatively low-margin operation, with adjusted operating margins of roughly 4% to 5%. But analysts say it may now be stable and profitable enough to operate independently – even if investors value it at a discount to CVS's other businesses.

In a note Wednesday, RBC Capital Markets analyst Ben Hendrix said the lower valuation investors assign to the retail pharmacy unit has weighed on CVS's overall value and obscured the earnings strength of businesses like Aetna and Caremark.

The underlying growth of the insurance and PBM businesses is also stronger than that of retail pharmacy, Tanquilut said, after accounting for changes to Caremark's rebate arrangements.

Aetna in particular has gotten a better handle on medical costs by exiting unprofitable markets, expanding value-based care models, and tightening utilization management.

The breakup also comes as Caremark faces mounting scrutiny over PBM practices, including state-level legal and regulatory challenges involving the businesses and their relationships with pharmacies.

Republicans in Tennessee and Arkansas have passed laws that would ban any one company from owning both a pharmacy and a PBM — thought the Arkansas measure has been held up in court. Cherny noted that legal hurdles involving PBMs in other states have continued to pile up.

“Through what seems to be a never-ending litany of state lawsuits, it feels like it's been under more pressure,” Cherny said. “It's unclear if it just became, for lack of a better term, too tenuous to move forward with those two entities combined as one. I’m not sure.”

Even so, Joyner told the Wall Street Journal that the company wasn’t splitting up because of regulatory issues. CVS has said the separation follows a strategic review and is intended to give each business greater focus and flexibility.

The move also marks a significant departure from the integrated healthcare strategy CVS spent years building.

CVS spent roughly $70 billion to acquire Aetna in 2018 and argued that combining health insurance, pharmacy benefit management, retail pharmacies and health providers under one roof would help coordinate care and create a competitive advantage. CVS was still promoting that model as recently as its investor day last year.

The proposed split suggests the company now sees more value in separating at least some of those businesses – though it is not abandoning vertical integration altogether.

Aetna and Caremark will remain together in the healthcare company, alongside CVS’s specialty pharmacy, its primary care clinic Oak Street Health and its home-visit provider Signify Health. The separate pharmacy company will include roughly 9,000 retail stores, mail pharmacy operations and MinuteClinic, a retail medical clinic located inside certain CVS locations.

Both sides are large enough that they can stand on their own, Cherny said. Caremark is one of the country's three largest PBMs, while CVS has one of the largest retail pharmacy footprints in the U.S.

But separating the company raises questions about how much of the commercial relationship between the businesses can survive.

The relationship has historically benefited both sides. As it stands, Caremark can use CVS's extensive retail footprint to offer members convenient access to prescriptions and steer them toward CVS locations through preferred pharmacy arrangements. Programs such as Maintenance Choice, for example, give eligible members the option to fill certain maintenance medications at CVS retail pharmacies or through mail order.

Those arrangements also helped direct prescription volume to CVS's stores, giving the retail pharmacy unit an important source of customers. Because the businesses shared a parent company, the financial benefits of that relationship ultimately went to CVS as a whole.

In a note, RBC's Hendrix also raised questions about how the split could impact Aetna's Medicare Advantage quality ratings, which determine whether health plans qualify for bonus payments from the government. Retail pharmacists have played a role in engaging Aetna members, and a separation could complicate that relationship.

But Joyner told analysts Wednesday that the synergy between the two businesses would be "preserved in commercial agreements between the two organizations," adding that "we don't need the integration of an operating structure."

Joyner said CVS will be working on those potential agreements over the next 15 to 18 months.

Preferred pharmacy networks are common across the industry, and separate ownership does not prevent Caremark from continuing to favor CVS pharmacies through negotiated arrangements, Cherny said. The difference is that under separate ownership, the profits from those arrangements would no longer accrue to a single company, he noted.

“The issue is not whether the companies can continue working together in some capacity, but whether any future contractual arrangements preserve the SAME competitive advantages and flexibility as today under common ownership,” Cherny said in an afternoon note on Wednesday.

Still, both businesses face challenges on their own.

Tanquilut cautioned that some of CVS's recent retail pharmacy gains have come as Walgreens and other competitors have closed stores, raising questions about whether the growth can be sustained. The business also remains exposed to consumer pressure and competition from online pharmacies.

Caremark, meanwhile, faces continued regulatory scrutiny and potential headwinds from changes to the federal 340B drug discount program, which requires drugmakers to offer discounted medications to eligible hospitals and other healthcare providers serving low-income and vulnerable patients.

CVS participates in the program by providing pharmacy services to those organizations, but tweaks to manufacturer data-sharing policies and potential reimbursement changes could weigh on that business in 2027, Tanquilut said.

Aetna, meanwhile, is still working through a multiyear plan to rebuild its margins, as it looks to improve the profitability of its Medicare Advantage business.

Ultimately, CVS is betting that the value of letting investors assess its businesses separately will outweigh the benefits of keeping them under one roof. The split will test whether each can thrive independently.

#306. Wendy's rejects $1.4 billion takeover proposal from Nelson Peltz’s Trian

Wendy's on Thursday said it is rejecting a $1.4 billion takeover offer from a group of investors led by Nelson Peltz's Trian Fund Management.

The group offered $7.35 per share in cash on Oct. 28 to take the struggling burger chain private. In addition to Trian, it included investment firms BlueFive Capital and Sixth Street Partners, plus Flynn Group, one of Wendy's largest franchisees.

In February, Wendy's board formed a committee of independent directors to assess any potential bid from Trian in response to a regulatory filing from the firm. The committee found that the proposal "substantially undervalues" Wendy's and does not reflect potential gains from the chain's ongoing turnaround strategy, the company said in a press release Thursday.

"Our confidence rests firmly in [CEO] Bob Wright's proven track record of seasoned industry leadership, including restaurant business turnarounds," Art Winkleblack, Wendy's board chair and a member of the independent committee, said in a statement.

Trian declined to comment on Wendy's decision.

To stave off a hostile takeover, Wendy's has adopted a shareholder rights plan — or poison pill — with a one-year duration, as of Wednesday, the company said. Wendy's added that the investor group led by Trian rejected an offer from Wendy's for a standstill agreement that would have paused additional share purchases, adding to Wendy's decision to use a poison pill.

The rejection is the latest in the long-running saga between Peltz and the burger chain, which dates back to an activist campaign he led more than two decades ago. A Peltz firm has even owned Wendy's before. In 2008, his company Triarc, which also owned Arby's, bought Wendy's in a $2.3 billion deal. It later sold Arby's, turning Wendy's into a stand-alone company again.

In 2024, Wendy's named Peltz as chairman emeritus after he spent 17 years on the company's board. Trian executive Peter May and Peltz's son, Bradley, still sit on Wendy's board.

Wendy's also reported its third-quarter results on Thursday. Its same-store sales declined for the seventh straight quarter. However, its quarterly earnings and revenue topped Wall Street's expectations.

Shares of Wendy's fell less than 1% in morning trading. The takeover offer had lifted the stock, which has lost about 60% of its value over the last three years.

Wendy's is not the only restaurant chain that has found itself in the crosshairs of activist investors. Applebee's owner Dine Brands recently adopted a poison pill to block a takeover bid from investment firms Arkhouse Management and Cobblers Cove Capital.
