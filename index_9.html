#468. Axiom Math wants to keep AI slop out of mathematics

Buy One Founder Summit Ticket, Get a Second for Free. Register now.

Founder Summit begins Nov. 4, 9:00 a.m. ET. Get your pass savings & deals before it starts. REGISTER HERE.

It feels like AI is cracking a new math problem every week. Last month, OpenAI released 719 manuscriptsclaiming solutions to some of the field’s hardest problems. But even the computer checks meant to confirm those results can slip. After years of AI slop, it’s fair to wonder whether math is next.

Axiom Math is betting math can avoid that fate, not by slowing down, but by standing on firmer ground. The $1.6 billion startup wants to build a verified foundation that AI agents can rely on to write software and, eventually, do science.

On Wednesday, the company announced it had formally verified the Stacks Project, one of the largest bodies of mathematics ever written by humans. Mathematicians often hold Stacks in rare regard — Columbia mathematician Peter Woit has claimed there’s “nothing else really comparable,” comparing the project to “the famous Bourbaki books,” a series that rebuilt math from its foundations in the 20th century.

Woit’s Columbia colleague Johan de Jong started the Stacks Project in 2005 as an open source reference. Mathematicians have contributed to it ever since, building out results from college-level algebra all the way up to the field’s most advanced ideas across algebra, geometry, and number theory.

The company’s fleet of AI agents translates the library’s roughly 7,654 pages into about 16 million lines of computer-checked code.

Formalizing math is a way of making sure the structure underneath it is sound. Mathematicians rewrite the proof as code in a programming language called Lean, and a program called a proof assistant checks the code line by line. If the proof passes, mathematicians can trust that it’s logically sound.

Engineers use the same idea, called formal verification, to confirm their chip designs work, while researchers in France have used it to catch bugs in software that calculates family benefits.

For a library like the Stacks Project, checking each theorem is only part of the job. The theorems and definitions in Stacks are interconnected, and each one builds on the ones before it. Like a family tree, many results in Stacks trace back to the ones that came before it.

That interconnectedness made Axiom unsure the project would work at first. The team had to keep every connection intact across about 16 million lines of Lean code.

“Things that are big and things that are delicate in life just don’t go together,” Axiom CEO Carina Hong told TechCrunch. “[Stacks] is a collection of mathematical results that is the foundation of mathematics. You want [theorems] to be consistent and not, like, paradoxical. You want the definitions to not fight with each other.”

The 25-year-old CEO dropped out of a Stanford math PhD program and founded the company in March 2025. In March, Axiom raised a $200 million Series A led by Menlo Ventures.

“We were not sure if vibe coding would be able to preserve the beauty of Stacks,” Hong said.

To pull off the project, the team gave its agents a loose structure and let them run in parallel. “With Stacks, we imposed a hierarchy … [but] it was not, like, a complete hierarchy of dictating what every single agent was doing,” said Ashvin Swaminathan, a member of Axiom’s technical staff. “The agents were finding tasks for themselves and identified things that they could work on in parallel.”

Axiom also trained its own models to be good at math, an approach Hong said most other verification startups haven’t taken. At first, the team relied on one of its in-house Lean experts to teach the agents a human sense of taste. Over time, Hong said, the agents have picked up taste on their own.

The result fills one of the biggest gaps in formalized math. “When you look at what has been formalized in mathematics, there’s this huge black hole. And that black hole covers algebraic geometry,” Swaminathan said.

Until now, researchers who wanted to check new work in algebraic geometry with Lean had little to build on, Swaminathan said. “Well, now they do.”

Axiom argues that each verified result becomes a building block, so future proofs can lean on checked math instead of starting from scratch. Terence Tao, the UCLA mathematician and Fields Medal winner, has made the general case more simply: “The more formalized proofs you have, the more formalized proofs you can make.”

Mathematicians aren’t Axiom’s only intended audience. Developers have long struggled to guarantee that their code is free of bugs and security holes. That problem grows as AI agents write more of the world’s code.

Axiom is betting that the software of the future will need to rest on verified foundations, the way Stacks now does.

“We believe that mathematics is the domain where you start building these things and that it’s a transferable skill,” Swaminathan said. “Agents can read our code, understand it, and build new infrastructure on top of it. Our Lean formalization is now the substrate that agents can build upon.”

The way Axiom’s agents built Stacks, working in parallel and picking their own tasks, is how Hong expects much of AI’s future work to happen. AI agents, she argues, will need a well-built, reusable body of knowledge to keep from producing slop.

“You want [agents] to have a reusable library for them to build on and keep pushing the frontier,” Hong said. “If you slop drop in a way that’s bad engineering choices in the beginning, then it’s very hard for them to grow that boundary.”

Hong is confident enough in that approach to issue a dare. “I openly challenge all the coding companies to work on formalization at this scale.”

Anthropic announced Thursday that Claude is getting a new tool called “Personal Finance,” designed to help users keep track of their money. With it, users can connect their bank and credit card accounts through Plaid and ask Claude questions about their finances. Anthropic says access is read-only, meaning Personal Finance can’t move, manage, or invest users’ money.

By letting Claude connect directly to bank and credit card accounts, Anthropic is positioning its chatbot as a financial companion that can help people understand their spending and make sense of their money, rather than simply offering them generic advice.

With the launch, Claude is playing catch-up to OpenAI’s ChatGPT, which introduced similar personal finance tools in May that also let users connect their financial accounts and ask questions about everything from spending patterns to long-term financial planning.

“Claude gives you answers in everyday language, from your balances and transactions, and explains any term that’s new to you,” Anthropic wrote in a blog post. “Like the rest of Claude, Personal Finance has no ads or sponsored recommendations, and it doesn’t offer credit cards, loans, or savings accounts of its own, so your money questions get a straight answer.”

For instance, at the end of the month, a user could ask Claude, “Where did my money go this month?” The chatbot will then add up spending across the connected accounts and sort it by category, such as rent and utilities, food and drink, and shopping. Users can choose to go deeper by following up with questions like, “Is that more than usual?” or “Anything else I’m missing?”

Anthropic says the tool goes beyond basic spending summaries by analyzing transactions across connected accounts to flag potential issues, such as duplicate charges, unexpected fees, and steadily increasing bills. Users can also ask broader questions about their finances, such as whether they’re spending more than they earn. The tool can also explain financial concepts like APR (annual percentage rate), while providing calculations that show users how much they’ve paid in interest over the past year.

Of course, some people will have reservations about connecting their financial accounts to an AI chatbot. Anthropic says Claude’s access is limited to each connected bank’s name, account name and type, the last four digits of the account number, balances, credit limits, and up to two years of transaction history.

Additionally, the company notes that when users chat with Claude in the Personal Finance hub, those conversations aren’t used to train its models.

Personal Finance starts rolling out in beta this week to users on the Pro, Max, and Ultra plans in the U.S.

The launch of the new tool comes as Anthropic has been expanding Claude into new verticals. For instance, Anthropic launched Claude for Legal and Claude for Financial Services earlier this year.

#453. The ex-Secret Service agent who flipped a hacker is now raising a second $250M cybersecurity fund

Buy One Founder Summit Ticket, Get a Second for Free. Register now.

Founder Summit begins Nov. 4, 9:00 a.m. ET. Get your pass savings & deals before it starts. REGISTER HERE.

Every time venture capitalists write a check, they’re secretly running numbers in their heads about different exit scenarios. It’s their job as fiduciaries. But nearly all will tell you (publicly, anyway) that every portfolio company is headed for an IPO.

Stephen Ward, the co-founder of Brightmind Partners, a two-year-old cybersecurity fund in New York, says otherwise outright. “You have to sell on the way up,” he says.

Ward’s unvarnished pragmatism is one reason he stands out. Another is the size of his firm’s debut fund late last year: a whopping $250 million fund, which he closed with co-founder Gur Talpaz. A third reason is his résumé, which includes a stint with the Coast Guard, nine years in the Secret Service, and CISO jobs at TIAA and Home Depot before he ever thought about investing.

One proof point that suggests the combination is working is SGNL, the identity security startup CrowdStrike agreed in January to buy for $740 million, 11 months after Brightmind led its Series A.

Now Ward is raising a second fund, targeting roughly the same size as the first one. This time, though, he’ll be doing it without Talpaz, who left in April along with another early colleague, leaving Ward as the firm’s sole general partner. He’s betting on himself at a moment when early-stage cybersecurity startups are raising more money than later-stage rivals for the first time in more than a decade.

His unemotional approach to exits traces back to an unlikely career. Ward says he grew up in northwest New Jersey between two farms, in a family that couldn’t afford college for his older brothers. After high school, he joined the Coast Guard, inspired by a commercial of a man jumping out of a helicopter, though he says he barely knew how to swim at the time. He spent four years in search and rescue.

Afterward, he studied criminal justice at Seton Hall in New Jersey and thought half-heartedly about law school. “I didn’t know what I wanted to do,” Ward told me last week over Zoom, wearing a pale hooded sweatshirt and looking more like a basketball coach than a venture capitalist. “I was kind of lost. I missed the military and the camaraderie and all of that.”

After graduating in 1998, he says, he mailed out some 300 résumés but no one responded. A Seton Hall professor who was also a chaplain to Secret Service personnel, as well as to members of the FBI and CIA, began introducing him around, and the Secret Service called first.

The Secret Service has two primary missions: protecting people and investigating financial crimes. Ward gravitated to investigations, then to “cyber” after the September 11 attacks in 2001. That year, he married a former college classmate, an American-born Syrian Muslim. Colleagues warned him the background check would be “miserable.” (They’re still happily married.)

What came next took Ward’s life down an unexpected path. Stationed in New York, he arrested a hacker who’d pulled off the largest credit card data theft of that era. Instead of charging him, however, Ward flipped him, turning him into an informant and running him for about three years. But the hacker kept hacking behind Ward’s back, and after he was caught again, he told the agency, as a diversionary tactic, that Ward was in on it.

By that time, Ward had left for the private sector, and he ultimately spent roughly 18 months clearing his name. “It was hard to get out of bed,” he said of that period, describing the investigation as “almost like a spouse cheating on you,” given his love of the agency. The hacker ultimately received a 20-year sentence, and Audible bought the rights to the story, turning it into an eight-episode podcast called “Operation Firewall.”

Ward’s corporate career has been a success by most measures, though he describes it as a series of jumps he really wasn’t qualified to make. His first private-sector job at J.P. Morgan, where he was recruited in 2007 to build a cybersecurity unit, was the hardest transition of his career, he says. He felt like “the dumbest person in the room,” missed his Secret Service crew, and twice thought about going back, unaware he was still under investigation. He stuck it out and now calls the investment bank the “best management school.”

Six years as CISO at the financial services company TIAA followed, and then two years at Home Depot starting in 2019. But in 2021, feeling burned out, he quit without anything lined up for the first time since age 16.

That chapter didn’t last long. The founders of Wiz, whose security software Ward had used at Home Depot, put him on a short list for a job at their company. Jeff Horing, a venture capitalist at Insight Partners, which had co-led Wiz’s $100 million Series A round in late 2020, interviewed Ward for the role, then offered him one at Insight instead.

It’s a good-news, bad-news story, given that Wiz was acquired last year by Google for $32 billion. When I asked him about it last week, Ward observed that the friend who took the Wiz job “made life-changing money,” but said it doesn’t bother him.

Regardless, he spent just three years at Insight, where he says he worked on Wiz, Abnormal Security, and Elisity, which IonQ agreed in October to buy for up to $1.3 billion. Then, at age 49, he thought about striking out on his own for the first time.

He says people told him it was a young man’s game. He left anyway and began cold-emailing limited partners. Ultimately, fewer than 20 LPs filled the fund, including Horing.

Ward started Brightmind in 2024 with Talpaz, a CrowdStrike corporate development executive whose deals overlapped with Ward’s during his time at Insight. Tayler Sipperly, another CrowdStrike corporate development alum, was the firm’s third employee.

The partnership ended this past spring. Asked what happened, Ward, using carefully worded legalese, cited “differences in opinions regarding operations and the future of Brightmind.” In August, Talpaz and Sipperly co-founded a new venture firm with Elia Zaitsev, CrowdStrike’s global CTO for the past three years. Called Cognition, it’s now reportedly targeting a $170 million first fund to back cybersecurity startups.

Brightmind’s first fund has made 14 investments to date, with one exit: that SNGL sale.

Some investors asked why Brightmind didn’t ride out that bet even longer. But Ward says he’s seen enough across his career that he feels confident in warning founders when they’re “going to race past the turn.”

In cybersecurity, he says, the best sales come between a startup’s third and seventh years. Indeed, he says his team tracks every security acquisition and what buyers are building versus buying. If Palo Alto Networks is likely to buy something, he says, CrowdStrike will experience “FOMO, then Zscaler is going to look over and go, ‘What did they just do? I should probably do the same thing.’”

Understanding how the pieces fit together is the trick, Ward explained, offering some hypotheticals. If CrowdStrike is building a product, he said, it’s unlikely to buy a portfolio company that makes something similar. Palo Alto Networks might love a company, but not if buying it would force its customers to switch away from something it bought five years ago.

“It’s very easy to go, ‘Good match,’ ‘terrible match,’” he said. “And that’s really what we spend a ton of time figuring out.”

He has timing on his side, at least. Early-stage cybersecurity startups are outraising their later-stage rivals for the first time in more than a decade, according to PitchBook, which counts $4.7 billion worth of seed-through-Series B deals through September.

That total is already ahead of the entirety of 2025, even though the number of deals fell to 248 from 358 last year. Seed rounds in particular are ballooning, per PitchBook. The average cybersecurity seed round jumped to $8.4 million in the first nine months of this year, from $5.54 million a year earlier.

Ward questions how much of that money is really going to AI security. By PitchBook’s count, 278 deals in the first half of 2026 were bucketed as such, but his team’s manual review found only 30 true ones. #369. Cruise companies aim to keep fuel costs down. Their strategies for doing so differ

Carnival, Royal Caribbean and Norwegian Cruise Line Holdings posted strong earnings for their most recent quarters despite rising fuel prices. How the leading cruise companies navigate fuel costs boils down to starkly different strategies.

Oil prices have skyrocketed since the U.S.-Israel war with Iran began on Feb. 28. Brent crude oil reached a six-month high on Friday, settling at $116.72 per barrel. With no clear end in sight for the conflict in the Middle East, the pressure affecting cruise ships' lifeblood seems poised to last into the holiday travel season and beyond.

Both Royal Caribbean and Norwegian practice "hedging," or a strategy of using derivatives to reduce exposure to swings in the price of fuel, to account for sharp increases.

Carnival CEO Josh Weinstein, on the other hand, told CNBC in an interview last month that cutting fuel consumption is the best way to avert the effects of expensive fuel.

"At the end of the day, fuel is an input cost for us. The best thing that we can do to control that cost is to use less," Weinstein said at the time.

Jefferies analyst David Katz told CNBC in an interview that companies that hedge do so primarily to manage the cost of fuel, but also to manage Wall Street expectations and prevent hectic stock trading based on fluctuations in the price of fuel.

"When you're public, the Street will trade your stock around based on the price of fuel," Katz said. "The Street's argument is that volatility puts pressure on your multiple."

"While it is mathematically most likely true that over the long term it is more expensive," he explained. "The benefit of having less fuel volatility in the model and less trading volatility is [it's] good for your multiple."

Despite the potential positives of the strategy, Carnival has maintained the stance it has held since 2019: Cutting fuel consumption works better than hedging.

And Carnival's third-quarter earnings report last month suggests the strategy may be paying off.

Carnival beat analysts' expectations on both the top and bottom lines for its most recent quarter and reported an all-time high net income of $1.9 billion.

Weinstein told CNBC in an interview on "Squawk on the Street" following the release that the company has been "maniacal" about cutting its fuel consumption, saying it's slashed its unit fuel consumption rate by 26% since 2019.

"We estimate we've saved about $700 million in 2026 based on that consumption savings rate going down since 2019," he said.

Carnival spent $615 million, or about 7.3% of its revenue, on fuel in the third quarter, according to its most recent earnings release. That's an increase from the $425 million, or 6.7% of revenue, that the company spent on fuel in the fourth quarter of 2025, before fuel prices spiked.

Carnival projects its fuel costs will rise to $640 million in the fourth quarter, according to its earnings release.

Royal Caribbean similarly beat analyst expectations for earnings per share for its third quarter, reported Tuesday. However, it narrowly missed on revenue.

CEO Jason Liberty attributed the company's beat in part to its hedging strategy during an interview on CNBC's "Squawk on the Street" Tuesday morning.

"We're a little bit over 60% hedged here for 2026, and so that's helping insulate us from some of the impacts on the rising fuel costs," he said.

"It's obviously still impacting us," Liberty said. "[High fuel prices] evaporated a good chunk of our Q3 earnings beat into the full-year guide. But we're continuing to hedge, and we watch."

Royal Caribbean spent about $347 million on fuel in the third quarter, or about 6.2% of its revenue, according to its earnings release. That compares with $293 million, or 6.8% of revenue, spent on fuel in the fourth quarter of 2025, which ended on Dec. 31.

The company projects fuel costs will continue to rise to $379 million in the fourth quarter.

Liberty pointed out that high fuel prices have not dampened demand from consumers for vacations.

"It's not that we don't see points of challenges, but really what we've seen is that even in those points of challenges, the consumer comes roaring back," he said.

Royal Caribbean reported strong bookings for the third quarter and said reservations for 2027 are already up by a high-teens percentage.

Norwegian Cruise Line Holdings beat on both the top and bottom lines for its third-quarter earnings, reported on Thursday, with revenue of $2.9 billion and adjusted earnings per share of $1.12.

The company has hedged approximately 48% of its fuel consumption for 2026, according to the earnings release.

Norwegian spent about $196 million on fuel in the third quarter, or about 6.6% of its revenue, according to its most recent earnings release. That's a lower share of revenue than the 7.4% — totaling $167 million — spent on fuel in the fourth quarter of 2025, which ended on Dec. 31.

The company projects fuel consumption to rise slightly in the fourth quarter, up to 255,000 metric tons from 239,000 metric tons consumed in the third quarter.

"These results reinforce our confidence in the appeal of our products and the opportunity to translate stronger revenue performance and disciplined cost management into improved profitability," Kempa said.

#315. Cheaper cars are selling considerably faster than at the beginning of the year

Consumers are concerned about rising fuel prices and affordability — and that could be showing up in how fast the least expensive cars are moving off dealer lots.

From the first quarter to the third, the number of days it took a dealer to sell a vehicle priced between $20,000 and $29,999 dropped 20%, according to data from Edmunds. That pace of selling saw the biggest acceleration of any price segment.

Vehicles priced from $30,000 to $39,999 sold about 18% faster, and the number of days it took to sell the next tier — $40,000 to $49,999 — dropped nearly 12%, according to Edmunds.

All of those price segments also took fewer days to turn than their more expensive counterparts. The lower-priced tiers took under 60 days to move off lots, with the least expensive ones selling the fastest. Every segment of vehicles above $50,000, meanwhile, took more than 60 days to sell.

"If you're a dealer and you have inventory and that price point, you're definitely noticing," said Edmunds Director of Insights Ivan Drury.

Cheaper vehicles may be selling faster, but they're still hard to find. The average listing price for a new vehicle in September was $49,726, according to Cox Automotive.

And, the overall sales numbers for some key segments priced under $40,000 have fallen. This has especially been true for compact cars, which are down roughly 4% since the beginning of 2026, and compact SUVs, down nearly 8% in the same period, according to Cox Automotive.

These different data points seem to be moving in opposite directions. On the one hand, cheaper cars are moving faster off dealer lots. On the other, sales of affordable segments are down.

There are a few reasons for this, according to analysts who follow the industry. One is that there might not be enough lower-priced vehicles on dealer lots in the first place.

"I think there are some supply things going on at that end of the market," said Cox Automotive Deputy Chief Economist Mark Strand. "We have to kind of grade the market on a curve until we see what's going on with fourth-quarter data."

The compact SUV segment, in particular, is suffering from the lack of two popular models.

The Toyota RAV4, which is the most popular SUV in the U.S., has been in short supply at many dealerships this year, as the automaker shifts production to its Kentucky factory, Strand said. The company also has shifted the entire lineup to a hybrid powertrain at a time when hybrids have been in exceptionally high demand.

The other missing SUV is the Ford Escape, which that automaker discontinued to make way for its upcoming midsize EV pickup truck.

The other slow-selling, low-priced segment — compact cars — faces structural inventory challenges, said AutoForecast Solutions Vice President of Global Forecasting Sam Fiorani.

"We've seen the disappearance of affordable, compact new vehicles over the last decade," he said.

Detroit automakers swapped out their lineups of compact coupes and sedans in favor of small crossover SUVs, which tend to carry slightly higher sticker prices than comparably sized passenger cars.

Currently, there is not a single compact passenger sedan for sale by an American manufacturer. Most on the market are Japanese or Korean, with a few German models, such as the Volkswagen Jetta and the Golf hatchback.

"You have all these brands that have just abandoned the marketplace," Fiorani said.

The smallest American passenger cars are the Ford Mustang, General Motors' Chevrolet Corvette, and the midsize Tesla Model 3 sedan, according to AutoForecast Solutions. The firm classifies both the Mustang and the Corvette as sports cars, and as such they occupy a different niche than compact sedans like the Honda Civic or Toyota Corolla.

The Mustang has a starting price of $33,490, the Model 3 starts at $34,990 and the Chevrolet Corvette starts at $71,300. Cars like the Civic, Corolla and Jetta, meanwhile, all start below $25,000.

"Compact vehicle buyers are very price-sensitive," Fiorani said. "And so when the price of gas goes up, that eats into their car payment. That means that they can't afford a $500 or $600 a month car payment, and they have to move into the used market."

Indeed, the wholesale used prices of compact cars have risen and are up more than almost any other segment, according to Cox Automotive. Wholesale prices — the prices vehicles sell for at large auctions, like Cox Automotive's Manheim auction — are indicators for prices overall in the fragmented used-car market.

Along with the rise in prices, auto finance stats show that a historically high number of buyers are stretching to afford cars.

Roughly one in four buyers financing their purchases are getting 84-month loans — a record. Nearly 3 in 10 trade-ins toward new-vehicle purchases carried negative equity in the third quarter, meaning the cars being traded in were worth less than the outstanding balances of the loans on them. Shoppers are often rolling these negative balances into new loans, which pushes up the new payment cost.

Automakers have also been more tightly aligning production with demand to avoid discounting vehicles. Incentives on average are about 6.6% of the average transaction price as of October, according to Cox Automotive. Leasing offers a way to get a car with a lower payment, but leasing rates are also down.

Vehicle trade-in data suggests that many buyers who are snapping up those fast-selling cheaper vehicles are either first-time buyers or are purchasing supplemental vehicles, Edmunds' Drury said.

The subcompact SUV segment is one lower-priced group that has seen sales rise in 2026. That is a segment that attracts a lot of first-time buyers. But this is still a relatively small share of the total car market, Fiorani said.

"It's not the mainstream vehicle that Americans want," he said. "It is the vehicle that entry-level buyers need to get to work, to get to school. ... It's people who don't have families yet."

Repeat buyers are less likely to want to step down into a compact car or SUV, Drury said.

"The moment you get into that other vehicle and it's a little bit smaller, it's not as comfortable, it doesn't fit all your stuff, all your kid's equipment for soccer practice, you're done," he said. "You're not giving up 360 cameras, ventilated seats, heated steering wheels, these niceties that you've become accustomed to. Automakers know that too. Dealers know that."

The truly cash-strapped are either snapping up the relatively few low-priced cars on dealer lots, moving to the used market, or holding on to their cars longer.

"The people who are trying to buy the smaller vehicles are really hurt by the way the economy is at the moment," Fiorani said. "So those people are trying to move into used vehicles or trying to maintain their current vehicle as long as they can. Automakers should be prepared for a softening in the marketplace."

#297. Automakers like Rivian and Ford are ‘democratizing 3D printing’ to reduce costs and increase speed

DETROIT — Automakers including Ford Motor and Rivian Automotive are leaning into a new type of manufacturing line: 3D printing.

Manufacturers say it's helping them reduce costs and increase speed. The technology has been around for decades, but it's becoming more commoditized in the automotive industry, with 3D printers used to create everything from vehicle parts and accessories to tools for assembly plants and testing units for research and development labs.

"At this point, we don't have a choice but to scale up and begin using it through all factions of Rivian," Jonathan Dankenbring, Rivian senior manager of prototype manufacturing, told CNBC during a tour of the automaker's expansive 3D-printing operations in suburban Detroit. "We're going to be democratizing 3D printing for the entire company."

Also known as additive manufacturing, 3D printing can create three-dimensional objects from digital designs by dispensing material layer by layer, much like a traditional printer does with ink on paper. Instead of ink, the machines use a wide variety of materials such as plastics, composites, ceramics and even metals.

Printers can range from hundreds of dollars for hobby-like devices to hundreds of thousands of dollars for specialty equipment, depending on how big the product being produced is and the material it needs. They can produce individual pieces or hundreds of smaller parts in a batch using powders and resins.

Rivian said it has spent more than $5 million on 3D-printing machines, with the cost of materials for the operations growing to about $1.7 million annually.

Dankenbring said despite the costs, the machines typically pay for themselves within a year. He said the technology helps lower supplier tooling costs, streamline logistics expenses and cut production times for parts from weeks to days, if not hours.

"It's well in the millions [for savings], but the main thing is speed for Rivian," Dankenbring said.

The all-electric vehicle company's latest expansion of 3D printing includes adding new machines that can build flexible silicone parts as well as installing a bay of machines at its plant in Normal, Illinois, for employees to print parts on demand.

"3D prints should be readily available for every engineer so that they can make a part and get it in their hands as soon as possible," Dankenbring said.

Rivian has also used the technology for electric-motor models for fluid testing, creating screw guides for tooling and stamping press inserts and some production parts, including trim pieces to fill gaps in some early builds.

There's a similar push underway at larger, more-established automakers like Ford and its crosstown rival General Motors, which have used 3D printing for many years, including in their massive assembly plants.

Instead of having a bay of 3D-printing machines like at Rivian, Ford has departments for additive manufacturing at many of its plants to produce and distribute needed parts. That includes a new vending machine for 3D-printed parts for production of F-150 pickup trucks near its Dearborn, Michigan, headquarters.

"By putting the vending machine up, all the team leaders have access," said Gregory Foresi, a Ford mechanical engineer who ran Ford's 3D-printing lab at the plant from 2024 until last month. "We're able to keep that thing stocked rather than have a bunch of parts laying around in the lab. So that's been pretty huge."

Foresi used roughly two dozen machines to print off thousands of tools, some of which have been designed by employees, such as torque bit sockets, hood jackers and hand tools for smoothing adhesive.

Employees can use an app to request that specific auto parts or tools be printed. Ford's 3D-printing teams help develop them or determine what to produce next, as the machining time to build different parts can range from hours to days, if not weeks.

Foresi said creating the tools with 3D printing has allowed the automaker to uniquely design them when needed, get them faster and, in many cases, receive them at a lower cost.

Ford also has used 3D-printed parts to help keep production running. For example, Foresi said a large machining wheel was needed to maintain assembly line production and the company was able to 3D print the part until a permanent replacement from a supplier was available.

"They didn't perform as well, and it's not really a replacement for the ones that we were purchasing, but they kept the plant running," Foresi said.

Ford said it expects to have 3D labs deployed at more than 23 plants by the end of this year. Its current 3D-printing program has more than 200 machines, and there are additional printers outside the official program, the company said.

GM has used 3D-printed parts for some of its low-volume products in addition to research and development and inside plants, including recently expanding its use of industrial Stratasys printers that can cost hundreds of thousands of dollars based on use case, training and certification.

The Detroit automaker uses 135 components that are 3D-printed for its bespoke Cadillac Celestiq as well as other components for its performance Cadillac Blackwing sedans, including a unique medallion on the manual shifter knob.

Fadi Abro, senior global director of automotive and mobility at 3D-printing company Stratasys, said GM has been a great example of how companies can "standardize and then scale" 3D printing.

"The material properties improved so significantly that the use cases improved," he said, noting the company works with virtually every major automaker. "From a product perspective, we have to get the technology to become faster and more accessible, in an economic sense."
