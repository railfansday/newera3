#481. Jensen Huang’s exit puts Nvidia’s support for open source in question

The open source community just lost one of its strongest advocates.

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang resigned Thursday after he admitted to an affair with an alleged Chinese spy, raising concerns about U.S. national security and the motives behind the executive’s support for softer chip export controls on China.

Huang has been a staunch supporter of open source AI, framing the ecosystem as good for safety, innovation, diffusion, and sovereignty. And as frontier AI labs increasingly move to reduce their reliance on Nvidia and build their own chips, supporting open source is also a good way for the company to diversify its customer base by selling chips to nation-states, enterprises, and any organization that wants to own its own models.

Still, Huang’s resignation raises questions about whether Nvidia will carry on his legacy by continuing to support the open model community.

Jensen no longer being CEO would be very very bad for the open model cause in the US.

Huang’s departure also comes as Nvidia has yet to finalize a deal to acquire open source platform Hugging Face for $13 billion. Hugging Face CEO Clem Delangue said in a post on X that the company would continue to fight for open source, whatever happens next.

Nvidia also agreed to pay $6 billion for a non-exclusive license for open AI coding startup Poolside’s technology.

Within Nvidia, Huang pushed for the development of the Nemotron family of open-weight AI models built for long-running agentic AI systems and reasoning workloads — potential competitors to closed frontier models. One company insider told TechCrunch that they don’t expect a change in leadership to have an impact on Nemotron development.

Huang also backed companies that would make American open models more competitive, investing in startups like Reflection AI and Thinking Machines Lab.

Huang’s influence extended beyond Nvidia’s investments and product roadmap. He also emerged as one of the most prominent voices pushing back against Washington’s efforts to restrict the open AI ecosystem.

Earlier this year, Huang wrote an open letter signed by 25 companies, including Microsoft, Meta, IBM, and Palantir, urging the U.S. government not to over-restrict open-weight AI models, many of which come from China.

The impetus for that letter was Washington’s debate over how the U.S. should respond to allegations that Chinese AI labs are stealing intellectual property from American counterparts like Anthropic and OpenAI, both of which have reportedly appealed directly to Washington on the matter, urging stronger protections.

That combination of Huang’s personal visibility and Nvidia’s market power made him the strongest counterweight to arguments from closed-model providers like Anthropic and OpenAI, which have advocated for greater restrictions on advanced AI models due to safety reasons.

Without Huang, the concern among open source community members is that whoever takes over will be more cautious. Nvidia has named CFO Colette Kress as interim CEO. It’s not clear how Kress will approach the Hugging Face deal or support for the open model ecosystem, but founder-driven priorities tend to get a downgrade when leadership changes.

Huang’s exit could also strengthen the arguments of those in Washington who are arguing for tougher restrictions on Chinese AI companies, particularly as the scandal surrounding his relationship with Alice Huang, the Megaspeed executive and alleged spy, puts renewed scrutiny on his efforts to loosen chip export controls.

Restrictions on open-weight models from China could kneecap competition for American companies, the most powerful of which have kept their models closed.

Still, supporting open source isn’t simply a passion project Huang pursued out of idealism. It’s increasingly central to Nvidia’s business strategy.

As some of Nvidia’s biggest customers, including Google and OpenAI, develop their own chips, Nvidia has an incentive to cultivate a much broader ecosystem of AI developers and customers.

More players training, fine-tuning, and running models mean more GPUs sold, and less dependence on a handful of frontier labs.

Huang may be gone, but Nvidia still needs an open AI ecosystem. The question now is whether that commitment can survive without the man who made it a priority.

#476. Nectar launches an AI agent to automate creator discovery and outreach for brands

Nectar, an AI-powered operating system for marketing, is launching a new AI agent designed to take a lot of the manual work out of influencer marketing, from finding the right creators to contacting them, managing partnerships, and tracking results.

Founded by sisters Misbah and Farah Uraizee, both former Meta employees, Nectar came out of stealth last year and uses AI agents to help brands manage social media, customer conversations, content moderation, creator partnerships, and competitor research.

Announced Thursday, its new “Creator Agent” can search for creators, check their content and brand partnerships, send personalized messages, coordinate collaborations, and monitor how campaigns perform. Instead of having marketing teams spend weeks scrolling through social media, building spreadsheets, and sending individual DMs, Nectar wants its agent to handle much of that work automatically.

Nectar joins other companies introducing agentic AI assistants designed to automate influencer outreach, including Aspire and GRIN. The launch also comes as more brands turn to creators to reach customers. U.S. spending on creator advertising is expected to reach $44 billion in 2026, according to the Interactive Advertising Bureau. As companies work with hundreds or even thousands of creators, keeping track of relationships and measuring results can get pretty difficult.

Nectar says its Creator Agent searches more than 300 million creator profiles to find potential partners, including smaller creators who may not yet have a large following but are gaining traction. The system analyzes videos to understand what creators post about, how their content fits a brand, and whether their previous partnerships are appropriate. It also checks brand safety by reviewing content in context rather than simply searching for specific keywords.

Nectar then creates ranked lists of recommendations and adds new matches as creators become more popular.

Once it finds a potential match, the agent can draft and send messages in a brand’s voice, create custom landing pages to collect creator information, and help manage contracts, product gifting, and shipping details. It also remembers previous conversations and interactions, so brands can keep track of relationships without relying on spreadsheets.

“Brands can share voice guidelines in any format, and Nectar picks up the rest from their history,” CEO Misbah Uraizee tells TechCrunch. “On top of that sits a set of proprietary guardrails that keep every draft on tone and in context. Nectar understands what is being said in a video, reads the tone of the conversation, and knows the difference between a quote post on X and a TikTok comment section. A brand should not sound identical in both, and with Nectar it doesn’t.”

Another feature looks for personal milestones in creators’ lives, such as an engagement, a new baby, or completing a marathon. The agent can then suggest a personalized message or gift, helping brands build relationships that feel less like one-off advertising deals.

Creator Agent is available today globally and covers Instagram creators and TikTokers.

The company plans to make the agent available through its Model Context Protocol (MCP), allowing teams to use it through AI assistants such as Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, and Copilot, as well as their own custom-built agents.

This past May, Nectar raised a $30 million Series A round led by Menlo Ventures and its Anthology Fund, which is a joint initiative launched with Anthropic.

#472. Google’s latest Search upgrade promises better answers to your most complicated questions

Google Search is getting better at understanding complicated questions, including those with multiple parts, highly specific requests, or confusing typos. The updates are coming via the company’s newly released Gemini 4 Hydrogen model, which Google claims is its “biggest model improvement to Search yet.”

While Google’s frontier models, Gemini 4 Carbon and Gemini 4 Argon, are best at deep reasoning for hard problems, Hydrogen is designed for fast execution across a variety of agentic workflows, or tasks in which an AI carries out multiple steps on its own, the company explained in an announcement.

The idea behind the model’s release is that using the most powerful model isn’t always needed — sometimes a faster, cheaper, and reasonably smart one will do. Google says Hydrogen completes tasks 2.5 times faster than Gemini 3.8 Flash, its previous fast model, but it’s still pretty smart, even beating the more powerful Gemini 4 Argon on one broad intelligence benchmark, the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, at its default high thinking level.

In Search, Hydrogen brings several improvements, including a better understanding of user intent, the ability to offer more helpful links and visual responses, and better answers across the different languages supported by Google’s AI Mode.

In terms of intent, Google says Hydrogen can handle more nuanced and complicated questions more quickly. This means people can ask their questions in their natural train of thought, including those that have multiple parts or that include highly specific requests. Hydrogen is also better at navigating hard-to-understand typos, Google says. Because of its improvements and better ability to follow instructions and call the right tools, Hydrogen is able to more precisely address each part of a user’s question.

Its advanced reasoning also improves Search’s fan-out technique, which breaks down a single user’s prompt into multiple subqueries. This allows people to discover websites related to the query that they may have otherwise missed, the blog post explains.

The addition of Hydrogen to Search will also mean more images, structured responses, comparison tables, and custom tools will appear as visual responses to users’ queries in AI Mode.

Google also says Hydrogen can work on its own for everyday work in areas like writing code, running tests, or verifying code changes, or it can be paired with Carbon and Argon in workflows that combine several models.

The model offers four thinking levels: low, medium, high (default), and a new, extra-high tier (“xhigh”), all of which are designed to complete tasks faster with fewer output tokens.

On multi-step tasks like deep research, data analysis, and broad codebase navigation, Hydrogen ranks No. 5 on Agent Arena’s benchmarks, the company pointed out.

Google says the Hydrogen update is rolling out to all Search users globally.

#463. Nebius tests the neocloud playbook with $40 billion in planned spending

In its third-quarter earnings call Thursday, the neocloud company Nebius laid out $40 billion in planned spending for 2027, far more than Wall Street had expected. It’s an ambitious plan, but it rests on a string of planned projects that are already starting to show cracks.

Nebius plans to fund the buildout by relying on customer pre-payments and loans backed by customer contracts — essentially using the future value of its data centers to finance their construction. But that can be a risky strategy if those projects get bogged down by delays or cancellations.

“Building at this scale is hard, and delays are part of our industry right now,” CEO Arkady Volozh said during Thursday’s earnings call. “We are not immune.”

The earnings announcement comes on the heels of reports that Microsoft is cooling on neocloud deals that aren’t delivering on time. That’s a problem for companies like Nebius, whose financing strategy is becoming more dependent on repayments. Because if it can’t get to capacity on time, it will keep piling on costs while revenues slip.

One of Nebius’ largest customers is Microsoft, which is paying for AI computing capacity at a Vineland, New Jersey, data center that is now months behind schedule. Nebius’ contract with Microsoft is worth up to $19.4 billion, but it’s already letting deadlines slip. Only just over half of the Microsoft capacity is now planned for delivery by the end of the year, with the rest coming in Q2 2027.

The delay is because DataOne, the third-party operator of the site, “decided to wind down the temporary generation faster than it was expected,” according to Andrey Korolenko, chief product and infrastructure officer, as the site switches over to Bloom Energy fuel cells.

Still, that transition wasn’t entirely by choice. In August, the investigative site Floodlight identified at least 45 natural gas generators in operation at the DataOne site, even though it had not received any permits for operating gas generators. New Jersey regulators fined the DataOne facility $1.1 million in response, finding that the use of the generators violated federal law.

DataOne is also facing a noise-complaint lawsuit from nearby residents over a constant humming noise allegedly emitted by the data center.

According to Nebius’ summary of its Microsoft contract, Microsoft can terminate affected service after a grace period if dates are missed and no alternative capacity is offered. The tranches were originally meant to be deployed in 2025 and 2026, so a Q2 2027 delivery date falls well beyond that window. The contract also includes financial penalties for late delivery.

Nebius said that Microsoft “has been part of the planning process.” Nebius did not say whether Microsoft has agreed to the new dates, but Microsoft has not publicly canceled anything.

TechCrunch has reached out to Nebius, Microsoft, and DataOne for comment.

On the call, Marc Boroditsky, Nebius’s chief revenue officer, said the company’s deals with Microsoft and Meta both came with significant prepayment, which arrived long before delivery. But Bloomberg reported last week that Microsoft is tightening oversight on projects run by third parties, including Nebius, IREN, and Nscale, and debating whether it’s worth signing new agreements. Microsoft also sent employees to the site in New Jersey, per Bloomberg.

In its own quarterly earnings report, Microsoft noted that its capex spend was lower than expected due to third-party delays. The company is clearly not above pulling back from planned agreements where its partner doesn’t meet its share of the bargain. Bloomberg also reported that Microsoft won’t use an option to more than double its Nscale agreement for a Texas data center and recently stopped being an anchor customer for Nscale’s planned Essex site after power delays.

Nebius says the delays it faces are coming from sites it doesn’t control, with the data centers it is building itself coming online on time or ahead of schedule. Those, Nebius says, will supply most of its new capacity from 2027. New deals signed in Q3 came with prepayments that more than cover the cost of building capacity behind them, says Boroditsky. Nebius ended the quarter with $7.4 billion in cash.

Total prepayments plus asset-backed financing of investment-grade contracts are expected to cover up to 85% of the $40 to $45 billion 2027 capex spend. CFO Maria Del Dado Alonso Sanchez named Microsoft and Meta as contracts that can be financed this way. Boroditsky also said Nebius could “tap prepayments to shore up any additional cash requirements.”

Nebius forecast 2027 revenue of $12 to $13 billion, and said it expects to break even on a pre-tax basis by the fourth quarter.

Nebius reported third-quarter revenue of $981 million, up 571% from a year earlier. Roughly a third of that was a one-time payment tied to a new partnership model. Nebius cut its 2026 revenue forecast by $200 million to $3 billion, citing delays to third-party sites like New Jersey. Shares ended the day down 17%.

#453. Democrats launch Nvidia investigation, write scathing letter on Huang’s ‘scheme’ with Trump

Democrats on Thursday opened an inquiry into how the Trump administration handled Nvidia’s chip sales to China. Earlier on Thursday, the company ousted its CEO Jensen Huang. He also agreed to testify to a different set of Republican-led Congressional committees.

Huang was removed from the company he founded after he confessed to an extramarital affair with a woman accused of being a Chinese spy. (He has since been accused of having more affairs, with Nvidia employees.)

The alleged Chinese spy, Alice Huang, was an executive of Nvidia customer Megaspeed, a Singapore-based company that U.S. officials suspect of helping route advanced Nvidia chips into China. The Democratic inquiry comes from Rep. Richard Neal (D-Mass.), the top Democrat on the House Ways and Means Committee, and Sen. Ron Wyden (D-Ore.), the top Democrat on the Senate Finance Committee. As the minority party, they can ask questions but can’t compel answers.

Their humdinger of a letter to Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick asks the department to preserve records about an arrangement they find questionable and, they allege, illegal.

“The scheme worked like this,” as the letter describes it. President Trump agreed to sales of Nvidia’s H200 chips to China as long as the chips, manufactured by Taiwan, were first shipped to the United States. On arriving, Trump added a tariff “effectively taking a cut of Nvidia’s sales to China through what appears to be an unconstitutional export tax system,” the letter said. Then the Commerce Department, under Lutnick, issued an export license.

By the way, the deal between Trump and Nvidia to share revenue was public. In August 2025, Nvidia and AMD both agreed to share 15% of their revenue from chip sales to China with the U.S. government as part of a deal to secure export licenses, the AP reported at the time.

But the letter questions why Lutnick continued to allow Megaspeed to buy chips even after he was reportedly informed by U.S. intelligence of their suspicions of Alice Huang and of Jensen Huang’s relationship with her. It questions why the department reportedly green-lit other Asian companies to buy Nvidia chips “with no physical presence at their listed addresses.” So the inquiry is aimed at the administration, not just at Nvidia and Huang.

The letter also questions how Nvidia’s donations to Trump’s projects factored in. It notes that the export deal was approved after Trump learned of “Mr. Huang’s compromised position” in sleeping with an alleged spy, and after Nvidia donated $26 million toward Trump’s projects, including the ballroom. It appears to be asking if Trump used his knowledge of the affair to secure donations from Nvidia.

Interestingly, in a “60 Minutes” interview with President Trump that aired on Sunday, Trump mentioned how he and his team had threatened an unnamed AI exec with jail, and after that, the exec “totally changed.”

This Congressional investigation into Nvidia’s China sales and its dealings with Trump is the second one, so far, since news of the alleged spy affair broke. The Republican-led group involves leaders from the House Select Committee on the Chinese Communist Party, the House Committee on Foreign Affairs, and the House Intelligence Committee. That group has demanded that Huang come to them and testify in a closed-door session. They had threatened to subpoena him if he didn’t voluntarily agree. On Thursday, after his resignation, he agreed.

#444. Israel’s VCs see opportunity for challengers in Nvidia’s turmoil

Former Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang’s resignation has been received with worry in Israel, the company’s “second home,” as Huang described it in 2023. The company’s importance flows both ways, as the semiconductor giant has provided a major boost to Israel’s economic forecasts in recent years.

Intel, Microsoft, and many homegrown startups also conduct a sizable share of their R&D in Israel. But owing to Nvidia’s stronghold on AI given the centrality of its GPUs for training LLMs and using them, many in the country are wondering how much of a reshuffle will result from the company’s leadership crisis.

As with everything at Nvidia, Huang drove the company to acquire Israeli chipmaker Mellanox for $6.9 billion in 2019 and use the company as the base for its largest R&D facility outside of the U.S. Nvidia also made plans to build a multibillion-dollar campus in northern Israel.

“Jensen brought Nvidia to Israel with the acquisition of Mellanox, so there is a strong positive sentiment towards Nvidia and Jensen in Israel’s tech community,” Israeli repeat cybersecurity entrepreneur Pavel Gurvich told TechCrunch.

Beyond the 6,000 locals Nvidia employs, the bigger question is whether the company will remain as committed to Israel with someone else at the helm. As a result, his line of succession is being observed just as closely by those who hope the company continues to do so as by those who don’t want it to happen.

The latter have already gathered that they may be in for disappointment. In a 2023 interview that is now doing the rounds on X, interim CEO Colette Kress had declared that as Nvidia’s CFO, she typically started her day “with Israel meetings, and only then continue[d] to California.”

Kress may not remain in charge in the long run, but the many names floated for the role include two Israelis, Apple’s Johny Srouji and Nvidia’s Michael Kagan — although they’re not at the top of the still quite tentative list.

Regardless of who gets chosen, Huang’s successor will likely have less of a grip on Nvidia and the tech industry as a whole. But investors don’t think that would cause demand for Nvidia’s product to fade away.

According to Yanai Oron, a general partner at Vertex Ventures Israel, “slower decisions at Nvidia could give competitors an opening, but if that uncertainty also slows AI spending, they could end up with a bigger share of a smaller opportunity.”

Nvidia has been responsible for a vast share of the spending in the global AI industry. Under Huang’s leadership, the company used its cash reserves to massively invest across the spectrum, both in companies and in its own next-generation chips.

Such an empire may not be doomed to fall with its architect’s ouster, but it does seem less likely to become a full-on monopoly. According to Adi Levanon, a founder and managing partner at Selah Ventures, “[Huang’s] departure doesn’t dismantle Nvidia’s technology or ecosystem, but it could give competitors more room to shape what comes next.”

And now that companies globally are deploying increasingly demanding AI agents into their workflows, there’s been a lot of interest — and some venture capital — flowing into developers of AI-specific chips and inference software that could compete with Nvidia’s.

“These challengers now have a window to prove a different approach delivers better economics; a leadership change alone won’t win them the business,” Levanon said.

#441. Microsoft’s latest email outage turned into a full-blown global cloud crisis

Microsoft is slowly recovering from its daylong outage after much of the company’s systems were hit by a cascading software issue.

The problems, which started Thursday, were initially flagged as affecting the company’s services in the U.S. and the Americas, but quickly spread around the world, as engineers struggled to recover downed servers.

What began as an Outlook email and Teams video conferencing outage soon developed into a full-blown meltdown affecting the company’s cloud services for both consumer and business email, files, and documents. The outage also knocked out the company’s Copilot AI tools and Defender security screening software, and prevented system administrators from accessing their internal Intune dashboards and features. Some users reportedly experienced issues with accessing their cloud-based Windows computers.

Microsoft hasn’t yet said what caused the issues, only saying the outage involved “a large number of servers being unexpectedly unavailable.” The company said it continues to investigate the cause.

The outage affected a broad range of industries, including TechCrunch’s own email. We’ve reached out to Microsoft for comment about the incident.

As of Friday morning on the U.S. East Coast, Microsoft’s Outlook email service had begun to recover and some emails have been trickling in. The recovery comes after engineers initially could not find the cause of the issue that was causing servers in Microsoft’s data centers to fail.

Doug Madory, the director of internet analysis at Kentik, a company that monitors internet traffic worldwide, said in a post on Bluesky that traffic was returning to Microsoft’s services after about 18 hours of downtime.

Microsoft said in its latest status update on Friday that there was “no evidence of malicious activity associated with this incident,” and later noted that there was “not related to cyber or AI / agents.” That may be some solace for the millions who went without their email and online services yesterday, but the company is likely to face questions about its inability to swiftly recover from an outage of its own making.

A Microsoft spokesperson did not respond to our questions asking if the company has the technical means to detect malicious activity, such as logs, or if Microsoft has a way to check what happened.

“We’re seeing encouraging recovery progress, with service availability and email delivery performance improving across a growing portion of affected infrastructure,” said Charles Lamanna, Microsoft’s executive vice president for Copilot, agents, and platform, in a statement on Friday. “Our teams are reviewing what happened and how we can prevent similar issues going forward.”

The last major incident affecting Microsoft services was in September, when a dayslong outage hit Microsoft’s cloud services. During that outage, Microsoft blamed a “core authentication configuration” issue that prevented users from accessing the service.

#408. This young startup wants to power floating data centers with the ocean’s heat, and investors are already on board

Anyone who has weathered a hurricane can tell you that the oceans — which fuel the storms — contain a tremendous amount of energy. Thanks to global warming, they’re holding more than ever.

A new startup wants to harness that energy to power data centers.

Drogue, which has kept a low profile until now, is building a system that harvests heat from the ocean to power data centers on board floating vessels. According to founder and CEO Sam Sipe, the first prototype should be bobbing around the ocean, crunching bits, less than two years from now.

The goal, Sipe said, is to help data center developers deploy servers in AI-friendly timelines. “The data center industry is a real estate game, and so they’re playing on 8- to 10-year timelines, including interoperability with utilities and [independent system operators]” that manage regional power grids, he said. “AI demand has increased 10x in just the last three years. It’s completely incompatible.”

Drogue was founded just six months ago in Washington, D.C., and the startup told TechCrunch exclusively that it has already raised $20 million, including from XYZ Venture Capital, Toyota Ventures, Tamarack Global, Dream, FDVC, Garuda Ventures, Flexcap, Boost VC, and Slow Ventures, among other investors.

In those six months, Drogue has designed a multi-megawatt prototype that will be installed on a traditional oceangoing ship and sent out to sea in early 2028. Future versions will be built on floating structures that more closely resemble offshore oil platforms.

To Sipe, the decision to turn to the ocean was obvious.

After years flying E-2D Hawkeyes off U.S. Navy aircraft carriers, he had an appreciation for the ocean’s immense power. “Most people think, oh, ocean — hard waves, storms, corrosion, biofouling. And I just see this majestic place and resource that we just ignore,” he said.

As the planet warms, the oceans have been absorbing an additional 5.8 million terawatt-hours of energy every year. By comparison, all of human civilization uses about 180,000 terawatt-hours each year. If Drogue were to take some, surely the ocean wouldn’t miss it.

The startup’s technology works like a heat pump run in reverse. A heat pump uses electricity to move heat from one place to another; Drogue’s system instead uses a difference in temperature to generate electricity. This approach, known as ocean thermal energy conversion, or OTEC, was first developed in the 1950s.

Surface waters are warmer, heated by the sun, while down-deep temperatures are much colder, about 39°F on average (4°C). “There’s basically no amount of heat we can pull out of that that will collapse that temperature differential,” Sipe said.

To leverage the temperature differential, the startup’s system draws in warm surface water, uses it to cool the data centers, and then sends the now-warmer water to boil ammonia, a chemical that turns to gas at -28°F. That ammonia gas is then sent through a turbine to generate electricity. Once through the turbine, it courses through a condenser, which turns the gas back into a liquid. There, cold water drawn from 1,000 meters deep passes over the condenser’s fins to absorb the heat. The warmed water is then released, and dissipates into the ocean. The ammonia gets sent back to the start of the sealed loop, and the cycle repeats.

The basic idea was tested in the 1980s but tossed aside. “A lot has changed since the ’80s, specifically manufacturing techniques around the pipe and the heat exchangers,” Sipe said.

To find out why, Sipe sought out people who had worked on those early efforts. “I just talked to everyone, basically interviewed them and figured out what went wrong, what went right, what would you do next. And everyone was like, dude, this stuff works. It’s just a matter of being able to raise enough capital to build something big enough, because there’s a sweet spot,” he said.

There also had to be customers who could use that power while still at sea. Sending power to shore would require lengthy, expensive cables, and manufacturing on board would require similarly expensive shipping of materials and finished goods. Data centers are ideal — the servers are compact relative to other equipment, and shipping data off the platform is incredibly cheap. Each vessel would live at sea for about a decade, Sipe said.

Drogue already has “approval in principle,” a preliminary sign-off, from the American Bureau of Shipping, which sets and checks safety standards for ships. Now it’s in the process of buying its first ship to start building a prototype ready for the open ocean.

In many ways, Drogue’s concept is similar to Elon Musk’s plan for orbital data centers: find a cheap source of power, and get the data centers as close to it as possible. Except unlike orbital data centers, Drogue’s don’t need to launch expensive rockets to get there. They can just sail a ship on the ocean, something humanity has been doing for millennia.

The surge in fuel prices since the U.S. and Israel attacked Iran in February has driven up the cost of shipping by sea, air, rail and road.

"If anything, it benefits the scale players over the little players, but it's kind of a little bit bad for everybody," Cheris said. "I think that is the challenge."

#365. Where's the growth? Legacy automakers face a tougher 2027 as costs rise, market tightens

DETROIT — There's a growing concern that the global automotive industry may be stuck in neutral, if not reverse, heading into 2027.

The auto industry — from the companies to their customers — has been surprisingly resilient this decade, but there are fears that rising costs, weaker vehicle pricing and a more challenging market could threaten profits and growth going forward.

Automakers also face pressure outside the U.S. from an onslaught of Chinese carmakers, a thinner new-product pipeline after retreating from some all-electric plans, and company- and region-specific challenges.

"With investor focus increasingly shifting to 2027, the setup appears challenging," Barclays analyst Dan Levy said in an investor note last month. "Rising inflation, continued China pressure, affordability concerns in the U.S., and a flat global production outlook suggest another year of limited earnings growth."

Several Wall Street analysts have said automakers will need continued pricing strength and additional cost savings to offset inflation, higher investment spending and a potentially weaker economy just to maintain their current levels.

"It's a little bit more optimistic in the U.S. and then very pessimistic in Europe," RBC Capital Markets analyst Tom Narayan told CNBC. "As a whole, there's some negativity on '27."

Automakers from the U.S. to Europe to Asia have been facing a growing list of headwinds.

For U.S.-based legacy automakers, Ford Motor said it expects next year's results to be in line with 2026, while General Motors grew more cautious about its ability to continue growing earnings as much as it had previously expected.

"We continue to believe that 2027 will be another strong year and better than 2026, assuming customer demand remains strong, and commodity, logistics and other recent cost pressures moderate," GM CEO Mary Barra told investors Oct. 20.

GM CFO Paul Jacobson said that while it's too early to officially forecast, the company is facing additional inflationary costs that could lead to another $2 billion headwind in 2027, like it expects this year. He specifically cited costs of commodity inflation, logistics and DRAM, or dynamic random-access memory chips as challenges.

Those chips are critical components in modern vehicles and are used for everything from temporary data storage and real-time processing from infotainment systems to safety-critical systems.

GM and others have been able to offset outside pressures through cost cuts, operational efficiencies and resilient consumer demand for higher-margin vehicles. However, there's a limit to what companies can do.

"There's only so much pricing you can push to consumers," Narayan said. "They're already at pretty high pricing."

European automakers including Volkswagen, Mercedes-Benz and BMW in recent weeks reduced their 2026 earnings forecasts in response to U.S. tariffs, the growth of Chinese automakers and rising costs.

Most recently, BMW this week said accelerated restructuring measures would reduce its automotive pretax earnings margin this year by 2.5 percentage points. The company said the restructuring should support "positive effects" into 2027 and a 3% to 5% pretax profit margin for 2028. That compares with a 5.3% automotive margin last year and 3.4% during the third quarter.

Chrysler parent Stellantis, which has large operations in Europe and the U.S., is facing its own challenges, as it's in the midst of a restructuring that aims for a low-single-digit adjusted operating margin this year. It has maintained a 2026 guidance that includes a mid-single-digit percentage increase in net revenue and a low-single-digit adjusted operating margin.

In Asia, South Korean automaker Hyundai Motor, which has experienced notable growth in recent years, also cut key metrics when it reported earnings late last month. The company lowered its 2026 wholesales target to 4 million vehicles, down from 4.16 million, and cut its operating-margin target to between 5.5% and 6.5%, down from a previous goal of 6.3% to 7.3%.

Toyota Motor is an outlier for its most recent quarter and outlook, as the world's largest automaker increased its full-year operating income forecast by 500 billion Japanese yen (about $3.2 billion) on Thursday.

Toyota's results were boosted by weakness in the Japanese yen as well as strong hybrid vehicle demand in the U.S. However, the increase in operating income was its first in six quarters.

Broadly, the changes to earnings and outlooks come as production for the global industry slows.

Consulting and insights firm GlobalData expects global light-vehicle production of about 90.96 million units in 2026, down 2.1% compared with last year. It forecast a production increase of less than 1% next year. That comes on the heels of strong annual growth earlier this decade, led by China.

Cox Automotive reported the average transaction price of a new U.S. vehicle in September was more than $50,500. That was just shy of the record of $50,614 in December 2025.

Lofty vehicle prices have led to record-high and record-long financing deals, as well as concerns that customers are getting priced out of the new-vehicle market.

The typical U.S. new-vehicle payment reached $776 a month in September, the highest since December 2023, according to Cox Automotive.

Cox Automotive chief economist Jeremy Robb, discussing the third quarter for the automotive industry, rated the overall economy, buyer ability and borrowing costs as cautionary "yellow lights."

But he did have some "green lights," such as good credit availability and employment conditions, which could give some relief for automakers and consumers, many of whom need vehicles for their everyday lives.

Robb said there's a "tug-of-war" between headwinds such as rising costs, interest rates and fuel prices and tailwinds such as growth in liquid assets and savings that has helped offset some of the additional costs.

"Consumers remain under financial pressure, but the vehicle demand continues to hold, showing more resiliency in the auto industry than we've seen in other parts of the economy," Robb said during a Sept. 24 call, noting consumer sentiment remains low.

#357. Cosm rides premium moviegoing wave with more immersive screenings and a wider library

The next big thing in premium large format theatrical viewing takes inspiration from the stars.

Cosm, an immersive entertainment technology company, got its start programming planetariums — its name a combination of "cosmos" and "colosseum." The company's massive, domed screens first offered guests a view of the universe, now they mimic courtside seats at sports venues around the world and drop film fans into some of Hollywood's most iconic sets.

At a time when the theatrical industry is seeing its biggest growth from the sale of premium large format, or PLF, tickets, Cosm seeks to offer a fresh take on an elevated viewing experience. And it's quickly expanding both its footprint and its content library.

Since 2024, Cosm has opened four theaters — near Los Angeles, Dallas, Atlanta and Detroit — with a Cleveland hub set to open in 2027. These experimental venues have garnered the attention of sports fans, screening live matchups in an arena-like environment. Showings often sell out, according to Cosm.

More recently, Cosm has partnered with Hollywood studios like Warner Bros., now a part of Skydance, to bring the likes of "The Matrix," "Willy Wonka & the Chocolate Factory," "Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone" and "It" to its locations. Cosm recently signed a deal with Universal, which will bring "Shrek" to its domes in December.

The agreements mark a lucrative new chapter for the privately held Cosm, expanding its audience reach and viewing opportunities beyond timed, live events.

As a privately held company, Cosm doesn't disclose its financials. Tickets cost anywhere from $18 for a matinee to around $70 for prime seats or in-demand showings. That doesn't include added fees for concessions or items from special, themed menus tied to screenings.

Sony invested $100 million in the company in June, taking a minority stake and placing Sony Pictures Entertainment Chairman and CEO Ravi Ahuja onto its board of directors.

Cosm also counts among its investors Cosm chairman Steve Winn's firm, Mirasol Capital; former Milwaukee Bucks co-owner Marc Lasry's Avenue Sports Fund; Rocket Mortgage co-founder Dan Gilbert's Rock; and David Blitzer, managing partner of the New Jersey Devils and Philadelphia 76ers, who invested through his firm Bolt Ventures.

"Cosm is really early in its growth trajectory at the moment, and we saw an opportunity to partner with the company at, what I would say is at, a very important stage in its development," Jay Levine, chief strategy and business operations officer at Sony, told CNBC. "They have about five locations with the goal of a pretty significant rollout. And the real reason why we did it was it gave us a lot of exposure to a growing distribution and what we deem an experiential platform that we think has long-term potential."

Sony has made several plays in the theatrical space in recent years. It bought Alamo Drafthouse in 2024 and entered a long-term lease to reopen and restore the iconic Cinerama Dome in Los Angeles in July of this year.

"Our experiences strategy is around tech, it's around fandom, and it's around new and innovative approaches to content distribution," Levine said. "For us, we're all about these differentiated experiences."

Cosm's 87-foot-diameter, concave screens drop viewers into the middle of the action, whether it's an NFL game or inside the world of "The Matrix." The company's forthcoming Cleveland location will have an even bigger dome, at 100 feet in diameter.

The experience is akin to that of the Sphere in Las Vegas, which has garnered headlines since opening in 2023 for hosting high-profile concerts and events.

"People absolutely love to live out what they love in fandom, just like wearing a sports jersey," Alexis Scalice, head of Cosm Studios, told CNBC. "And I love that I'm seeing that same behavior happen with cinema."

Stadium-style seating is nestled within each Cosm dome, with booths and tables set on each level. The dome's width is such that it fills much of the viewer's periphery, giving the sensation that they are part of the on-screen environment.

The Los Angeles and Dallas locations have capacity for around 350 seats each, while the newer domes in Atlanta and Detroit have up to 500 available seats. Cosm has not shared how many seats the Cleveland location will have, but with its larger screen it is expected to have a larger number of seats.

For live sports events, Cosm has specialty cameras mounted in arenas that broadcast back to the dome, creating the illusion of being on the field of play.

For theatrical features, the film is played in a large rectangle in the center of the dome and the background is transformed to extend the scene.

In Cosm's presentation of "Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone," for example, during the iconic scene where thousands of Hogwarts acceptance letters flood through the fireplace and into a living room, the background is swarmed with animated envelopes.

Cosm has partnered with experiential production company Little Cinema and creative studio MakeMake to create the visuals and environments for each scene of a film released in the dome. From storyboarding to post-production, this process can take up to a year as Cosm, the participating studio and the visual effects artists craft and render each moment.

"We really work hard not to take away from the story, so the film remains the hero in the narrative," Scalice said. "Working very closely with Warner Bros. and our creative partners to make sure that we're not detracting too much from the story, but we're enhancing those moments for more elevated emotional response."

With the exception of "It," which was released in 2017, Cosm's theatrical offerings thus far have been films that were released decades ago and that typically have long-established fan bases.

That helps Cosm leverage what Scalice called "that nostalgia factor" — "parents coming in that ... grew up on this, and they want to share it with their children."

"We can reinvigorate these legendary films," she added.

Cosm has dabbled across genres — horror, fantasy, science fiction — and also demographics — family-friendly and R-rated. "Shrek," set to debut Dec. 16, will be its first foray into animation.

"The thing that's so incredible about it is everybody thought initially it's just going to be working for sports, and when you look at what they've done, a lot of the titles with 'Harry Potter,' with 'Shrek,' and some of the other ones, I mean, the fill rates are actually incredible," Sony's Levine said.

Studios and theatrical exhibitors alike are looking for innovative ways to showcase Hollywood's product to moviegoers, especially as major shifts in viewing habits have disrupted the business.

In the post-pandemic era, the cinema space has been defined by fewer screens and fewer moviegoers, but those declines have been masked by higher movie ticket prices, particularly for PLF experiences.

These elevated offerings — think Imax, Dolby Cinema, ScreenX and 4DX — have bigger screens and better sound or introduce a unique experience, such as moving seats and fog machines. They're particularly popular with Gen Z and millennials, who've yet to be deterred by rising average ticket prices.

While an average standard movie ticket costs around $13, premium tickets average more than $18 each, according to data from EntTelligence.

Prices for Cosm's events fluctuate based on showtime and venue. Sony's Levine said the company's ticket prices and attendance rates are "higher than I think anybody really would have thought."

Unlike Cosm's sports offerings, which can also be viewed from outside the dome from its hall and deck viewing areas at a lower price, theatrical presentations are only available in the dome.

"They have no problem selling these tickets because people want to experience these things, like an immersive format," said Mike Polydoros, CEO at cinema marketing firm PaperAirplane Media. "That is something they can't get at home."

#327. Poppi founders are going after PepsiCo's Gatorade with new beverage startup Jetty

After disrupting the soda category with Poppi, founders Allison and Stephen Ellsworth are now taking aim at hydration with their new brand, Jetty.

Starting Monday, Jetty drinks and ready-to-mix electrolyte packets will be on shelves at Whole Foods grocery stores and available online at Target and Amazon.

The new, self-funded venture directly pits the Ellsworths against PepsiCo, which bought Poppi for $1.95 billion in 2025. Pepsi owns Gatorade, which dominates the U.S. sports drink category with about 60% market share, according to Numerator data.

Poppi shook up the soda category with prebiotic fiber, low calorie and sugar counts and its fun marketing aimed at Generation Z and millennials. Jetty looks to upend the hydration category through a similar strategy. To do so, it will face off not only against Gatorade but also a swarm of other upstarts looking to get in on the electrolyte-fueled trend.

For decades, Gatorade and other sports drinks have pitched their products to athletes who need electrolytes and fluids after an intense workout, although many consumers drink them every day without even sweating. Jetty is aimed at people who need a hydration boost, but not the same level of electrolytes or sugar as a traditional sports drink.

"No one has really created a product for just everyday hydration — literally everyday hydration for normal people," Allison Ellsworth, Jetty's chief brand officer, told CNBC. "Like I woke up this morning, I didn't run a marathon."

Following Poppi's sale to PepsiCo, Ellsworth stayed on as an advisor to the brand, although she said she is no longer involved in the day-to-day operations.

After 10 years building Poppi, Ellsworth said, she finally had free time. As Ellsworth tells it, she went to the doctor for the first time in a decade. She started using a GLP-1 medication and lost 60 pounds.

Ellsworth's husband, Stephen, now CEO of the new brand, noticed she wasn't hydrating, even when she came back from the gym.

GLP-1 medications such as Ozempic and Mounjaro can make people less thirsty. But because staying hydrated can also help mitigate some of the drugs' side effects, such as nausea and headaches, it can become more important.

"I physically struggle getting liquid into my body," Ellsworth said.

Plain old water is often less appealing to consumers, even dehydrated GLP-1 users, but more flavorful options with added benefits can be tempting. About 44% of GLP-1 users report consuming more functional beverages, according to EY-Parthenon's third annual survey of more than 3,600 current or former GLP-1 users.

Ellsworth sees a broader appeal for Jetty, too.

"Our core audience is going to be millennial moms and Gen Z," she said. "Gen Z just isn't relating to these old school brands, the legacy of the Gatorades and the Powerades. They're just not cool."

Consumers broadly have become more interested in staying hydrated.

Dollar sales of hydration and electrolyte products climbed 20% to $2.6 billion over the 52 weeks ended Oct. 4, based on data from SPINS, which tracks new universal product codes, or UPCs. The category also has a strong repeat buyer rate of 62%.

However, the Ellsworths aren't the only ones to notice the potential in hydration. Big food and beverage companies have been looking to hydration, too.

You'd recognise an e-scooter if you saw one. The Department for Transport's (DfT's) definition of an e-scooter includes no pedals, two wheels and a maximum continuous power rating of 500W.

And so far, the trials around England appear to have proved popular. According to DfT research, fom January 2022 to May 2024, 36.9 million trips were made across 30 trial areas outside of London. On average, nearly 19,500 e-scooters were available to hire every day.

Dr Yasir Ali, a reader in Transport Modelling at Loughborough University, says e-scooters are "complementary" to other modes of transport, rather than replacing them. Due to factors such as range and being exposed to the elements, they are less suitable for longer trips, rural areas with less infrastructure or in bad weather. They work best as "a first- and last-mile solution" in urban areas, to reduce reliance on cars for short trips.

Shared mobility charity CoMoUK's 2025 report suggests that for more than half of the people using rental e-scooters, the most common use was travelling to work or school. Its analysis of DfT data suggests the majority of people using hired e-scooters are male and under 35.

"Sometimes it's a bit difficult to take the bus from dot to dot", says Yan Zhang, who works at Southampton university and doesn't drive, "so for shorter distances, I'll take the scooter."

Like Yan Zhang, e-scooter users are less likely to have a car, CoMo's report says - this is partly explained by official trials' concentration in urban areas

Around half of respondents to CoMoUK's online survey used it in conjunction with another mode of transport - such as riding to a railway station, like Keith.

Earlier evidence from a national evaluation of the trials published in 2022 found that although some users would have driven instead had they not had access to an e-scooter, the biggest "modal shift" - meaning switch in ways of getting around - to e-scooters was from walking. Given walking is an active mode of travel, this has raised questions over how significant the public health impact of e-scooter use really is.

And the usage of privately owned e-scooters isn't reflected in this research. The DfT has said between 760,000 and 1.2 million people in England aged 16 and over own one - despite the fact that it remains illegal to ride them in public.

In the eyes of the law, e-scooters are classed as motor vehicles. Outside of the trials, they can't be insured, and thus can only legally be ridden on private land, with the owner's permission.

Parking bays for e-scooters and e-bikes are available across Southampton

The trials themselves are bound by strict rules. The devices' top speed is limited. Businesses who operate the pilots must insure them. Users must have a full or provisional driving licence, and they must not ride on the pavement. Helmets are recommended but not mandatory.

Richard Dilks, CEO of CoMoUK says "mass-scale use of something that is inherently low-carbon, low-impact, low-cost" is a "good thing" but "we also don't have rules of engagement about how they should be ridden".

And as a result of the delays in introducing regulation, Loughborogh University's Yasir Ali says "we are increasingly in a situation where the law and reality have diverged", with "the disadvantages of e-scooter use without securing the full benefits of regulation".

Back in Southampton, I meet a lady pushing a black e-scooter, who doesn't want to give her name. She says nobody told her when she bought it that it was illegal to ride on the streets.

The Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency has a unit that carries out surveillance of retailers to check they're complying with the law. But earlier this month, an investigation by the Press Association news agency discovered some retailers were advertising them for commuting purposes.

Police tell me they regularly see children illegally riding private e-scooters. Margaret Winchcomb, deputy executive director at the Parliamentary Advisory Council for Transport Safety (PACTS), says some are being specifically marketed at kids.

Police say they will seize privately owned e-scooters that are ridden illegally

So what can police do? Forces regularly launch crackdowns where they seize illegally used devices, and often crush them. Each year, awareness campaigns run urging parents not to buy their kids an e-scooter for Christmas. But enforcement is a challenge.

Insp Matt Boiles from Avon and Somerset's road policing team says problems arise even with the legal trial devices - for example, people riding them after drinking alcohol, which counts as drink-driving. Riding with another person on the back is another common issue.

But officers see private e-scooters being illegally ridden in public places "all the time, every single day" and they can be really hard to catch - it isn't always safe for police in a car to pursue someone on an e-scooter, he says.

Insp Boiles despairs over parents buying them for children: "We do have parents attend a police station quite a lot, getting very, very upset that their child's Â£500 e-scooter has been seized." But "the legislation is really, really clear that you can't have it back".

There's a potential risk of fire if unregulated devices with lithium batteries are left to charge unattended. Most train operators have banned e-scooters on board for this reason.

But there's also the fact that riders are unprotected in the case of falling off or being hit by motorised vehicles. And - along with e-bikes - they can end up involved in collisions where pedestrians are hurt. Many trial e-scooters in the UK are restricted to 12.5mph, but privately owned scooters' speeds aren't.

Some coroners have even raised concerns. In 2022, a coroner in London called for action after the death of 14-year-old Fatima Abukah on a privately owned scooter.

According to the DfT, in 2024 - the most recent year for which figures are available - 1,390 casualties were recorded in collisions that involved e-scooter in England, Scotland and Wales, three more than in the previous 12 months. The vast majority, 1,096, were e-scooter users themselves.

Young boys and men appear to be over-represented in the stats. Of the casualties, 302 - the most of any age and sex category - were males aged 10-19.

Six deaths were recorded in 2024, unchanged from the previous year. Five of these were riders and one a pedestrian.

Although the numbers aren't conclusive, it is thought more accidents are happening on privately owned e-scooters. Winchcomb says police statistics "aren't reflective of the number of injuries".

Nonetheless, campaigners believe there is enough evidence to show that regulation is urgently needed.

Carly Calland's 14-year-old son Jacob died in March 2025 of a catastrophic head injury. He was a passenger on an e-scooter that was involved with a collision with a car.

If privately owned e-scooters are legalised for public use, Carly believes, there should be mandatory helmets, a ban on carrying passengers and penalties for parents that allow children to ride illegally.

"If Jacob was wearing a helmet that day, he would still be here," Carly, from Wythenshawe in Greater Manchester, says.

Carly is not against e-scooters. They "are really good for people to get to work, and they are eco-friendly", she tells me, but "they just need to be used in the correct way."

What happens if the current situation goes on? Her answer is emphatic: "More deaths."

Meanwhile a number of major European cities have ended their shared e-scooter schemes. Paris abolished them after a referendum in 2023. Brussels is the most recent, announcing theirs would end in 2027, following a rise in accidents.

The last evaluation of the trials in England was in 2022. New findings are expected by early next year.

In recent years, successive British transport secretaries have supported the idea of a new law to legalise and regulate the e-scooters available for sale. E-scooters were mentioned in the 2022 Queen's speech, but nothing materialised.

#305. Eli Lilly’s Foundayo obesity pill eases sleep apnea in late-stage trial, paving way for approval

Eli Lilly on Monday said its obesity pill Foundayo significantly reduced the severity of obstructive sleep apnea, the most common sleep-related breathing disorder, in patients with obesity, in a late-stage trial.

The drugmaker plans to file by the end of the year for U.S. approval of the daily pill for moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea, or OSA, in adults with obesity or who are overweight. The results make the pill — which rolled out in the U.S. in April — one of several GLP-1 treatments to show promise in treating the condition.

OSA refers to interrupted breathing during sleep due to narrowed or blocked airways, which can cause drops in oxygen and increases in blood pressure that are associated with serious complications, such as hypertension, heart failure and stroke. The condition affects nearly 1 billion adults globally, including 80 million people ages 20 and over in the U.S., according to the National Council on Aging. Most have never been diagnosed, Lilly said in a release.

Lilly's blockbuster weight loss injection Zepbound became the first medication to win U.S. approval for OSA in December 2024. Patients have limited treatment options outside of that drug and many use cumbersome machines that provide positive airway pressure, or PAP, to allow for normal breathing.

"Foundayo is already making a difference for people living with obesity, and with plans to submit these results to the FDA by the end of the year, we could have the opportunity to help people living with OSA," Dr. Thomas Seck, senior vice president of product development of Lilly Cardiometabolic Health, said in a release.

The trial tested Foundayo in two independent studies, the first of which included 363 patients who were unable or unwilling to use PAP therapy. The second study included 349 people who were on PAP therapy for at least three months at the time of screening and continued that treatment during the trial.

Patients in both studies who took Foundayo started at a low dose and increased it gradually at four-week intervals to different maximum dosages.

Researchers specifically examined how much the pill reduced the so-called apnea-hypopnea index, or AHI, which records the number of times per hour a person's breathing shows a restricted or completely blocked airway. The index is used to evaluate the severity of obstructive sleep apnea and the effectiveness of treatments for the condition.

People who took Foundayo and were not using PAP therapy had an average of 21.1 fewer AHI events per hour at 52 weeks, compared with an average reduction of 4.8 per hour for the placebo, according to the results.

Foundayo also met the trial's second goal in that group, leading to an overall AHI reduction of 43.8% compared with a decrease of 6.3% for the placebo. Lilly said 31.5% of the participants in the Foundayo group met the criteria for remission or improvement to mild OSA.

Meanwhile, patients who were on both Foundayo and PAP therapy had an average of 18.3 fewer AHI events per hour at 52 weeks, compared with an average reduction of 5.5 per hour for the placebo.

Foundayo led to an overall AHI reduction of around 27.5% in that group, compared with a decrease of 3% for the placebo. The company said 19.6% of the participants on Foundayo and PAP therapy also met the criteria for remission or mild OSA.

Foundayo also reduced what's known as the sleep apnea-specific hypoxic burden — a measure of how deeply and for how long oxygen levels fall during sleep. That metric fell by 55.9% in people not using PAP therapy and 45.8% in those using it.

Lilly said the pill also helped patients lose roughly 11% of their weight across groups at 52 weeks and helped to lower a marker of inflammation.

The company said the safety data and how well patients tolerated Foundayo were generally consistent with previous trials on the pill.

Around 12% of people on Foundayo who were not using PAP therapy discontinued due to side effects, compared to 3.8% with the placebo. Meanwhile, 6.8% of those on both Lilly's pill and PAP discontinued the drug due to side effects, compared with 0.6% in the placebo group.

The most common side effects were gastrointestinal, including nausea, diarrhea, constipation and vomiting, and were generally mild to moderate in severity. That's consistent with other GLP-1 medications.

#304. Aviation faces hotter, stormier skies – and passengers might have to accept more disruption

Aviation faces hotter, stormier skies â and passengers might have to accept more disruption

As the country sweltered through one of the many heatwaves of a baking summer in late June, towering thunderclouds were gathering over the south of England and across much of northern Europe. It was a Saturday, and with the holiday season getting under way, thousands of passengers were heading to London's Heathrow and Gatwick airports to catch flights abroad. It was always going to be a very busy day.

What followed, however, was chaos for travellers. As air traffic controllers struggled to route aircraft around the storms, airspace became heavily congested and many planes due to fly were simply forced to wait on the ground. In the end, some 900 flights to and from both airports were delayed, some by up to 11 hours. Dozens more were cancelled. At airports across Europe, passengers expecting to fly into London also faced long waits and uncertainty.

The weather in the UK may now have cooled, and passengers may soon forget their experiences. But some within the industry believe what happened in June should be seen as a warning.

The timing in this case was clearly unlucky. But there are concerns that such incidents are only going to become more common as the effects of climate change mount up around the world and weather systems are likely to become increasingly unstable, making disruption more likely and adding to costs for airlines, airports and travellers.

Tim Atkinson, an airline risk consultant and former pilot, says the very crowded skies over the UK and northern Europe mean the region is particularly exposed.

He thinks there is a real risk, for example, that all of London's airports could be affected by storms at the same time, potentially with major consequences in the air and on the ground.

"What we're seeing now is very rapid climate change," he says.

"We're going from the kind of weather activity which takes out an airport, and which the system kind of copes withâ¦ to a situation where, with very little predictability, it would be possible to lose all of the London airports from real-time operation quite swiftly and with little notice".

Such a scenario, he thinks, would make the disruption for passengers caused by the incident in June seem relatively minor â and could even put lives at risk. But how likely is it?

For controllers at National Air Traffic Services's (NATS) main control centre in Swanwick, Hampshire, thunderstorms are a major preoccupation. When I visit, it is a hot and humid day, there are ominous dark clouds in the sky, and heavy, thundery downpours are hitting the surrounding region.

In Terminal Control, the part of the building responsible for flights heading to or from airports in London and southern England, those same storm cells are being monitored continuously. Their progress is tracked on large radar screens, while at the same time weather forecasts are carefully scrutinised.

Air traffic tends to come in waves during the day. Right now, at lunchtime, it is relatively quiet and the storms have yet to affect major routes. But with activity expected to intensify in the afternoon, staff are already preparing for what could be a tricky few hours.

Stormy weather can shrink the available airspace, meaning traffic has to be restricted and rerouted. If planes are unable to land at one airport, for example due to high winds, they may need diverting to another. But that can create its own problems; if too many unscheduled aircraft land at an already busy airport, it might not have sufficient infrastructure to accommodate them all.

Heavy rain and storms can make flying harder

Planes need fuel and somewhere to park. Passengers need food and facilities. Ultimately, if there isn't space, it can simply refuse to accept all but emergency traffic.

This is where the scenario set out by Atkinson comes in. He warns of a situation where traffic is diverted from Heathrow, Gatwick, Luton and Stansted at the same time, leaving aircraft coming in across the Atlantic and a large number of European flights all looking for somewhere to land at the same time, and some with dwindling fuel reserves.

This would at the very least create serious disruption for passengers, who might end up hundreds of miles from their destinations. But it could also, he thinks, create "a very serious risk event, such as an aircraft running out of fuel in the sky".

Aircraft are legally required to carry enough fuel to take them to an alternative airport if necessary, and to allow them to fly for an extra 30 minutes on top of that.

Not everyone shares Atkinson's concern. Former airline executive Andrew Charlton, who manages the Swiss-based consultancy Aviation Advocacy, thinks the risk is low.

"You've always got to imagine the worst-case scenario", he says. "But I also think we need to keep that worst case in context.

"Yes, it's possible to imagine it. But I'm also fairly comfortable with the ability of this industry to react well and make good decisionsâ¦ of all the things that wake me up at 3amâ¦this is not the top of that list."

Thunderstorms are caused by what is known as convective activity, where moist air close to the ground warms rapidly and rises quickly. As it rises, it expands and cools, allowing the moisture it carries to condense, forming large clouds. More heat means more convective energy, potentially creating instability. In addition, for every degree the Earth warms, the atmosphere can hold about 7% more moisture, increasing the probability of heavy rainfall.

It is not clear whether this means we will actually get more thunderstorms in the UK and across Europe, as so many variables are involved beyond the simple matter of energy and moisture. Some parts of the world may even get fewer. However, studies have shown that higher latitudes, particularly in the northern hemisphere, will see the biggest increases in favourable conditions for storms to form, and those that do may be more violent. According to the UK's Met Office, "while we may not see more thunderstorms overall, the ones we do experience could be more impactful".

Where possible, pilots will avoid flying through convective weather. Even though aircraft are designed to cope with severe conditions, storms can produce intense turbulence, capable of damaging the structural frame of the plane, as well as throwing people around the cabin if they are not strapped in. Hail can also be a big problem, potentially cracking windscreens or causing engine problems. So, however much inconvenience it may cause passengers, crews will invariably put safety first.

New technology helps pilots navigate tricky flying conditions

Onboard weather radars can help aircrew to see up to 320 nautical miles ahead. They also have access to live weather forecasting resources online. But thunderstorms can move quickly and, according to Prof Guy Gratton, an expert in aeronautics at Cranfield University, these systems are essentially "tactical rather than strategic". In other words, although they enable pilots to see storm cells on their screens from a relatively short distance away, and request a diversion, they do not allow for detailed long-distance planning.

The result is that unplanned diversions often happen late in flight, which is likely to mean burning more fuel. If storm delays do become more common, says Andrew Charlton, then aircraft will have to load more fuel as a matter of course, in case there is trouble brewing ahead. "Aircraft are going to have to fly further, and they're going to have to factor in the assumption they're going to fly further," he says. For an airline, fuel is one of its major costs â and if the fuel bill goes up, he says, it is inevitable passengers will pay more.

More intense storms would be likely to make air traffic management in busy regions harder and potentially lead to more delays and cancellations. However, it is possible some of this could be mitigated using new technologies.

Both Heathrow and Gatwick, for example, have moved away from mandating minimum distances between incoming aircraft in favour of a system which continually calculates spacing times based on wind speeds and aircraft types. In practice, this is meant to allow controllers to reduce the distance between landing aircraft, particularly in high winds, and get them on the ground more quickly. According to NATS, it has successfully improved landing rates and reduced delays by a significant margin.

However, there could be other effects from more severe storms as well. Heavier rainfall, for instance, increases the likelihood of flooding. In a worst case, this can paralyse airport operations, as happened in April 2024 when torrential downpours hit Dubai International Airport. It briefly had to close its runways, while disruption lasted for two days. More than 1,200 flights were cancelled, affecting tens of thousands of passengers.

Passengers were delayed during the floods in Dubai

But even if runways and taxiways themselves are clear, and aircraft are able to fly without any difficulty, passengers may struggle to catch their flights if roads, railways or car parks are flooded.

Protecting against such risks can be expensive. Gatwick Airport, which experienced severe flooding over the Christmas period in 2013, has spent tens of millions of pounds on mitigation strategies in recent years â although those figures are dwarfed by the more than Â£2bn it is expecting to invest in its current expansion plans.

The problem of clear-air turbulence is also a real one. It cannot be seen, and is becoming increasingly common as the climate warms.

This is when air moves violently at high altitudes with no clouds or other visual cues to help a pilot avoid it. For passengers, it usually only means a little bit of bumpiness, but occasionally it can be a lot more serious. On 1 March 2023, for example, a Lufthansa flight from Austin, Texas to Frankfurt encountered what the airline described as "brief but severe" turbulence.

Those aboard described how passengers and flight crew were thrown around the cabin as the plane suddenly lost altitude. After it made an emergency landing in Washington DC, seven people were taken to hospital.

While still rare, this kind of turbulence is already much more prevalent than it used to be and is likely to become even more so.

"You know the projections are pretty dire: doubling, trebling or worse in the amount of severe turbulence on many busy flight routes around the world," says Paul Williams, a professor of atmospheric science at the University of Reading. He says incidents over the North Atlantic have increased by 55% since 1979.

Nevertheless, he remains confident solutions can be found, including improvements to forecasting and making use of laser-based technology.

There is another climate-related threat to aviation, which may prove challenging to overcome: rising sea levels. When Hurricane Sandy arrived off the US East Coast in October 2012, it provoked a storm surge that flooded the city. The two runways at New York's LaGuardia airport were left under water for several days, preventing it from reopening even after the winds had subsided.

Significant flooding that would have affected the New York City area once in every 500 years in pre-industrial times is now experienced every 25 years or so â and could occur every five years by the middle of the century, according to one major study. That makes LaGuardia, built on reclaimed land next to Flushing Bay, appear highly vulnerable.

LaGuardia could be at greater risk of flooding and future closures

It is not alone. With some 40% of the world's population living within 100km of coastlines, many airports have been built on low-lying areas near the sea. According to research by Newcastle University, 269 airports around the world are already at risk of flooding due to rising sea levels, and that total is expected to rise. Among those potentially most exposed are the likes of Amsterdam Schiphol, London City, Bahrain International and Newark Liberty International.

Many airports do already have some degree of flood protection, especially larger ones. But stopping the flood risks from rising over the next century means expensive investment in flood defences, raising runways, or even moving airports. It could cost up to $57bn (Â£42bn) according to the Newcastle report. Such costs are ultimately likely to be met by taxpayers or by travellers.

The aviation industry is often criticised for its own contribution to climate change emissions. It faces a forbidding challenge in attempting to meet a target of being carbon neutral by 2050, while also accommodating growth that is expected to see the global aircraft fleet double in size over the next two decades.

"I think the best we can do is mitigate", says Andrew Charlton.

"We need to make sure we have enough space at air terminals for people whose flights are being disrupted and delayed, that we have enough hotel rooms and parking space and so on."

But ultimately, he says, "disagreeable moments are going to happen more".

#292. My hometown shows that high streets have to change or die

My hometown shows that high streets have to change or die

The empty building that used to be the home of Marks & Spencer in Aberdeen is one of the first things you see when you arrive off the train and come into the city centre. The sign has gone, though the shape of the lettering can still be made out; the windows are blacked out and the doors boarded up.

This is my hometown. As a teenager, I spent hours on Union Street, its main thoroughfare, nicknamed the Granite Mile after the local silver-grey stone. It was the place to try on all the latest fashions from the likes of Clockhouse and Chelsea Girl.

The street used to be packed with shoppers from across the north-east of Scotland.

"I come to Union Street to take a bus somewhere else... there's nothing to shop for nowâ¦ it's horrendous," complains Marie Sim, a lifelong Aberdeen resident.

It's a similar story across the country: High Streets, once bursting with shops, hit first by the rise of out-of-town retail centres and then by online commerce.

But Aberdeen is in the thick of an ambitious turnaround plan. The port city has essentially declared war on its empty shop problem, taking an unusually hands-on approach to filling vacant units and bringing long-neglected upper floors of buildings into use.

The Marks & Spencer flagship store in Aberdeen closed in August, 80 years after it opened there

On top of that, the council is pouring tens of millions into trying to make the city centre a destination again. Like many other places, it's leaning into the "experience economy", with a new indoor food and drink market and a major redevelopment of the seafront.

High Streets are integral to how we feel about where we live. The sight of vacant shops and graffiti can quickly become a vicious circle, deterring shoppers, discouraging investment and leading to anti-social behaviour.

Politicians of all stripes have been trying for years to breathe new life into town centres - and Andy Burnham, who has made a focus on "place" part of his political identity, is the latest. He argues they should become "symbols of Britain's renaissance".

But can it be achieved? And if they're to survive, do town centres need a fundamental rethink?

One in eight properties on British High Streets are currently vacant, according to Green Street, the property and retail analytics company, a number that's stayed stubbornly high for years.

"If there was a silver bullet, it would have been fired years ago," says Jackie Sadek, a former government adviser on urban regeneration, who has spent nearly four decades working in town centre renewal.

One of the biggest obstacles is fractured ownership. Shops and buildings are often owned by dozens of different landlords, sometimes through offshore companies or complex structures, each with their own interests and priorities. That can slow decision-making and make large-scale regeneration difficult.

But even with empty units that are available, you need to find someone willing to take them on.

One in eight properties on British High Streets are currently vacant

The cost of running a High Street business has also risen; employment costs are higher, and there's the burden of business rates.

Firms need customers. Footfall on many High Streets has been in long-term decline as shopping and services move online, and this enormous structural shift has yet to play out fully.

Two-thirds of town centre experts think there is still 20% to 40% too much retail space, according to a survey by retail body Revo and real estate consultancy Lambert Smith Hampton.

In Aberdeen's boom years of North Sea oil, from the late 1970s through to the early 2010s, it didn't have to work hard to fill its hotels and restaurants and get the cash tills ringing because of the wealth the energy industry brought to the city.

I remember weekends with friends as a teenager, spending whatever cash we had in the fashion chains and shoe shops. Later came the pubs and bar-hopping, and trips to the art deco Capitol Theatre (now office space), where I saw Duran Duran.

But by the end of 2022, Union Street had hit rock bottom. Aberdonians had long deserted the High Street for a shopping centre, which opened in 2009 next to the railway station.

"They'd fallen out of love with their city," says Bob Keiller, head of Our Union Street coalition, a community-led group formed after an emergency summit to reverse the decline.

The first thing it did was listen to what residents wanted, receiving thousands of submissions. Restoring a bit of civic pride and filling empty units became the focus.

It's been a co-ordinated effort, involving letting agents, the council, landlords, businesses and a big team of volunteers, led by Keiller, a former FTSE 100 CEO, who isn't getting paid. He has a map on his office wall, detailing the status of every property, unit by unit, with colour-coded Post-it notes.

They audited the street, tracking down absent landlords, and logging what was empty and why. Volunteers offered to give the empty properties a clean and a lick of paint, to make them more attractive to let. They also spruced up the streets, picking litter, weeding, and painting bins. A small team still goes out once a fortnight.

Bob Keiller heads a community-led group set up to help revive Aberdeen's Union Street

There's a website where all the available properties are collated; crucially, all of the financial incentives are also listed. These can include council fit-out grants (where a new business gets money to decorate premises), and a two-year discount from business rates.

In 2023, 57 of the 198 units on Union Street were empty. As of today, 33 have been filled, with a further 14 in the process of being let.

But it can feel like a game of whack-a-mole because since the initiative started, another 20 units have become vacant. "The key thing is, we're filling quicker than emptying," Keiller insists.

Keiller's operations director, David McKeith, describes it as putting an arm round the shoulder of those they can help and being a "wasp in the ear" of people who just say no.

At one end of the office, a small, young team produces social media content, linking visitors and residents to a new Our Aberdeen app, showing what the city can offer.

For Keiller, the "small wins" - like tackling graffiti - are needed to help change public perception.

"We've realised the psychology is not to do it once but be prepared to do it two or three times before people that do these things eventually say, 'there's no point in putting it there because somebody's going to paint over it.'"

Attention is also turning to the often-forgotten upper floors of buildings along the once-iconic Union Street. In a separate initiative run by the business group Aberdeen Inspired, experts are getting inside those vacant high-up spaces to identify the barriers to bringing them back into use.

Aberdeen sought the help of a High Street troubleshooter, Iain Nicholson, who's worked with dozens of towns and cities through his Vacant Shops Academy.

Out of more than 1,000 empty units he helped audit nationally, on average more than half didn't have a letting agent actively marketing the property. He says some councils don't even know who the landlords are.

Empty shops, he says, won't all be filled by the market alone. Towns and cities need to encourage a broader range of occupiers, from arts and culture to leisure, health and well-being.

He says what's striking about Aberdeen is its joined-up approach, and the fact people "rolled up their sleeves" and overcame differences. "That's not something you can yet say about everywhere."

Every town and city faces its own mix of challenges.

Nicholson also helped Crewe in Cheshire. As a smaller place than Aberdeen, it is focusing on arts, culture, and community, with a new women's centre opened this month.

And English councils are being encouraged to take a more interventionist role.

They can now force an empty commercial unit that's been vacant for at least a year to be offered to tenants through a High Street Rental Auction, even if the landlord doesn't want to let it.

A noodle bar (left) is one of the new businesses in the area. Parts of the city centre have been undergoing construction work (right)

It's early days but the mere prospect of losing control of a property is delivering some results. Bassetlaw Council in Nottinghamshire, an early adopter of the scheme, says more than 30 shops across the district have been let or are currently under offer as a result of the policy.

But filling empty units is only part of the challenge. The bigger question is what town and city centres are actually for.

Changing habits mean we won't need so many shops. Instead, it's argued, town centres will need more people living and working in them, with new homes and office space alongside services that can't be done online. With an ageing population, for example, bringing NHS health centres into the heart of a town could be a big opportunity.

In Aberdeen, the building that used to house BHS - a department store shuttered in 2016 - is being converted into an entrance plaza for the new food and drink market.

The physical appearance of Union Street is changing too. Like many Victorian shopping thoroughfares, it was built with a wide road (probably to give space to horses and carriages). Now parts of the pavements are being widened to make it a more pleasant place to spend time, though many Aberdonians are weary of the disruption, and of new traffic restrictions.

Money is a big barrier. Aberdeen's new indoor food and drink market is costing Â£40m, funded by the council and the UK Levelling Up Fund. In other places, regeneration schemes can struggle because the cost of redevelopment outweighs the likely financial return, making support from the public sector essential. And the economics are becoming more challenging.

"Higher interest rates, rising costs of construction and other issues are impacting the viability and delivery of new housing and big redevelopment projects," says Steve Norris, a director at Lambert Smith Hampton. He says that big projects usually require cash from both the public and private sectors.

He wants to see "more creative ways of funding".

Part of Burnham's plan is to give local leaders more freedom to invest in town centres and drive regeneration, building on the approach he has championed in Greater Manchester. But bigger projects take years.

Volunteers have played a role in the redevelopment of Aberdeen's Union Street

Making it easier and cheaper to open and run a High Street business is one of the more immediate solutions being called for, with business rates a particular concern.

Burnham has promised further rates reform. All eyes are on this month's Budget to see how far he will go. All eyes are on this month's Budget to see how far he will go in England. Business rates are devolved in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

The chief executive of Next, Simon Wolfson, argues that the bigger challenge is not business rates, but planning reform.

"The most important thing that the government can do for high streets is let them develop," says Wolfson, who's also a Conservative peer.

Back in Aberdeen, there are some signs of traction.

Lauren Reid and her mother Rose moved their fashion boutique to Union Street in 2024. A council grant helped with the refurbishment after they decided to make the leap.

Can they make the sums add up? "We're getting there," she says.

She feels the same about the future of her street. "I always say Rome wasn't built in a day."

Lauren Reid and her mother Rose moved their fashion boutique to Union Street with the help of a council grant

Right now the area feels like a construction site - but another gaping hole will soon be filled. Robert Keane is a local property developer who in 2023 bought the Trinity shopping centre, once home to Debenhams. The space is now set to be re-occupied by a national leisure brand. A notoriously tiring stairway leading to the Trinity from the train station is also being replaced by an escalator.

"We saw a real good opportunity, because it was dying," he says.

He reckons he can make a go of it, cutting the cost of parking and overheads, resetting rents and being more nimble than previous far-flung owners.

It was a bold move given Lambert Smith Hampton suggests that more than one in 10 UK shopping centres is ripe for demolition.

But he's betting the dynamics of Aberdeen are finally changing.

"I think by 2027 it should look and feel like a different city."

That is certainly the hope. Yet walking along Union Street today, past the boarded-up shopfronts and roadworks, it can still feel like a city caught between its past and whatever comes next.
