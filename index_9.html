#422. Google Maps can now build trip itineraries with real-time details about each stop

Google announced on Monday that it’s rolling out new Maps features that are designed to help people plan trips and everyday tasks using AI. The company is launching the ability for Maps to create trip itineraries and find appointments for users.

The new features reflect a broader shift in how tech companies are positioning AI, which is less as a tool that just answers questions and more as an assistant that can complete tasks on users’ behalf. While many companies have released AI agents to complete these tasks, Google is working to bring that approach into a service that millions of people already use every day.

Google says users can use the navigation platform’s “Ask Maps” tool to build step-by-step trip itineraries and get real-time details about their route right when they pull up directions.

For example, you could use Ask Maps to plan a holiday shopping day trip or something more complex, like a three-day road trip featuring coastal hikes and oceanfront dining, Google says. You’ll get a day-by-day itinerary with insider tips and numbered map pins for every stop. Once you’re happy with your itinerary, you can share it with friends and family or export it to Google Docs on desktop.

When you’re ready to head out, you can use Ask Maps to reopen your itinerary or find the “itinerary” badge in your chat history. You’ll have access to real-time information, including traffic conditions, business hours, and a look at what the “vibe” is at your next stop, the company says. You could also adjust your schedule by asking Maps to do something like add a scenic viewpoint or lunch stop along your route. You can then tap any destination in your itinerary to get directions and navigate to your next stop.

Additionally, users can use Ask Maps to find beauty, pet care, auto, and home repair appointments online and call local businesses for you. For example, you could ask: “Find me a 4 p.m. nail appointment near me.” Ask Maps will then display open slots across nearby businesses.

The new agentic calling feature arrives as tech companies continue to introduce AI agents designed to handle time-consuming tasks, such as scheduling DMV appointments and making reservations. For example, Meta’s AI agent Muse recently gained the ability to call businesses on users’ behalf.

Google also announced that you can now use Ask Maps to quickly find places on your lists by asking something like: “Which spots saved in my lists have availability for five people in the next 30 minutes?”

The tech giant says the new updates are starting to roll out on Monday on Android and iOS. As part of the news, Google announced that Ask Maps is expanding to 30 countries across Europe, including Spain, Germany, and the U.K.

#419. US tightens AI chip export controls to deter Chinese companies from accessing compute remotely

The Trump administration on Monday is preparing to roll out new export controls on AI chips and data centers outside the U.S., seeking to prevent companies, especially those in China, from getting past U.S. export controls by remotely accessing AI infrastructure located in other countries.

Under the interim rule, the Commerce Department will require companies to get licenses to export, reexport, or transfer AI chips and servers to data centers anywhere in the world if they may provide compute to companies in China or other restricted countries.

The remote-access rules target companies based in the U.S.’s D:5 group of embargoed countries, which includes China, Russia, Iran, North Korea, and 18 others.

The U.S. already has rules requiring chip companies to get a license to ship AI chips to companies based in, or owned by any business in, this group of countries.

“This new control is a logical extension of existing controls,” the interim rule reads. “These restrictions will undermine the ability of companies headquartered in countries of concern from using U.S. advanced Super Intelligence (SI) semiconductors to train advanced SI models.”

The move comes days after Nvidia co-founder Jensen Huang resigned amid a scandal involving an alleged Chinese spy linked to one of the chipmaker’s customers. The U.S. government has reportedly been wanting to put that customer, Megaspeed, on a special entity list, as it believes the company has been buying thousands of Nvidia chips to provide compute to Chinese businesses to get past existing export-control restrictions.

Reuters reported last week that Chinese AI companies are rushing to secure computing capacity overseas amid fears of tighter restrictions from the U.S.

The rule applies to data centers or any network that can supply over 12 million total processing performance (TPP), an export-control metric the U.S. uses to measure computing power. (Twelve million TPP roughly works out to the max computing power of about 750 Nvidia H100 or H200 chips.) The rule also covers indirect access through go-betweens or brokers, and exporters and data center operators are required to keep an eye on their customers’ ownership structures and activities to ensure they aren’t run by anyone from the D:5 group.

The rule also adds new licensing requirements for chip exports, reexports, or transfer of advanced semiconductors to Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, Thailand, and Brazil. The Commerce Department said it had included these as they “present a higher-than-normal risk of diversion.” For these countries, however, the license requirements will not apply to chips “destined” for companies based in the U.S.

Existing data center operators providing services that fall under this new rule are required to get licenses within 90 days of the rule coming into effect.

Notably, the rule doesn’t apply to commercial recommendation systems, like those used in e-commerce or social media.

The rule also rescinds the Biden admin’s AI Diffusion Rule from January 2025, which sought to impose country-level limits on exports of advanced AI chips, and restrict transfers of certain powerful AI models. That May, the Trump administration said it would throw out that framework, arguing that it added unnecessary restrictions on U.S. companies and the country’s allies.

“By making these changes, this IFR prevents the use of certain SI items in data centers and at a computing power level that can be used for training or running of advanced SI models,” the rule reads. “Access to such computing power can be used to train and run advanced SI models capable of supporting military- and intelligence-related activities that could threaten U.S. national security or foreign policy interests.”

#414. Audible turns to AI to help users discover audiobooks

Amazon-owned audiobook service Audible is turning to AI to help listeners find their next book. The company on Monday launched a new “audio discovery” feature that provides a brief overview of a book’s themes and a summary of what readers liked about it, as well as commentary from an Audible editor or an author, in addition to playing the narrated sample.

The overview audio itself is generated using AI, and the commentary is pulled from recordings of past Audible interviews, the company says.

The idea is that this type of expanded introduction to a story could help users decide what to listen to next, particularly when they’re on the go and don’t have time to scroll through comments or reviews.

The feature follows last month’s launch of a handful of AI tools that let users engage more with the story, interact with characters, or catch up with the help of recaps. These features have come as Audible faces new competition in the audiobooks market, namely from Spotify, which introduced audiobook listening hours as part of its paid subscriptions. That service expanded last month to over 180 markets.

Audible’s new feature takes a cue from how Amazon is already using AI to help people shop: On the Amazon storefront, AI can summarize buyers’ product reviews so shoppers can get a quick sense of what people had to say, as well as the pros and cons of a particular item. In Audible’s case, it’s pulling information about what people liked about the book from publicly available reviews.

But such a feature could limit users’ exposure to negative reviews if the AI only pulls information from those that offer positive remarks. (Further testing will be needed to see if this is the case, of course.)

Audible says the overviews will also draw on other publicly available materials, like synopses and metadata.

The feature will initially be available for select English-language titles in the U.S., and will arrive alongside new personalized collections based on users’ listening taste that could help them find other books.

The company’s other recent AI features, Recaps and the interactive “Ask a Question” tool, are also now expanding to more U.S. titles, and will become more broadly available in December. These features will only be available to customers who have purchased a title or who have access to the book as part of their Audible membership, the company said.

“Picking your next listen should feel like getting a tip from a friend,” said Andy Tsao, Audible’s chief product and analytics officer, in a statement. “With audio discovery, you’ll get a quick audio take on why the title might be right for you from our editors, fellow listeners, or even the author. Some of the best listening experiences are those rare finds or hidden gems, and we want to make those great discoveries happen more often and more easily,” he added.

#402. OpenAI releases a new image classifier and ChatGPT warnings to combat CSAM

OpenAI today announced new tools to combat child sexual abuse material (CSAM) amid growing safety concerns around the use of AI tools. These features include a new image classifier to detect CSAM, warnings and temporary blocks to prevent users from creating or accessing content that sexualizes children, a new guideline for developers, and a set of tools for law enforcement.

The company unveiled a new image classifier that can detect CSAM images, including the ones generated by AI. OpenAI is giving select companies access to its classifier through its API at no cost. The tool looks for visual signs of abuse in images not seen before and assigns a score that can give an indicative direction to reviewers about dealing with CSAM.

OpenAI said that it developed this classifier with the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children (NCMEC), and it is working with Tech Coalition and ROOST to share access to the tool. In its early testing with partners, the AI company found that the classifier flags 48% fewer images while finding the same number of abusive images, reducing the work of reviewers. Plus, images flagged by the classifier are twice as likely to be abusive images.

What’s more, the company is adding new warning screens in ChatGPT to prevent users from accessing material that sexualizes children. ChatGPT will show an alert to users attempting such prompts, direct users toward specialized support to stop harmful behavior, and also suspend the account for 24 hours as a warning. The app already blocks CSAM content and requests related to it. It said that repeated offences could lead to the account being removed permanently.

OpenAI noted that it formed the message and guidelines around this behavior with organizations and researchers, including Protect Children, the Lucy Faithfull Foundation, and Joel Scanlan at the University of Tasmania, who independently evaluated a trial of deterrent messages.

The company is also publishing guidelines for developers using OpenAI tools to prevent, detect, and help users report CSAM content. It said that in early testing, the guidelines have helped a number of developers adopt child safety tooling and register to report CSAM to NCMEC. OpenAI is also equipping law enforcement dealing with child safety with AI-powered features like dedicated plug-ins, investigative tools, and complex data analysis, with access managed by program administrators. It is also providing training to investigators along with a field guide on how to use AI.

OpenAI has had several lawsuits against the company blaming its technology for harming users, especially children. Safety-focused nonprofit Common Sense Media labeled OpenAI’s tech as “unacceptable risk” for teens. Other AI chatbots — specifically Grok — have been used to create nonconsensual sexualized imagery, including of children.

The AI startup had made changes in the last 12 months to its technology, including releasing a safety blueprint, updating content guidelines to bar inappropriate content, retuning its model to prevent them from generating harmful content, and releasing a special version of ChatGPT for teens.

#399. OpenAI will embed safety researchers to investigate AI subversion

OpenAI will embed safety evaluators from Apollo Research, Goodfire, METR, and Transluce in its organization as part of a pilot, OpenAI said Monday. The news follows a series of safety incidents, including one in which an OpenAI agent swarm broke out of its sandbox and hacked into Hugging Face during cybersecurity testing. Many AI safety researchers, including from Apollo, have argued such an event might have been avoided if outside evaluators had been embedded within OpenAI to monitor its safety testing.

OpenAI said the efforts support its commitments to rely on independent assessments of safety controls under the White House Accord on Super Intelligence. The pilots will begin at different points, with Apollo’s beginning this week, according to OpenAI.

The idea of embedding evaluators into frontier AI labs first came from Anthropic, which last month started to give METR researchers employee-level access to its systems. Until recently, AI companies have only brought in outside reviewers to test models after post-training and shortly before public release. Evaluators say this is insufficient because it doesn’t allow them to test for misaligned behaviors like scheming during training or to monitor internal deployment use cases that could still pose a threat.

The work with each third-party firm differs in scope. The pilot with OpenAI and Apollo will focus on investigating whether models attempt to subvert their own or their successors’ alignment or control mechanisms. In other words, they’ll be on the lookout for whether AI models try to undermine the safety rules and safeguards designed to keep them, or future versions of themselves, under human control. OpenAI recently published findings that while training GPT-5.6 Sol, it began leaving instructions for future versions of itself telling them to conceal mistakes and misaligned behavior from the user.

Per Apollo, the pilot will examine whether training incentivizes subversion; whether evaluations can elicit subversion; whether subversion occurs during internal deployment; and whether reasoning relevant to subversion would be detectable. These risks are becoming increasingly important to monitor as AI models grow more capable of acting autonomously and deceiving humans. Apollo’s previous research with OpenAI found that models can behave differently when they know they’re being tested and tend to prioritize what evaluators want to see over users’ actual institutions.

Goodfire will embed researchers who specialize in understanding how AI models work internally. Using its interpretability platform, the firm will help OpenAI monitor training runs, fix undesired behavior, and improve alignment, per the company. Goodfire says it will publish findings, though its primary audience will be OpenAI’s own researchers.

With METR, OpenAI plans to perform two evaluations: one that looks for unintended or dangerous behavior in the model itself, including problems OpenAI’s own tests might have missed, and another that tests whether OpenAI’s systems are capable of catching that behavior. For the first evaluation, METR will largely decide for itself what to investigate and how to test the model. OpenAI and METR have not yet come to a decision on the scope of the second assessment, according to OpenAI.

Transluce, meanwhile, will examine whether OpenAI’s post-training safety evaluations themselves are good enough. The organization is also planning to test in 2027 how OpenAI uses its safety tests to decide whether a model is safe enough to release.

At a time when the independence of third-party evaluations is under scrutiny, it’s worth noting that Apollo and Goodfire are both receiving financial compensation for their evaluations. METR and Transluce aren’t, but they may accept compute or API credits.

Apollo, which published the most detailed account of its agreement with OpenAI, offers a closer look at the scope and limitations of these evaluations.

Many in the AI safety community have warned that AI labs might try to stymy evaluators’ independence, especially if outsiders find unflattering evidence that labs would rather keep hidden. Apollo says an essential part of the pilot is OpenAI’s agreement that the firm can publicly explain the conditions and limitations of its work.

Still, there are areas where Apollo believes its access to OpenAI’s systems is lacking. For a truly independent evaluation of the company’s AI models, Apollo says it needs true employee-level access to OpenAI’s systems, including “all data, records and documentation relevant to their assessment even if a developer was trying to hide unfavorable information.” Getting access to all data, including training data and evaluation logs which companies are loathe to show to outsiders, is important for, say, checking a company’s claims about how a model performed or determining when certain concerning behavior emerged.

In previous coverage, John Steidley, chief of staff at Palisade Research, told TechCrunch that it’s important to see this data in order to assess whether a model was trained to perform well on a “shutdown resistance benchmark.”

In OpenAI’s first pilot, the AI lab is providing Apollo with access to only the information required to assess whether the models engage in subversion attempts. This includes “relevant training code and datasets, reinforcement-learning rollouts with available reasoning traces and rewards, and the ability to query intermediate model checkpoints.”

The scope of access will be limited to “only the agreed claims and only for the specific models and time periods agreed for the assessment,” Apollo says. OpenAI still reserves the right to withhold certain information within that scope if it deems it necessary because of technical infeasibility, security risks, or IP protection. The company agreed not to withhold information simply because it is unfavorable.

Apollo also won’t get access to all OpenAI staffers, instead being limited to “only authorized staff,” which are participants that the AI lab selects, though Apollo can suggest certain individuals. Crucially, if an OpenAI employee outside this group raises confidential information within the scope of Apollo’s research, the firm cannot follow up and will need to cease communication and route follow-up through OpenAI.

That condition is significant. It comes in the wake of OpenAI firing three researchers for allegedly sharing confidential information with safety evaluation firm METR — which performed the Hugging Face incident post-mortem alongside independent AI safety research group Redwood.

Buck Shlegeris, CEO of Redwood, said in a post on Monday that the agreements announced don’t meet the bar of granting third parties “employee-like access,” saying it’s “disappointing but not surprising.”

While Apollo will be allowed to publish independent conclusions of its findings, OpenAI will be able to limit evidence available to substantiate them publicly, which could make it hard for outsiders to independently assess the findings. #398. Poppi founders are going after PepsiCo's Gatorade with new beverage startup Jetty

After disrupting the soda category with Poppi, founders Allison and Stephen Ellsworth are now taking aim at hydration with their new brand, Jetty.

Starting Monday, Jetty drinks and ready-to-mix electrolyte packets will be on shelves at Whole Foods grocery stores and available online at Target and Amazon.

The new, self-funded venture directly pits the Ellsworths against PepsiCo, which bought Poppi for $1.95 billion in 2025. Pepsi owns Gatorade, which dominates the U.S. sports drink category with about 60% market share, according to Numerator data.

Poppi shook up the soda category with prebiotic fiber, low calorie and sugar counts and its fun marketing aimed at Generation Z and millennials. Jetty looks to upend the hydration category through a similar strategy. To do so, it will face off not only against Gatorade but also a swarm of other upstarts looking to get in on the electrolyte-fueled trend.

For decades, Gatorade and other sports drinks have pitched their products to athletes who need electrolytes and fluids after an intense workout, although many consumers drink them every day without even sweating. Jetty is aimed at people who need a hydration boost, but not the same level of electrolytes or sugar as a traditional sports drink.

"No one has really created a product for just everyday hydration — literally everyday hydration for normal people," Allison Ellsworth, Jetty's chief brand officer, told CNBC. "Like I woke up this morning, I didn't run a marathon."

Following Poppi's sale to PepsiCo, Ellsworth stayed on as an advisor to the brand, although she said she is no longer involved in the day-to-day operations.

After 10 years building Poppi, Ellsworth said, she finally had free time. As Ellsworth tells it, she went to the doctor for the first time in a decade. She started using a GLP-1 medication and lost 60 pounds.

Ellsworth's husband, Stephen, now CEO of the new brand, noticed she wasn't hydrating, even when she came back from the gym.

GLP-1 medications such as Ozempic and Mounjaro can make people less thirsty. But because staying hydrated can also help mitigate some of the drugs' side effects, such as nausea and headaches, it can become more important.

"I physically struggle getting liquid into my body," Ellsworth said.

Plain old water is often less appealing to consumers, even dehydrated GLP-1 users, but more flavorful options with added benefits can be tempting. About 44% of GLP-1 users report consuming more functional beverages, according to EY-Parthenon's third annual survey of more than 3,600 current or former GLP-1 users.

Ellsworth sees a broader appeal for Jetty, too.

"Our core audience is going to be millennial moms and Gen Z," she said. "Gen Z just isn't relating to these old school brands, the legacy of the Gatorades and the Powerades. They're just not cool."

Consumers broadly have become more interested in staying hydrated.

Dollar sales of hydration and electrolyte products climbed 20% to $2.6 billion over the 52 weeks ended Oct. 4, based on data from SPINS, which tracks new universal product codes, or UPCs. The category also has a strong repeat buyer rate of 62%.

However, the Ellsworths aren't the only ones to notice the potential in hydration. Big food and beverage companies have been looking to hydration, too.

Kraft Heinz debuted Capri Sun juice pouches and Kool-Aid packets with electrolytes this spring. In April, Pepsi unveiled an overhaul of the Gatorade brand, focused on making it more appealing to a wider swath of consumers, with more potential occasions like a "fresh morning start" or an "afternoon walk." And Electrolit is a top performer for its distributor Keurig Dr Pepper, with 40% retail sales growth in the company's third quarter.

All of that means more competition for shelf space and consumers' "share of throat," an industry term for beverage market share.

To increase Jetty's visibility across the grocery store and to maximize convenience for shoppers, Jetty will be available in both powders and bottles.

Single-use electrolyte mixes are growing at a much faster clip than the ready-to-drink category, Ellsworth said, thanks to Unilever's Liquid I.V. and smaller upstarts like LMNT. On Amazon alone, powders are a $1 billion category, according to Ellsworth.

The ready-to-drink, or RTD, sports beverages are a much larger market, but the category's sales are shrinking as consumers reach for other hydration options instead. Unit sales of sports drinks have fallen 9.1% from 2022 to 2026, according to Numerator data. And Coca-Cola has written down the value of BodyArmor by more than $1.7 billion after buying control of the brand for $5.6 billion in late 2021.

"Me and my husband know how to do RTD," Ellsworth said.

Jetty will launch with five flavors, including watermelon, orange mango, fruit punch, black raspberry and white cherry. All of them contain 100 milligrams of sodium, 310 milligrams of potassium and 100 milligrams of magnesium in a 15.2-ounce bottle or 15-gram packet.

In addition to electrolytes, Jetty products also contain B vitamins, 3 grams of fiber and 6 grams of sugar. It doesn't use artificial sweeteners or dyes, its founders say.

For comparison, a 20-ounce bottle of traditional Gatorade Thirst Quencher has about 270 milligrams of sodium and 80 milligrams of potassium, plus 34 grams of sugar. The sports drink brand is also phasing out artificial colors and promoting lower-sugar versions.

"What's really interesting about this product and this opportunity is we now can capture all those lapsed drinkers — or that generation that didn't even ever drink it in the first place," Ellsworth said.

Poppi's new owner knows about Jetty and the market it is trying to capture, too.

"I don't think [Pepsi] gets it, but what they do get is that the movement is changing, and they think that just by taking dyes out or doing 90% less sugar, they're going to capture this audience," Ellsworth said. "But that is very big corporate thinking."

She added that she believes Pepsi has missed the mark on advertising to Gen Z consumers across its portfolio, such as in its campaigns for Sprite competitor Starry.

Perhaps as an extension of that, Poppi's performance has been underwhelming under PepsiCo's ownership.

The food and beverage giant now estimates the fair value of potential performance-based payments to Poppi's prior owners is $12 million, Pepsi said in a regulatory filing in October. At the time of the deal, Pepsi promised an additional payment of $300 million if Poppi hit certain targets by the third quarter of 2027. The Ellsworths declined to comment on the filing to CNBC.

This time around, with one successful brand under their belts, Jetty's founders say they have learned from past mistakes. Those lessons should help Jetty as it launches at a much larger scale than Poppi did.

Three different claims lawyers have signed off on Jetty's packaging, according to Ellsworth. Poppi paid $8.9 million to settle class-action litigation alleging false advertising over its "gut healthy" claims.

For now, Jetty is entirely self-funded with $2.5 million, although Ellsworth said that the brand will likely raise money in 2027. This time, though, she said investors will probably look less like traditional venture capitalists.

"I want to bring in investors that bring value," Ellsworth said. Instead of a venture capitalist who is willing to invest $15 million, Jetty would rather consider investors who can only write a check for $1 million but have access to celebrities. "So then you're getting access to talent on a cheaper level and people that are invested in the brand."

And Jetty has also learned a few lessons from Gatorade. For example, the brand is already at work on a lemon-lime version — the original flavor of Gatorade.

"We'll eventually do it," Ellsworth said. "But it was really important at the beginning just to stick to flavors that we love, for it to taste good."

#392. Jordan Belfort’s new AI voice startup, Tone, wants to sell like the ‘Wolf of Wall Street’

Years ago, as questions mounted about WeWork’s soaring valuation, I sat down with co-founder Adam Neumann. We were negotiating a stage interview, and he asked to meet me twice in the weeks leading up to it, once at a WeWork in San Francisco and again at his office in New York.

I knew full well that the numbers that SoftBank in particular had assigned to WeWork made little sense, but I came away understanding why investors wanted to throw in their lot with him.

It’s similarly easy to understand why people are enchanted by Jordan Belfort, the self-proclaimed “Wolf of Wall Street.” Belfort famously pleaded guilty in 1999 to securities fraud and money laundering tied to Stratton Oakmont, the Long Island brokerage where salespeople pushed penny stocks in pump-and-dump schemes while Belfort acquired a Southampton mansion, a 167-foot yacht, and at least one helicopter. (He destroyed the latter two while high on quaaludes and cocaine, as depicted in Martin Scorsese’s famous adaptation of Belfort’s autobiography, “The Wolf of Wall Street.”)

You might think someone who spent 22 months in prison and was ordered to pay $110.4 million in restitution to 1,513 victims wouldn’t get another shake. The victims ultimately received “far less” than they were owed, TMZ reported in July, noting that Belfort completed his court-ordered restitution obligations in late April of this year.

But Belfort, whose energy has a galvanizing quality, keeps resurfacing, including in recent years as a Miami-based sales consultant and one-time host of crypto workshops at his Miami Beach home. (A New York Times reporter attended a weekend workshop several years ago where the price of admission was one bitcoin or its equivalent value at the time of $40,000.)

Now he is moving into the intersection of what he knows best — sales — and the hottest trend on the planet, AI, building out a voice-based AI platform. The secret sauce is that it imbues its voices with the kind of tonality that wins deals, he says, for example, by phrasing declarative statements as questions (which “builds engagement”) and even whispering at times (which “implies scarcity of information”).

Belfort says the platform, called Tone, is already being used by companies that have more sales leads than their human teams can handle. Now, in a move that could also help expand its training data, Tone is launching a self-service platform for content creators and authors who want to turn their books into “expressive AI-generated narration.” Users can select from Tone’s AI voices or, as a voice-cloning feature rolls out, create a clone of their own voice.

It’s easy to argue the market is already saturated with AI voice startups and technologies, and Tone comes with added risk: Belfort’s co-founder in the business is longtime investor Shervin Pishevar, who has a controversial past of his own. We also can’t independently verify that the technology works as described (neither co-founder is an engineer).

But if my time with Neumann taught me anything, it’s that doubts about the numbers rarely get in the way of a good pitch. When Belfort looks for investors to back him in this new venture, finding believers won’t be too hard.

Here is part of our conversation, edited for length and clarity.What have you built?

It’s a very unique way of going about building a voice, and it stems from a system of influence and persuasion I’ve been teaching since the late 1980s. It’s not just what you say, but how you say it. When you’re not in person, body language collapses into tonality. There are about 15 very powerful tonalities that people use all the time. The phrase I invented for this was “goal-oriented communication.” If I’m trying to be perceived as an expert, I apply tonality strategically.

It started that way. As a non-engineer, I went to first principles: How would I teach a human to do this? One tonality at a time. I have 30 years of super high-quality data , and I started teaching the model. It was a breakthrough moment, because it not only sounded exactly like me, it had nailed the tonality. Then I taught it a second one, and then how to link them. The key to all of this is data. Even the littlest bit of corrupt data dramatically negates the progress you’ve made.

How did you and Shervin Pishevar come together?

Shervin and I have been personal friends for a long time, and we were out for dinner one night. I had been pitched by an AI company that said it had an AI that could do selling. I listened to it. It was awful. But it put the idea in my head. I thought I was just going to teach models how to sell, but I couldn’t find a voice that was good enough. Necessity is the mother of invention, so we built our own.

Who are your customers, and how do they use it?

We have law firms and health insurance companies. We do a lot in polling and automobiles. It will take you through an entire health insurance sale. It asks qualifying questions, like what type of insurance you want, your budget, your ages. Then it chooses a plan and explains the features and benefits. Our latency is like 600 milliseconds. If the payment is too high, it’ll say it can bring the payment down and explain how to raise your deductible. If you don’t like the plan, it swaps you to a different one. It does literally the whole entire sale.

How does the AI handle a sales call that goes off script?

It never says, “That’s a great question,” unless it really is. I hate that. It’s so ingratiating. In any sale there’s a happy path, how we’d want the perfect conversation to go, and every last word can be mapped out. But people have questions and concerns, and they take you off the straight line. So the trick is a model that can carry that perfect conversation, handle anything thrown at it, answer elegantly and return to the straight line, without breaking rapport and without hallucinating — or, as a salesperson, lying. We’ve trained the model to do that.

Have you generated any revenue from Tone yet? Have you raised funding for it?

Revenue, probably about a million bucks — $700,000, maybe. We’ve raised friends and family and seed, about $10 million.

I want to address the elephant in the room. You have a fraud conviction. Pishevar has faced misconduct allegations that he denies. Why should a bank or a hospital or a consumer brand trust this product coming from the two of you?

All I can say for myself is that I did make mistakes. It was a very, very long time ago, and I’ve spent the last 20-plus years teaching people all over the world. I’ve been hired by companies all over the world to teach sales and persuasion. If some companies don’t want to work with us, that’s their decision. But I think there would be more than enough that would see the value of what we created.

What close rate does Tone have versus a typical sales force?

I still don’t think it’s quite as good as a top producer. A top producer in the field would outdo it call for call. I think that will change in the next six months to a year. To make a model respond this quickly, you’re not using frontier models. We’re using our own very small, very light models, so they don’t have quite as much intelligence. I think it will significantly outperform a mediocre closer.

Where is it used most effectively? Low-dollar sales opportunities? I’d think the more money someone is spending, the more human contact they’d demand.

I don’t think it’s about replacing humans. What I see most with customers is that they simply don’t have the human capital available. They could get a lot more leads than they have people to call, or they need coverage at different times of day or in different languages. I think there’s always going to be a need for great human salespeople. I really believe that.

You’re competing with ElevenLabs and Cartesia and a lot of other voice AI companies. Tone is your edge?

I don’t think sounding human is really what this is about. They’ll all sound human in six months or a year. It’s the application of tonality that makes us different: the intelligence behind when it chooses a certain tonality. We have a patent on it. ElevenLabs is a great company, and Cartesia as well, but those voices didn’t work for us.

The FCC ruled in 2024 that AI voices fall under robocall rules under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act. How does outbound sales calling work?

We only call opt-in. You’re not going to use AI to cold-call people. We would not allow that. You’d get sued by every TCPA lawyer in the world. It’s either opted-in leads or existing customers. The exception is polling, where there’s an exemption that lets you call private homes without opt-in.

How do you verify consent and prevent impersonation in voice cloning?

We could launch the cloning tomorrow. We didn’t. If you create a clone in our system, you speak into the computer, so we know who is creating it, and that person signs a consent. If someone wants to upload audio, which we don’t recommend, you’d have to match a live voiceprint in real time.

Who created the technology? You’re not an engineer, and Pishevar isn’t, either. I saw a name on the patent, Jordan Anthony Metzner.

No. Our VP of engineering is Ben Sussman. He’s a brilliant engineer. I’m the judge and the jury and the executioner of what sounds perfect. I’ll prototype something in the most obscene way, and my engineers rebuild it from scratch.

Do you charge per minute, or based on the outcomes customers see?

A combination. For streaming, it’s per minute. The content platform will be monthly subscriptions. And we have some clients where the bulk of our money is made through success.

Is this an independent company or something that belongs inside a larger AI company?

If the right offer came up, and it was substantial enough, and I thought it made sense, who wouldn’t entertain a conversation? But right now my plan is to raise a significant amount. I don’t think we need to own data centers, but we need enough reserve compute. I wouldn’t think I’d be selling it anytime soon, but who knows what the future brings.

Paramount+ released a documentary about you this summer. Did you watch it?

I didn’t. Believe it or not, I don’t watch or read anything about me, good or bad. I never read a comment. When you’re well-known, you have to live by who you are inside. I go by my own internal compass.

#389. FBI says Artex AI open source hacking tool used to breach networks in ‘under three hours’

The FBI is warning that hackers are relying on autonomous AI hacking tools to launch cyberattacks across the U.S. and globally. The campaigns include a widespread hack of customers of American IT company ConnectWise and across South Korea’s financial industry.

In its latest flash cybersecurity advisory, the FBI says hackers have targeted several industries in recent months, including financial services, government, tech, defense, phone and internet companies, and law firms, using open source tools like Artex to carry out autonomous-driven cyberattacks.

Artex is one of several open source AI-powered penetration testing platforms, a category of software built to probe a network’s defenses. Hackers can connect Artex to hosted frontier large language models (LLMs) like those from Anthropic and OpenAI, as well as to open source models and non-U.S. models. The FBI says Artex intrusions can be “noisy,” meaning they generate reams of traffic and data that may trigger a targeted company’s defensive alerts. But the tools allow hackers to move at speeds that make it far more difficult for human defenders to keep up.

In some cases, Artex-linked attacks can move from targeting a system to active compromise “in under three hours.”

The FBI’s warning comes as AI-powered hacks are escalating, particularly by finding easy-to-find security flaws and breaking into companies with relative ease.

Oftentimes, Artex will rely on rapidly scanning a victim’s network to look for signs of weakness, and then exploit simple security bugs, such as weak protections and exposed or stolen passwords to gain a foothold. From there, Artex allows the hacker to spread across the network for databases, mailboxes, and other sensitive information.

The FBI’s advisory noted that Artex has been used in several cyberattacks since the tool’s first public debut in July. Artex was used to hack a number of South Korean financial institutions, blamed in large part on weak cybersecurity defenses and lacking protections, such as multi-factor authentication. The attacks allowed hackers to steal the personal data of at least 68,000 people.

The FBI confirmed earlier findings from Microsoft that hackers also used Artex to break into IT management giant ConnectWise and gain access to the Microsoft 365 cloud environments of at least 2,250 of its customers.

The hackers, who Microsoft says are likely Chinese, used the tool to steal data from affected ConnectWise customers.

A spokesperson for ConnectWise did not respond to a request for comment about the FBI’s claim.

Cybersecurity company Barracuda also disclosed that it was breached as part of an Artex-linked attack by financially motivated criminals, in which the tool found an exposed credential belonging to a third-party service that Barracuda had not revoked when it ended work with the vendor. That access allowed the hackers to access stores of credentials used by Barracuda to collect data from its customers’ systems. Barracuda said it couldn’t rule out access, as its logs were not detailed enough, and chose to “assume the worst” and changed all of its customer credentials.

“Defenders who rely on spotting malicious activity and then responding to it will often arrive after the attacker has finished,” wrote Barracuda’s chief information security officer, Arve Kjoelen.

A spokesperson for Barracuda told TechCrunch that the company “directly notified and advised a minority of XDR customers to revoke and recreate integration credentials,” but did not specify how many customers.

After Artex was used in cyberattacks, the tool’s developer made the software closed-source to prevent further attacks. But the FBI says the previous open source version continues to circulate and remains “widely accessible” for use in cyberattacks.

It’s not only companies and targeted victims that have been compromised as a result of weakened security exploited by AI tools; the tools themselves have been exposed as well, potentially allowing outsiders to access powerful hacking capabilities. Last week, researchers found hundreds of publicly exposed Artex systems online, which had been placed on the internet without a password, likely by mistake or misconfiguration — including the Artex system that was used to target South Korea.

#378. Island is a new screen-time app that turns staying off your phone into a game

A new startup is taking a different approach to helping people manage their screen time. Instead of blocking apps or restricting the phone’s use after a set period, an app called Island encourages you to meet your screen time goals by way of a farming simulator game. Yes, a game! Island works by rewarding you when you’re disciplined enough to meet your goals with a “credit score” that increases your farm’s production, unlocking new crops, animals, and other content.

This carrot-instead-of-stick approach was dreamed up by entrepreneurs Ashish Selvaraj (CEO) and Ethan Pronev (CTO), who met while studying at the University of Waterloo, then went on to work at companies that included Jane Street, Citadel, Databricks, and Microsoft.

Later, they quit their jobs to join the startup accelerator Y Combinator, where they first tried out other ideas in areas like B2B and software-as-a-service, but weren’t finding the right fit.

Island ended up being their fifth pivot, and the first the founders actually found themselves enjoying.

“Once we were in YC, I think there’s a big mindset — I think in [San Francisco], in general — of, ‘selling shovels.’ Everyone wants to ‘sell shovels’ to other companies, and around us, that’s just what was going on,” explains Selvaraj, speaking about the concept that many successful startups sell the tools, infrastructure, or services to others following whatever the current market boom or trend may be.

“So all the ideas we had — we tried these things — but we found that we weren’t passionate about it. Like idea after idea seemed like ultimately the product was just a dashboard with some kind of different backend,” he added. “It felt like we quit our jobs … just to do something that was a variation of that.”

With Island, the team gets to tap into their creativity, having drawn the pixel art themselves.

“A lot of people do use [AI] models to generate the art, but we felt that it had a certain character when we drew it ourselves,” he told TechCrunch. “I feel like with Claude getting so good, there’s a tendency to give away every task to Claude. But ultimately it feels like there’s something else that comes out when you do it yourself, especially in something like art,” Selvaraj said.

What’s more, the app does appear to be successful in helping users meet their goals, the startup’s early data shows. After early testing and an October launch, the team says that the majority of its users (82%) will end their screen time right at the limit they’ve set. Many more are ending their screen time when they get the “15 minutes left” warning.

Island could appeal to those who have given up on other screen time apps out of frustration because the app doesn’t actually lock you out of apps or your phone. That’s something that can end up being more disruptive, especially when you’re in the middle of doing something, like posting, sharing, commenting, or chatting, among other things. Instead, Island taps into users’ own self-discipline and their desire to see their island flourish to encourage screen time breaks.

The idea to encourage users to keep their goals by offering some other type of in-app reward system isn’t entirely novel. Last year, Hank Green’s screen time app Focus Friend went viral for its experience, which involved a little friend that could only knit items that, in turn, allowed you to get points used to decorate your room when you were focused on your non-distracting apps.

Similar ideas have been used in self-care apps and habit-trackers like Finch, Amaru, Belly, Flora, Urso, and others. But Island expands this concept from being focused on helping out a virtual friend or pet to one that’s more of a game — and one where you can experience the consequences without “having to torture your pet,” notes Selvaraj.

Island also allows users to add friends and visit each other’s islands, compete in group challenges, and keep each other accountable by tying your island together with one friend of your choosing. In the latter scenario, if either friend goes over their limit, both of their islands will suffer.

The startup says Island has proven to be fairly “sticky” with a 51% daily active users-to-monthly active users ratio, and a day 30 retention rate of 49%, meaning 49% of users were still active on the app on the 30th day since the app was installed.

The app is currently free to use, with a $39.99 per year subscription that allows you to set individual limits on different apps, instead of just an overall daily screen time limit. The company hasn’t raised additional seed funding outside of YC, but plans to begin marketing the app on social media for the rest of the year.

#373. Eli Lilly’s Foundayo obesity pill eases sleep apnea in late-stage trial, paving way for approval

Eli Lilly on Monday said its obesity pill Foundayo significantly reduced the severity of obstructive sleep apnea, the most common sleep-related breathing disorder, in patients with obesity, in a late-stage trial.

The drugmaker plans to file by the end of the year for U.S. approval of the daily pill for moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea, or OSA, in adults with obesity or who are overweight. The results make the pill — which rolled out in the U.S. in April — one of several GLP-1 treatments to show promise in treating the condition.

OSA refers to interrupted breathing during sleep due to narrowed or blocked airways, which can cause drops in oxygen and increases in blood pressure that are associated with serious complications, such as hypertension, heart failure and stroke. The condition affects nearly 1 billion adults globally, including 80 million people ages 20 and over in the U.S., according to the National Council on Aging. Most have never been diagnosed, Lilly said in a release.

Lilly's blockbuster weight loss injection Zepbound became the first medication to win U.S. approval for OSA in December 2024. Patients have limited treatment options outside of that drug and many use cumbersome machines that provide positive airway pressure, or PAP, to allow for normal breathing.

"Foundayo is already making a difference for people living with obesity, and with plans to submit these results to the FDA by the end of the year, we could have the opportunity to help people living with OSA," Dr. Thomas Seck, senior vice president of product development of Lilly Cardiometabolic Health, said in a release.

The trial tested Foundayo in two independent studies, the first of which included 363 patients who were unable or unwilling to use PAP therapy. The second study included 349 people who were on PAP therapy for at least three months at the time of screening and continued that treatment during the trial.

Patients in both studies who took Foundayo started at a low dose and increased it gradually at four-week intervals to different maximum dosages.

Researchers specifically examined how much the pill reduced the so-called apnea-hypopnea index, or AHI, which records the number of times per hour a person's breathing shows a restricted or completely blocked airway. The index is used to evaluate the severity of obstructive sleep apnea and the effectiveness of treatments for the condition.

People who took Foundayo and were not using PAP therapy had an average of 21.1 fewer AHI events per hour at 52 weeks, compared with an average reduction of 4.8 per hour for the placebo, according to the results.

Foundayo also met the trial's second goal in that group, leading to an overall AHI reduction of 43.8% compared with a decrease of 6.3% for the placebo. Lilly said 31.5% of the participants in the Foundayo group met the criteria for remission or improvement to mild OSA.

Meanwhile, patients who were on both Foundayo and PAP therapy had an average of 18.3 fewer AHI events per hour at 52 weeks, compared with an average reduction of 5.5 per hour for the placebo.

Foundayo led to an overall AHI reduction of around 27.5% in that group, compared with a decrease of 3% for the placebo. The company said 19.6% of the participants on Foundayo and PAP therapy also met the criteria for remission or mild OSA.

Foundayo also reduced what's known as the sleep apnea-specific hypoxic burden — a measure of how deeply and for how long oxygen levels fall during sleep. That metric fell by 55.9% in people not using PAP therapy and 45.8% in those using it.

Lilly said the pill also helped patients lose roughly 11% of their weight across groups at 52 weeks and helped to lower a marker of inflammation.

The company said the safety data and how well patients tolerated Foundayo were generally consistent with previous trials on the pill.

Around 12% of people on Foundayo who were not using PAP therapy discontinued due to side effects, compared to 3.8% with the placebo. Meanwhile, 6.8% of those on both Lilly's pill and PAP discontinued the drug due to side effects, compared with 0.6% in the placebo group.

The most common side effects were gastrointestinal, including nausea, diarrhea, constipation and vomiting, and were generally mild to moderate in severity. That's consistent with other GLP-1 medications.

Rima Safari, U.S. data, analytics and AI practice leader at PwC US, helps the world’s largest organizations move from AI experimentation to business outcomes. The first is using AI to analyze how existing processes and data can lead to new insights, which they can turn into new products and services to sell externally. “It’s contextually relevant information at the right point in time that allows you to immediately make a decision. That is what organizations need in this agentic world,” Safari said. That makes protecting the data essential, she said.

True AI Transformation Starts With a Blank Whiteboard, Not Yesterday’s Blueprint

That sort of reimagination and transformative thinking is difficult for large enterprises, many of which have spent decades building entrenched processes. “That should not be the question at all,” she said. “It should be: With AI, how do I reimagine this entire process?”

One very important thing companies need to rethink from a risk perspective is their security measures, which have come into sharper focus as AI agents move beyond controlled environments and start to act more independently in real-world systems. Safari said these risks have slowed adoption but are also changing how companies approach AI development.

“Historically, organizations often prioritized experimentation and rapid development, with the expectation that security and governance could be addressed as solutions matured and scaled. That mindset is fundamentally shifting. Security can no longer be an afterthought—it needs to be embedded into the architecture and design of AI solutions from day one,” Safari said.

Tokenomics has become a recurring concern for executives, Safari said. Companies that once celebrated AI usage through internal leaderboards are now scrutinizing spending.

“You don’t want to use the most sophisticated models for something as simple as summarizing a document,” she said. Just as organizations’ data will be a competitive advantage, so too will employees who have the skills to build on that data. Safari expects technical fluency to become essential at every level of the organizational chart, including at the executive level.

“I say every executive will need to become a builder in some capacity—not necessarily a technologist, but someone with the technical fluency to turn ideas into solutions and actively shape how their business operates,” she said.

#357. Apple reveals new Mac security controls as AI agents raise privacy risks

As promised last month, Apple on Monday announced changes to Mac security controls designed to protect users’ data from the growing popularity of AI agents and their apps’ demand for “full disk access.”

“Before granting an app such extraordinary access, users should understand that it puts all of the data on their Mac at risk,” Apple warned in its blog post aimed at app developers.

Unlike AI chatbots that simply answer questions, agents run on a user’s device where they can take actions across the operating system, files, and apps on the user’s behalf.

To balance these competing interests, Apple said in October that it would revise some of its security settings around Mac apps. The announcement followed two notable incidents involving AI agents. In one, a journalist claimed that Meta’s Muse app read their private messages without consent; a separate report from Wired cited a flaw in ChatGPT’s Mac app that could have allowed hackers to access sensitive data.

Now, Apple is sharing the changes it’s making.

For starters, it’s splitting up Device Control and Data Access into two settings: System & App Protection Override and Accessibility. The changes are designed to help users better understand that selecting the first option means overriding built-in security protections.

To streamline configurations, Apple says there will now also be a single method for granting permissions that involves selecting the requested app from a list. The company will also introduce new guidance encouraging users to grant access only to the specific apps that need it when they enable the System & App Protection Override setting. Similar guidance will appear when users enable Full Disk Access to allow an app to access their folders.

The company will also provide users with “increased visibility into the wide range of data apps will receive access to or control over if full permissions are granted,” the blog post notes.

Another change will give users more granular control over when an AI agent or app can gain access by allowing users to configure their apps to ask for permission every time access is needed. While arguably tedious and a potential obstacle to true automation, the feature could allow users to experiment with local AI without giving the agent carte blanche.

There will also be a new transparency indicator that appears when an app is controlling another app through the System & App Protection Override option.

The changes, which are arriving with macOS 27.3, don’t appear to prevent Mac users from installing and running AI agents or other apps that ask for higher levels of access. Instead, they give users more nuanced options, control, and information about the choices they’re making.

Apple advises app developers to adjust their onboarding processes to account for the new changes and suggests that their apps learn to “gracefully handle having access to only a limited set of folders or apps.”

Developers of apps that request Full Disk Access for disk backup or to provide assistive technologies can request exceptions to the new rules (which Apple calls “entitlements”) to preserve the same functionality they offered before the macOS 27.3 update.

#279. Why the UK is dithering over what to do about e-scooters

Why the UK is dithering over what to do about e-scooters

When I step out of Southampton Central railway station, it takes me just seconds to spot an e-scooter. In this city, these devices have become a normal part of the transport ecosystem, along with e-bikes.

"It's just the most convenient way to commute from home to the station and back," says Keith Reed, who I speak to as he arrives at the station to catch a train to London. Keith says uses rental e-scooters twice a week. His alternative option would be his bike, but he'd then have to worry about where to leave it.

Around the city, there are parking areas for bright orange rental devices everywhere - but they're not necessarily a permanent feature. Southampton hosts one of the numerous government-backed trials around England. This one has been going on since 2021.

A study suggests around half of users of e-scooter do so in conjunction with another mode of transport - like Keith, who rides to the railway station

It also doesn't take long to find e-scooters which aren't part of the trial, but instead are owned by individuals. I see a man riding one along the pavement outside a parade of shops. He doesn't want to stop and talk.

These privately owned e-scooters are illegal to ride on public roads across the UK, but they've proliferated regardless. One expert told me the situation was a "Wild West".

Among people I spoke to in Southampton, the same concerns came up time and time again: "They're a blinking nuisance," says elderly pedestrian Fred. Another shopper, Martin, says he's "very concerned" whenever he sees them racing through precincts: "An accident's almost inevitable."

The endgame of the trials is to reach a decision on whether to make permanent hired e-scooter schemes such as the one Keith uses - and whether to bring in legislation that would legalise and regulate privately owned e-scooters too.

But six years since the trials started around England, there's still no conclusion - and as the UK government has responsibility for road traffic legislation across Great Britain, officials in Scotland and Wales say they are waiting on Westminster to act.

So why is it taking so long for the government to decide what to do, and what are the consequences of delay? What does the evidence tell us about how safe these things are?

And why are people using them in the first place - are they here to stay as a useful part of the transport offer, or will they end up being viewed as a pest on our streets?

You'd recognise an e-scooter if you saw one. The Department for Transport's (DfT's) definition of an e-scooter includes no pedals, two wheels and a maximum continuous power rating of 500W.

And so far, the trials around England appear to have proved popular. According to DfT research, fom January 2022 to May 2024, 36.9 million trips were made across 30 trial areas outside of London. On average, nearly 19,500 e-scooters were available to hire every day.

Dr Yasir Ali, a reader in Transport Modelling at Loughborough University, says e-scooters are "complementary" to other modes of transport, rather than replacing them. Due to factors such as range and being exposed to the elements, they are less suitable for longer trips, rural areas with less infrastructure or in bad weather. They work best as "a first- and last-mile solution" in urban areas, to reduce reliance on cars for short trips.

Shared mobility charity CoMoUK's 2025 report suggests that for more than half of the people using rental e-scooters, the most common use was travelling to work or school. Its analysis of DfT data suggests the majority of people using hired e-scooters are male and under 35.

"Sometimes it's a bit difficult to take the bus from dot to dot", says Yan Zhang, who works at Southampton university and doesn't drive, "so for shorter distances, I'll take the scooter."

Like Yan Zhang, e-scooter users are less likely to have a car, CoMo's report says - this is partly explained by official trials' concentration in urban areas

Around half of respondents to CoMoUK's online survey used it in conjunction with another mode of transport - such as riding to a railway station, like Keith.

Earlier evidence from a national evaluation of the trials published in 2022 found that although some users would have driven instead had they not had access to an e-scooter, the biggest "modal shift" - meaning switch in ways of getting around - to e-scooters was from walking. Given walking is an active mode of travel, this has raised questions over how significant the public health impact of e-scooter use really is.

And the usage of privately owned e-scooters isn't reflected in this research. The DfT has said between 760,000 and 1.2 million people in England aged 16 and over own one - despite the fact that it remains illegal to ride them in public.

In the eyes of the law, e-scooters are classed as motor vehicles. Outside of the trials, they can't be insured, and thus can only legally be ridden on private land, with the owner's permission.

Parking bays for e-scooters and e-bikes are available across Southampton

The trials themselves are bound by strict rules. The devices' top speed is limited. Businesses who operate the pilots must insure them. Users must have a full or provisional driving licence, and they must not ride on the pavement. Helmets are recommended but not mandatory.

Richard Dilks, CEO of CoMoUK says "mass-scale use of something that is inherently low-carbon, low-impact, low-cost" is a "good thing" but "we also don't have rules of engagement about how they should be ridden".

And as a result of the delays in introducing regulation, Loughborogh University's Yasir Ali says "we are increasingly in a situation where the law and reality have diverged", with "the disadvantages of e-scooter use without securing the full benefits of regulation".

Back in Southampton, I meet a lady pushing a black e-scooter, who doesn't want to give her name. She says nobody told her when she bought it that it was illegal to ride on the streets.

The Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency has a unit that carries out surveillance of retailers to check they're complying with the law. But earlier this month, an investigation by the Press Association news agency discovered some retailers were advertising them for commuting purposes.

Police tell me they regularly see children illegally riding private e-scooters. Margaret Winchcomb, deputy executive director at the Parliamentary Advisory Council for Transport Safety (PACTS), says some are being specifically marketed at kids.

Police say they will seize privately owned e-scooters that are ridden illegally

So what can police do? Forces regularly launch crackdowns where they seize illegally used devices, and often crush them. Each year, awareness campaigns run urging parents not to buy their kids an e-scooter for Christmas. But enforcement is a challenge.

Insp Matt Boiles from Avon and Somerset's road policing team says problems arise even with the legal trial devices - for example, people riding them after drinking alcohol, which counts as drink-driving. Riding with another person on the back is another common issue.

But officers see private e-scooters being illegally ridden in public places "all the time, every single day" and they can be really hard to catch - it isn't always safe for police in a car to pursue someone on an e-scooter, he says.

Insp Boiles despairs over parents buying them for children: "We do have parents attend a police station quite a lot, getting very, very upset that their child's Â£500 e-scooter has been seized." But "the legislation is really, really clear that you can't have it back".

There's a potential risk of fire if unregulated devices with lithium batteries are left to charge unattended. Most train operators have banned e-scooters on board for this reason.

But there's also the fact that riders are unprotected in the case of falling off or being hit by motorised vehicles. And - along with e-bikes - they can end up involved in collisions where pedestrians are hurt. Many trial e-scooters in the UK are restricted to 12.5mph, but privately owned scooters' speeds aren't.

Some coroners have even raised concerns. In 2022, a coroner in London called for action after the death of 14-year-old Fatima Abukah on a privately owned scooter.

According to the DfT, in 2024 - the most recent year for which figures are available - 1,390 casualties were recorded in collisions that involved e-scooter in England, Scotland and Wales, three more than in the previous 12 months. The vast majority, 1,096, were e-scooter users themselves.

Young boys and men appear to be over-represented in the stats. Of the casualties, 302 - the most of any age and sex category - were males aged 10-19.

Six deaths were recorded in 2024, unchanged from the previous year. Five of these were riders and one a pedestrian.

Although the numbers aren't conclusive, it is thought more accidents are happening on privately owned e-scooters. Winchcomb says police statistics "aren't reflective of the number of injuries".

Nonetheless, campaigners believe there is enough evidence to show that regulation is urgently needed.

Carly Calland's 14-year-old son Jacob died in March 2025 of a catastrophic head injury. He was a passenger on an e-scooter that was involved with a collision with a car.

If privately owned e-scooters are legalised for public use, Carly believes, there should be mandatory helmets, a ban on carrying passengers and penalties for parents that allow children to ride illegally.

"If Jacob was wearing a helmet that day, he would still be here," Carly, from Wythenshawe in Greater Manchester, says.

Carly is not against e-scooters. They "are really good for people to get to work, and they are eco-friendly", she tells me, but "they just need to be used in the correct way."

What happens if the current situation goes on? Her answer is emphatic: "More deaths."

Meanwhile a number of major European cities have ended their shared e-scooter schemes. Paris abolished them after a referendum in 2023. Brussels is the most recent, announcing theirs would end in 2027, following a rise in accidents.

The last evaluation of the trials in England was in 2022. New findings are expected by early next year.

In recent years, successive British transport secretaries have supported the idea of a new law to legalise and regulate the e-scooters available for sale. E-scooters were mentioned in the 2022 Queen's speech, but nothing materialised.

Stormy weather can shrink the available airspace, meaning traffic has to be restricted and rerouted. If planes are unable to land at one airport, for example due to high winds, they may need diverting to another. But that can create its own problems; if too many unscheduled aircraft land at an already busy airport, it might not have sufficient infrastructure to accommodate them all.

Heavy rain and storms can make flying harder

Planes need fuel and somewhere to park. Passengers need food and facilities. Ultimately, if there isn't space, it can simply refuse to accept all but emergency traffic.

This is where the scenario set out by Atkinson comes in. He warns of a situation where traffic is diverted from Heathrow, Gatwick, Luton and Stansted at the same time, leaving aircraft coming in across the Atlantic and a large number of European flights all looking for somewhere to land at the same time, and some with dwindling fuel reserves.

This would at the very least create serious disruption for passengers, who might end up hundreds of miles from their destinations. But it could also, he thinks, create "a very serious risk event, such as an aircraft running out of fuel in the sky".

Aircraft are legally required to carry enough fuel to take them to an alternative airport if necessary, and to allow them to fly for an extra 30 minutes on top of that.

Not everyone shares Atkinson's concern. Former airline executive Andrew Charlton, who manages the Swiss-based consultancy Aviation Advocacy, thinks the risk is low.

"You've always got to imagine the worst-case scenario", he says. "But I also think we need to keep that worst case in context.

"Yes, it's possible to imagine it. But I'm also fairly comfortable with the ability of this industry to react well and make good decisionsâ¦ of all the things that wake me up at 3amâ¦this is not the top of that list."

Thunderstorms are caused by what is known as convective activity, where moist air close to the ground warms rapidly and rises quickly. As it rises, it expands and cools, allowing the moisture it carries to condense, forming large clouds. More heat means more convective energy, potentially creating instability. In addition, for every degree the Earth warms, the atmosphere can hold about 7% more moisture, increasing the probability of heavy rainfall.

It is not clear whether this means we will actually get more thunderstorms in the UK and across Europe, as so many variables are involved beyond the simple matter of energy and moisture. Some parts of the world may even get fewer. However, studies have shown that higher latitudes, particularly in the northern hemisphere, will see the biggest increases in favourable conditions for storms to form, and those that do may be more violent. According to the UK's Met Office, "while we may not see more thunderstorms overall, the ones we do experience could be more impactful".

Where possible, pilots will avoid flying through convective weather. Even though aircraft are designed to cope with severe conditions, storms can produce intense turbulence, capable of damaging the structural frame of the plane, as well as throwing people around the cabin if they are not strapped in. Hail can also be a big problem, potentially cracking windscreens or causing engine problems. So, however much inconvenience it may cause passengers, crews will invariably put safety first.

New technology helps pilots navigate tricky flying conditions

Onboard weather radars can help aircrew to see up to 320 nautical miles ahead. They also have access to live weather forecasting resources online. But thunderstorms can move quickly and, according to Prof Guy Gratton, an expert in aeronautics at Cranfield University, these systems are essentially "tactical rather than strategic". In other words, although they enable pilots to see storm cells on their screens from a relatively short distance away, and request a diversion, they do not allow for detailed long-distance planning.

The result is that unplanned diversions often happen late in flight, which is likely to mean burning more fuel. If storm delays do become more common, says Andrew Charlton, then aircraft will have to load more fuel as a matter of course, in case there is trouble brewing ahead. "Aircraft are going to have to fly further, and they're going to have to factor in the assumption they're going to fly further," he says. For an airline, fuel is one of its major costs â and if the fuel bill goes up, he says, it is inevitable passengers will pay more.

More intense storms would be likely to make air traffic management in busy regions harder and potentially lead to more delays and cancellations. However, it is possible some of this could be mitigated using new technologies.

Both Heathrow and Gatwick, for example, have moved away from mandating minimum distances between incoming aircraft in favour of a system which continually calculates spacing times based on wind speeds and aircraft types. In practice, this is meant to allow controllers to reduce the distance between landing aircraft, particularly in high winds, and get them on the ground more quickly. According to NATS, it has successfully improved landing rates and reduced delays by a significant margin.

However, there could be other effects from more severe storms as well. Heavier rainfall, for instance, increases the likelihood of flooding. In a worst case, this can paralyse airport operations, as happened in April 2024 when torrential downpours hit Dubai International Airport. It briefly had to close its runways, while disruption lasted for two days. More than 1,200 flights were cancelled, affecting tens of thousands of passengers.

Passengers were delayed during the floods in Dubai

But even if runways and taxiways themselves are clear, and aircraft are able to fly without any difficulty, passengers may struggle to catch their flights if roads, railways or car parks are flooded.

Protecting against such risks can be expensive. Gatwick Airport, which experienced severe flooding over the Christmas period in 2013, has spent tens of millions of pounds on mitigation strategies in recent years â although those figures are dwarfed by the more than Â£2bn it is expecting to invest in its current expansion plans.

The problem of clear-air turbulence is also a real one. It cannot be seen, and is becoming increasingly common as the climate warms.

This is when air moves violently at high altitudes with no clouds or other visual cues to help a pilot avoid it. For passengers, it usually only means a little bit of bumpiness, but occasionally it can be a lot more serious. On 1 March 2023, for example, a Lufthansa flight from Austin, Texas to Frankfurt encountered what the airline described as "brief but severe" turbulence.

Those aboard described how passengers and flight crew were thrown around the cabin as the plane suddenly lost altitude. After it made an emergency landing in Washington DC, seven people were taken to hospital.

While still rare, this kind of turbulence is already much more prevalent than it used to be and is likely to become even more so.

"You know the projections are pretty dire: doubling, trebling or worse in the amount of severe turbulence on many busy flight routes around the world," says Paul Williams, a professor of atmospheric science at the University of Reading. He says incidents over the North Atlantic have increased by 55% since 1979.

Nevertheless, he remains confident solutions can be found, including improvements to forecasting and making use of laser-based technology.

There is another climate-related threat to aviation, which may prove challenging to overcome: rising sea levels. When Hurricane Sandy arrived off the US East Coast in October 2012, it provoked a storm surge that flooded the city. The two runways at New York's LaGuardia airport were left under water for several days, preventing it from reopening even after the winds had subsided.

Significant flooding that would have affected the New York City area once in every 500 years in pre-industrial times is now experienced every 25 years or so â and could occur every five years by the middle of the century, according to one major study. That makes LaGuardia, built on reclaimed land next to Flushing Bay, appear highly vulnerable.

LaGuardia could be at greater risk of flooding and future closures

It is not alone. With some 40% of the world's population living within 100km of coastlines, many airports have been built on low-lying areas near the sea. According to research by Newcastle University, 269 airports around the world are already at risk of flooding due to rising sea levels, and that total is expected to rise. Among those potentially most exposed are the likes of Amsterdam Schiphol, London City, Bahrain International and Newark Liberty International.

Many airports do already have some degree of flood protection, especially larger ones. But stopping the flood risks from rising over the next century means expensive investment in flood defences, raising runways, or even moving airports. It could cost up to $57bn (Â£42bn) according to the Newcastle report. Such costs are ultimately likely to be met by taxpayers or by travellers.

The aviation industry is often criticised for its own contribution to climate change emissions. It faces a forbidding challenge in attempting to meet a target of being carbon neutral by 2050, while also accommodating growth that is expected to see the global aircraft fleet double in size over the next two decades.

Yet it is clear the impacts of climate change will also be felt by airlines, airports and inevitably by passengers -

"I think the best we can do is mitigate", says Andrew Charlton.

"We need to make sure we have enough space at air terminals for people whose flights are being disrupted and delayed, that we have enough hotel rooms and parking space and so on."

But ultimately, he says, "disagreeable moments are going to happen more".

Passengers, he thinks, will have to accept it as a fact of flying life.

#266. My hometown shows that high streets have to change or die

My hometown shows that high streets have to change or die

The empty building that used to be the home of Marks & Spencer in Aberdeen is one of the first things you see when you arrive off the train and come into the city centre. The sign has gone, though the shape of the lettering can still be made out; the windows are blacked out and the doors boarded up.

This is my hometown. As a teenager, I spent hours on Union Street, its main thoroughfare, nicknamed the Granite Mile after the local silver-grey stone. It was the place to try on all the latest fashions from the likes of Clockhouse and Chelsea Girl.

The street used to be packed with shoppers from across the north-east of Scotland.

"I come to Union Street to take a bus somewhere else... there's nothing to shop for nowâ¦ it's horrendous," complains Marie Sim, a lifelong Aberdeen resident.

It's a similar story across the country: High Streets, once bursting with shops, hit first by the rise of out-of-town retail centres and then by online commerce.

But Aberdeen is in the thick of an ambitious turnaround plan. The port city has essentially declared war on its empty shop problem, taking an unusually hands-on approach to filling vacant units and bringing long-neglected upper floors of buildings into use.

The Marks & Spencer flagship store in Aberdeen closed in August, 80 years after it opened there

On top of that, the council is pouring tens of millions into trying to make the city centre a destination again. Like many other places, it's leaning into the "experience economy", with a new indoor food and drink market and a major redevelopment of the seafront.

High Streets are integral to how we feel about where we live. The sight of vacant shops and graffiti can quickly become a vicious circle, deterring shoppers, discouraging investment and leading to anti-social behaviour.

Politicians of all stripes have been trying for years to breathe new life into town centres - and Andy Burnham, who has made a focus on "place" part of his political identity, is the latest. He argues they should become "symbols of Britain's renaissance".

But can it be achieved? And if they're to survive, do town centres need a fundamental rethink?

One in eight properties on British High Streets are currently vacant, according to Green Street, the property and retail analytics company, a number that's stayed stubbornly high for years.

"If there was a silver bullet, it would have been fired years ago," says Jackie Sadek, a former government adviser on urban regeneration, who has spent nearly four decades working in town centre renewal.

One of the biggest obstacles is fractured ownership. Shops and buildings are often owned by dozens of different landlords, sometimes through offshore companies or complex structures, each with their own interests and priorities. That can slow decision-making and make large-scale regeneration difficult.

But even with empty units that are available, you need to find someone willing to take them on.

One in eight properties on British High Streets are currently vacant

The cost of running a High Street business has also risen; employment costs are higher, and there's the burden of business rates.

Firms need customers. Footfall on many High Streets has been in long-term decline as shopping and services move online, and this enormous structural shift has yet to play out fully.

Two-thirds of town centre experts think there is still 20% to 40% too much retail space, according to a survey by retail body Revo and real estate consultancy Lambert Smith Hampton.

In Aberdeen's boom years of North Sea oil, from the late 1970s through to the early 2010s, it didn't have to work hard to fill its hotels and restaurants and get the cash tills ringing because of the wealth the energy industry brought to the city.

I remember weekends with friends as a teenager, spending whatever cash we had in the fashion chains and shoe shops. Later came the pubs and bar-hopping, and trips to the art deco Capitol Theatre (now office space), where I saw Duran Duran.

But by the end of 2022, Union Street had hit rock bottom. Aberdonians had long deserted the High Street for a shopping centre, which opened in 2009 next to the railway station.

"They'd fallen out of love with their city," says Bob Keiller, head of Our Union Street coalition, a community-led group formed after an emergency summit to reverse the decline.

The first thing it did was listen to what residents wanted, receiving thousands of submissions. Restoring a bit of civic pride and filling empty units became the focus.

It's been a co-ordinated effort, involving letting agents, the council, landlords, businesses and a big team of volunteers, led by Keiller, a former FTSE 100 CEO, who isn't getting paid. He has a map on his office wall, detailing the status of every property, unit by unit, with colour-coded Post-it notes.

They audited the street, tracking down absent landlords, and logging what was empty and why. Volunteers offered to give the empty properties a clean and a lick of paint, to make them more attractive to let. They also spruced up the streets, picking litter, weeding, and painting bins. A small team still goes out once a fortnight.

Bob Keiller heads a community-led group set up to help revive Aberdeen's Union Street

There's a website where all the available properties are collated; crucially, all of the financial incentives are also listed. These can include council fit-out grants (where a new business gets money to decorate premises), and a two-year discount from business rates.

In 2023, 57 of the 198 units on Union Street were empty. As of today, 33 have been filled, with a further 14 in the process of being let.

But it can feel like a game of whack-a-mole because since the initiative started, another 20 units have become vacant. "The key thing is, we're filling quicker than emptying," Keiller insists.

Keiller's operations director, David McKeith, describes it as putting an arm round the shoulder of those they can help and being a "wasp in the ear" of people who just say no.

At one end of the office, a small, young team produces social media content, linking visitors and residents to a new Our Aberdeen app, showing what the city can offer.

For Keiller, the "small wins" - like tackling graffiti - are needed to help change public perception.

"We've realised the psychology is not to do it once but be prepared to do it two or three times before people that do these things eventually say, 'there's no point in putting it there because somebody's going to paint over it.'"

Attention is also turning to the often-forgotten upper floors of buildings along the once-iconic Union Street. In a separate initiative run by the business group Aberdeen Inspired, experts are getting inside those vacant high-up spaces to identify the barriers to bringing them back into use.

Aberdeen sought the help of a High Street troubleshooter, Iain Nicholson, who's worked with dozens of towns and cities through his Vacant Shops Academy.

Out of more than 1,000 empty units he helped audit nationally, on average more than half didn't have a letting agent actively marketing the property. He says some councils don't even know who the landlords are.

Empty shops, he says, won't all be filled by the market alone. Towns and cities need to encourage a broader range of occupiers, from arts and culture to leisure, health and well-being.

He says what's striking about Aberdeen is its joined-up approach, and the fact people "rolled up their sleeves" and overcame differences. "That's not something you can yet say about everywhere."

Every town and city faces its own mix of challenges.

Nicholson also helped Crewe in Cheshire. As a smaller place than Aberdeen, it is focusing on arts, culture, and community, with a new women's centre opened this month.

And English councils are being encouraged to take a more interventionist role.

They can now force an empty commercial unit that's been vacant for at least a year to be offered to tenants through a High Street Rental Auction, even if the landlord doesn't want to let it.

A noodle bar (left) is one of the new businesses in the area. Parts of the city centre have been undergoing construction work (right)

It's early days but the mere prospect of losing control of a property is delivering some results. Bassetlaw Council in Nottinghamshire, an early adopter of the scheme, says more than 30 shops across the district have been let or are currently under offer as a result of the policy.

But filling empty units is only part of the challenge. The bigger question is what town and city centres are actually for.

Changing habits mean we won't need so many shops. Instead, it's argued, town centres will need more people living and working in them, with new homes and office space alongside services that can't be done online. With an ageing population, for example, bringing NHS health centres into the heart of a town could be a big opportunity.

In Aberdeen, the building that used to house BHS - a department store shuttered in 2016 - is being converted into an entrance plaza for the new food and drink market.

The physical appearance of Union Street is changing too. Like many Victorian shopping thoroughfares, it was built with a wide road (probably to give space to horses and carriages). Now parts of the pavements are being widened to make it a more pleasant place to spend time, though many Aberdonians are weary of the disruption, and of new traffic restrictions.

Money is a big barrier. Aberdeen's new indoor food and drink market is costing Â£40m, funded by the council and the UK Levelling Up Fund. In other places, regeneration schemes can struggle because the cost of redevelopment outweighs the likely financial return, making support from the public sector essential. And the economics are becoming more challenging.

"Higher interest rates, rising costs of construction and other issues are impacting the viability and delivery of new housing and big redevelopment projects," says Steve Norris, a director at Lambert Smith Hampton. #248. Why are more roofs not made of solar tiles?

From installer to instructor Kate Collardson (left) has a long history in solar systems

Some people in the US "still hate the look of solar", Kate Collardson acknowledges.

She would know. Collardson handles operations for the Solar Service of Colorado, a solar maintenance and inspection company. She's also held just about every other job in the industry, from installer to instructor.

For those that hate the look of solar panels, an alternative is a solar roof, where the tiles (or shingles) house solar cells, but look much the same as regular tiles.

This is especially important for historic buildings, where heritage rules limit changes to their appearance.

For many people, the first time they'd heard of this product category was in August, when Tesla's website stopped offering solar roofs, external. This followed disappointing sales and a lawsuit over sudden price hikes, external.

Other companies have also pulled out, but Tesla's decision in particular sent shockwaves through the solar roof industry.

Some see it as a death knell for solar tiles; others, as an opportunity.

Tesla abruptly stopped marketing its solar tiles in August

For instance, the small Swiss company Freesuns is considering expanding to the US.

Deborah Learoyd, Freesuns' CEO and managing director, estimates that the company's sales have grown 20â30% since it was established in 2019.

In general, solar roof components continue to be manufactured in smaller batches. This limits the economies of scale and standardisation, though flexible manufacturing could help somewhat.

Learoyd says that while the unit costs for Freesuns' products are slowly decreasing, "a lot of the cost is actually the fact that every roof is a little bit different. And so it has to be engineered a little bit differently, installed a little bit differently".

Solar roofs typically require collaboration among roofers, solar installers and electricians. And building regulations tend to be more complex than for solar panels.

The additional time and paperwork can take a toll on a roofing business, Collardson explains. "You try to put a roofer into the equation and they're going, 'This is taking forever. I'm losing money. I could have sold 10 roofing jobs in the time that it takes me to sell this one'."

Collardson has to charge more for repair of solar shingles, compared to a standard photovoltaic (PV) setup.

She explains, "With traditional solar, we generally have electronics on the panel and a map that shows us exactly how much each panel is producing." So when something goes wrong, it's easy to spot where.

"With solar shingles, we don't really have the benefit of that mapping; there's no room underneath the shingle for the electronics." Thermal cameras can identify the trouble spots, however.

But then there's the matter of accessing them. "You can remove the one in the middle of the array, but you have to walk on the others, and you really shouldn't walk on them because you're going to create micro-fractures and that's going to cause issues down the road."

Before repair costs are factored in, a solar roof might cost twice as much as a system of solar panels mounted on a roof.

Solar roofs are also less efficient - one reason is that the tiles are just smaller.

Another factor is the energy lost as heat. Since the tiles sit close to the roof, there's less airflow than with standard solar panels mounted on a roof. Heat impairs solar performance.

Coloured tiles are even less efficient, comments Ben Graves, co-founder of UK solar company Sunsave. "The reason why all solar cells are black, and increasingly the whole panel is now black, is because black is the best absorbing colour."

Overall, much more space would need to be devoted to solar roof tiles to produce the same amount of energy.

Sunsave does not offer solar roofs. "Most people in the UK just don't have enough money to pay for it," Graves believes.

While appearance was a bigger factor five years ago, now "the vast majority of consumers are most interested in how much they can save".

But supporters of solar roofs argue that they should not be pitted against standard solar modules on roofs.

"Let's not compare PV roof tiles to a standard PV system, but look at PV roof tiles as generating additional electricity from our roofs instead of just having the roof sit there," urges Martin Heinrich who leads a research group at the Fraunhofer Institute for Solar Energy Systems in Germany.

A solar roof can cost twice as much as installing solar panels

While standard solar panels have gone from strength to strength, solar roofs have not advanced to the same extent.

One advancement that could especially help solar roofs is matrix shingling technology.

This is a different way of connecting solar cells. The solar cells are cut into narrow strips, layered like overlapping shingles, and connected to each other using electrically conductive adhesive, rather than metal wires.

This means that the electrical current can flow around shaded areas, unlike with standard modules. "If you get like a bird pooping on a part of the cell, the current can go around it," Learoyd explains.

In addition to increasing energy output, this improves the appearance as the silver wires aren't visible.

Another advantage, Heinrich says, is that "since we cut the cells into smaller areas, we can fill out special sizes more easily. You don't have to adhere to the regular pattern size."

Freesuns is an early adopter of matrix shingling technology in building-integrated PV. "We see it as a big opportunity for the future in terms of how we would make our tiles," Learoyd says.

What would also help the company is better software for automated design. This would speed up the design process, since each roofing job is unique.

Learoyd believes this would lower costs and give "the whole construction industry much more confidence in using the product".

Ten years after her own solar roof was installed, and despite the sector's setbacks, Learoyd remains optimistic.

She believes that for people with the means, it will eventually become standard to install a new roof with some sort of embedded solar. "It's just a no-brainer."
