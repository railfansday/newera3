#470. New WhatsApp head wants to make the app easier to use, give its AI more chops

WhatsApp has grown into one of the most popular messaging apps in the world, used by over 3 billion people. But with that growth, the app has also become complex, packing in features like communities, channels, stories and business features, and a lot of people find it hard to navigate.

Kunal Shah, who took over leadership of the app’s development from Will Cathcart earlier this year, now wants to make the app and actions within it simpler to use. In a new note shared on Tuesday, Shah said the company wants to make voice notes, groups, stickers, and video “simpler and more expressive.”

WhatsApp has long also tried to develop an ecosystem that lets users explore products and make purchases, and Shah’s note indicates more of that is coming, hinting at partnerships with payment companies and telecom providers to enable bill payment, loans, and eSIM purchases.

Shah also said the company wants to use AI to surface important information to users.

“It should quickly find what you need, get things done, and make sure you never miss a booking or an appointment. The best technology saves you time, so you can get back to the connections that matter,” he wrote.

WhatsApp currently offers an AI assistant that can summarize messages and create drafts. It’s not clear if the company will use its Muse agent to collate information and send reminders to people.

I think of WhatsApp like a shared language, spoken by 3bn+ people. Sometimes we use words, and sometimes it’s a voice note to a parent, a sticker that ends an argument, or a “home safely” at 2am. Language is at the heart of everything we as people do. We write letters, tell stories, send hard messages and make plans for the future. A WhatsApp chat is often where we start.

Like any language, the people who use it shape it. They don’t wait for permission. They find new things to say, and new ways to say them. Often, our users show us what should come next based on how they’re using the app and communicating in the world.

I’ve spent the last few months listening and understanding what our users need and want from us and, and what I heard is simple: Help people stay close to those who matter to them, and get more done on WhatsApp.

First, we want to make connections richer.  Sometimes hearing someone’s voice, sending a joke, agreeing on a plan or knowing they’re safe is the whole point. People send over 10 billion voice messages and over 4 billion stickers on WhatsApp every day. As the way people communicate evolves, we will evolve these experiences with them. We’ll make voice notes, groups, stickers and video simpler and more expressive, because how people feel close keeps changing.

Second, we want anything you do to be as simple as sending a message. Too many things start in a chat and end with many more steps somewhere else: paying a bill, borrowing money, staying connected when you travel. Each handoff to another task costs time. We’re working with financial services and telecom partners so that finishing something feels as easy as starting it.

Third, we want to make sure AI makes it all more efficient. It should quietly find what you need, get things done and make sure you never miss a booking or an appointment. The best technology saves you time, so you can get back to the connections that matter.

None of this matters without trust. Trust takes years to earn and a moment to lose, and I don’t take it lightly. Whatever we build, WhatsApp must remain simple, reliable and private. These principles we’ve had for years aren’t changing. Your personal messages and calls stay end-to-end encrypted. That isn’t changing, and neither is our quality bar.

What happens inside WhatsApp only matters for what it makes possible outside it: more time together, stronger communities, and fewer barriers between people and the lives they want to live.

#467. Reddit is turning user conversations into AI-powered product reviews

Shortly after announcing a plan to transition its site to a logged-in experience, Reddit is debuting a feature that could help make it more of a destination for people doing product research via the web. The company on Tuesday introduced “Reddit Reviews,” a feature that organizes conversations about products into dedicated reviews that include the highlights and key takeaways from users across its communities.

The company notes that the new experience builds on one of the ways people are already using Reddit: to learn from others what products are really like, and how they fit into their everyday lives. These first-hand accounts and recommendations can help consumers determine whether or not a particular product is right for them.

However, given the rise of generative AI and bots, it’s an area that’s also ripe for “astroturfing” — a term that refers to when a brand fakes public support. With Reddit now effectively turning what was casual online research into more of a structured product, it could become a larger target for these types of fake reviews and recommendations than before.

To address this, Reddit’s announcement points to its rules and its latest measures to combat automated abuse and spam, as well as its practice involving community moderators, as tools it will use to keep the reviews on the site legit.

The Reddit Reviews feature itself taps into AI to organize the conversations people are having where they describe their product experiences. These posts will often see users discussing how a product works for them, or why it fails, or what the trade-offs are that someone should consider before buying.

The company has been testing the product reviews feature since February, initially with U.S. users in areas like Electronics, Video Games, and Health & Beauty products. In September, Reddit expanded the test to include the Home & Garden and Fashion & Apparel categories as well.

Now, the company says Reddit Reviews is rolling out to all U.S. users on iOS, Android, and web, with an initial selection focused on around 10,000 products. Over time, it will roll out to more categories and expand beyond the U.S.

To use the feature, Reddit users can search for a product or category in the app’s search bar or click on a highlighted product name they see in someone’s comment. From there, the user is taken to a screen where important conversations are summarized under a “what people are saying” heading. This will also include a visual breakdown that shows how many of those comments are positive, negative, or mixed, and what sort of things people said they liked or disliked about the item.

For instance, a user considering buying an espresso machine might want to know about things like how much counter space it takes up or whether it’s easier for beginners to understand how to use it, says Reddit. Users can then browse the comments, filter them by topic, or click to be taken to the original conversation, where they can ask more questions or get advice.

Users can also move from one product review back to the full category to weigh other, similar products that have been discussed to compare their options, the company said.

The feature arrives just ahead of the busy holiday shopping season, when people spend a lot more time doing online product research ahead of their bigger purchases.

#455. Google broadens its AI hardware portfolio with two new specialized chips

Google is notching up its chipmaking game as the company seeks to reduce its dependence on chips made by other companies for its cloud computing business. Google on Monday unveiled two new variants of its tensor processing units (TPUs), in-house-developed chips meant for AI workloads, that broaden its chip portfolio to additional use cases: confidential inference and more memory capacity for large workloads.

The new units, TPU 8tx and TPU 8i Max, are part of the company’s eighth generation of chips introduced in April this year, and build on what those chips offered, which was more inference capacity and support for large training requirements.

TPU 8tx, designed with AMD, is based on the TPU 8t chip, and is geared specifically for confidentially training and running AI models, Google said in a blog post. The chip “builds security directly into the hardware layer, keeping sensitive data and model weights protected and isolated even while in active use,” the company wrote.

The chip also has a custom inferencing network that lets it run reasoning alongside training, so it can be used to distill and train small models on the fly. That ability, according to Google, could prove useful for cases where a company might want to train a model on its proprietary data without having to move the data between devices.

Meanwhile, TPU 8i Max basically combines two of the inference-focused TPU 8i chips the company launched in April, doubling the memory capacity, processing power and bandwidth, but in a single package. The result is a chip that lets AI models handle a larger range of information during queries.

The chip launch comes as hyperscalers like Microsoft, Amazon, and even AI labs like OpenAI and Anthropic are working on their own lines of AI-specific semiconductors and accompanying software in order to reduce their reliance on the dominance of Nvidia.

The news also comes at a particularly vulnerable moment for Nvidia, whose CEO Jensen Huang over the weekend admitted to having an affair with someone alleged to be a Chinese spy.

#452. Apple TV makes sign language interpretation a built-in accessibility feature

Apple TV is making its content more accessible to Deaf audiences with the launch of a new Sign Language Interpretation feature, the company announced on Tuesday. The feature will initially be available across a limited set of titles, starting this Friday with the release of “Being Heumann,” a biopic about disability rights activist Judy Heumann.

American Sign Language (ASL) and British Sign Language (BSL) will also be made available for Apple’s hit movie “F1.” Later this year, other titles will follow, including “Way of the Warrior Kid” and “Greyhound.”

What makes Apple’s offering different from other platforms is that the sign language version of the film is not a separate product — that is, it’s not a separate film designed just for Deaf viewers. Instead, Apple’s support for sign language is just a setting you enable that’s integrated with the main title, similar to how you could turn on or off closed captions.

When a title with the sign language option is playing, an interpreter appears in the bottom right of the screen, translating the spoken words into signs. Apple says it has partnered with a group of DASLs (Directors of Artistic Sign Language) to ensure that the interpreter for each film fits the tone of the film. Notably, the company is not using any AI-generated interpreters, as Apple says that’s not what the Deaf community has said it needs.

The feature is shipping in the latest release of iOS 27.2, and can be turned on through a new sign language option in Settings. After being enabled, a new button will appear in the supported Apple Original titles on Apple TV indicating that sign language is available.

These titles are labeled with a sign language icon that appears on the title page alongside other features like closed captions or audio descriptions. There’s also a new section under languages that will list the specific sign languages that are available with the given title.

To make the titles more discoverable, Apple TV will highlight which titles include the sign language option in a “Movies & Shows with American Sign Language” editorial collection. Meanwhile, users can easily surface supported titles via search by typing in keywords like “with ASL,” for example.

The new feature builds on Apple’s other investments in accessibility features, which it builds itself.

“At Apple, we believe everyone should be able to enjoy great stories and entertainment,” said Sarah Herrlinger, Apple’s senior director of Global Accessibility Policy and Initiatives, in a statement about the launch. “Bringing sign language interpretation to Apple TV is an exciting step forward in making the viewing experience more accessible and inclusive for Deaf audiences. Accessibility is a core value at Apple, and we’re proud to continue creating experiences that allow more people to enjoy the content they love,” she added.

A new app, Sundrop, aims to make those pictures more useful by turning them into weekly recaps, highlights, time lapses of the child growing up, and other cute scenes, like putting the child’s photo on a magazine cover or a rookie card.

While Google Photos and Apple Photos already provide extensive tools to organize and manage photos, Sundrop is taking aim at a particular niche: parents with a new baby (usually their first!) who take an overwhelming number of photos and want to do more with those pictures.

The app was developed over the past few months by the same team behind the read-it-later app, Matter: Ben Springwater, Sky Lan, and Rob Mackenzie (all dads themselves).

Scott Aaronson, one of the two researchers who posed the problem, writes that he doesn’t quite understand the proof but that he believes it’s correct because it was autoformalized in Lean, a coding language for math, and explained by a team of top researchers with whom PSI shared the solution.

The solution appears to close the door on one avenue quantum computers could follow to solve certain difficult problems at high speed, but not all of them. “It’s a negative result that allows people to know, okay, we’re no longer looking there,” Turturean said.

More broadly, it reflects the upheaval in the scientific world caused by AI problem-solving.

“A proof of full [Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture] was one of the last remaining open problems in quantum query complexity that everyone seemed to agree was centrally important,” Aaronson, who described himself feeling satisfied, awestruck, disappointed, and fatigued by the news, wrote. “I was surprised, honestly, to see it missing from OpenAI’s Mathocalypse dump a few weeks ago. “An effective kind of research system should have lots of things related to harnesses, lots related to tools, memory systems, simulation infrastructure,” PSI CEO Matt Pines told TechCrunch. “You want to be able to really go after a problem at breadth and depth, and be able to consider a wide range of potential alternatives.”

Hochul first announced the center in January with $55 million in donations and capital.

CVS Health plans to break itself up — and this time, the bet looks a lot more viable.

The healthcare conglomerate on Wednesday announced plans to split into two publicly traded companies by the first half of 2028. CVS would separate its pharmacy business – including roughly 9,000 retail locations – from its major insurance business and pharmacy benefit manager, Caremark.

The move comes more than two years after CVS considered a breakup but ultimately walked away from the idea. At the time, analysts questioned whether the retail pharmacy business could survive on its own.

“Back then, the idea of retail standing alone was unthinkable,” Brian Tanquilut, senior equity research analyst at Jefferies, told CNBC.

But the economics of the businesses have changed: CVS's retail pharmacy business has become more consistently profitable, while its insurer, Aetna, has improved its performance despite persistently high medical costs in Medicare Advantage plans. That could make it easier for the company to separate the units — and for investors to recognize the value of each on its own, rather than valuing them together as one sprawling healthcare conglomerate.

“For us, it's showing the stability of the pharmacy,” Michael Cherny, senior research analyst at Leerink Partners, said in an interview. “This business is a much different business from the one that's been considered three years ago, five years ago, 10 years ago, where there had been a litany of headwinds that the pharmacy just couldn't overcome.”

CVS CEO David Joyner told CNBC’s "Squawk on the Street" on Wednesday that back in 2024, the company was "in a turnaround" without the flexibility, cash or business performance to execute a split. Now, CVS has posted eight consecutive quarters of beating Wall Street earnings estimates and has raised its guidance several times over the last two years, thanks to improvement across its PBM, pharmacy and insurer.

Since taking the helm in 2024, Joyner has pursued an aggressive turnaround plan that involves $2 billion in cost cuts, store closures, leadership shakeups and efforts to rein in Medicare Advantage costs.

The company also now expects to generate at least $15.5 billion in operating cash flow in 2026, a roughly 70% increase from $9.1 billion in 2024, and says it has reached its leverage target ahead of schedule.

"What's changed in two years is the fact that we now can look at this business and the assets that are assembled and really determine, without any distress or without anything that we have to do, what is the right strategic decision to make," Joyner told analysts on a call Wednesday, adding that the decision comes "out of strength."

While investors have largely cheered CVS's progress in recent years, Wall Street appeared more pessimistic about Wednesday's announcement, as the company's shares dropped about 12%.

CVS's retail business has benefited from changes in the competitive landscape, including the widespread retrenchment and store closures of Walgreens, Rite Aid and other pharmacy operators over the last few years. That’s created opportunities for CVS to gain market share, some analysts told CNBC.

CVS has also introduced changes to its pharmacy reimbursement model. Its CostVantage program is designed to replace the traditional process with a more transparent, cost-based model. Some analysts say that has helped the company better align reimbursement with the cost of dispensing prescriptions and contributed to steadier profits.

Those developments represent a meaningful shift for a business that has faced years of pressure from declining prescription reimbursement, store closures and competition from online pharmacies and other retailers.

That doesn't mean the retail pharmacy has become a high-growth business. CVS's retail pharmacy segment remains a relatively low-margin operation, with adjusted operating margins of roughly 4% to 5%. But analysts say it may now be stable and profitable enough to operate independently – even if investors value it at a discount to CVS's other businesses.

In a note Wednesday, RBC Capital Markets analyst Ben Hendrix said the lower valuation investors assign to the retail pharmacy unit has weighed on CVS's overall value and obscured the earnings strength of businesses like Aetna and Caremark.

The underlying growth of the insurance and PBM businesses is also stronger than that of retail pharmacy, Tanquilut said, after accounting for changes to Caremark's rebate arrangements.

Aetna in particular has gotten a better handle on medical costs by exiting unprofitable markets, expanding value-based care models, and tightening utilization management.

The breakup also comes as Caremark faces mounting scrutiny over PBM practices, including state-level legal and regulatory challenges involving the businesses and their relationships with pharmacies.

Republicans in Tennessee and Arkansas have passed laws that would ban any one company from owning both a pharmacy and a PBM — thought the Arkansas measure has been held up in court. Cherny noted that legal hurdles involving PBMs in other states have continued to pile up.

“Through what seems to be a never-ending litany of state lawsuits, it feels like it's been under more pressure,” Cherny said. “It's unclear if it just became, for lack of a better term, too tenuous to move forward with those two entities combined as one. I’m not sure.”

Even so, Joyner told the Wall Street Journal that the company wasn’t splitting up because of regulatory issues. CVS has said the separation follows a strategic review and is intended to give each business greater focus and flexibility.

The move also marks a significant departure from the integrated healthcare strategy CVS spent years building.

CVS spent roughly $70 billion to acquire Aetna in 2018 and argued that combining health insurance, pharmacy benefit management, retail pharmacies and health providers under one roof would help coordinate care and create a competitive advantage. CVS was still promoting that model as recently as its investor day last year.

The proposed split suggests the company now sees more value in separating at least some of those businesses – though it is not abandoning vertical integration altogether.

Aetna and Caremark will remain together in the healthcare company, alongside CVS’s specialty pharmacy, its primary care clinic Oak Street Health and its home-visit provider Signify Health. The separate pharmacy company will include roughly 9,000 retail stores, mail pharmacy operations and MinuteClinic, a retail medical clinic located inside certain CVS locations.

Both sides are large enough that they can stand on their own, Cherny said. Caremark is one of the country's three largest PBMs, while CVS has one of the largest retail pharmacy footprints in the U.S.

But separating the company raises questions about how much of the commercial relationship between the businesses can survive.

The relationship has historically benefited both sides. As it stands, Caremark can use CVS's extensive retail footprint to offer members convenient access to prescriptions and steer them toward CVS locations through preferred pharmacy arrangements. Programs such as Maintenance Choice, for example, give eligible members the option to fill certain maintenance medications at CVS retail pharmacies or through mail order.

Those arrangements also helped direct prescription volume to CVS's stores, giving the retail pharmacy unit an important source of customers. Because the businesses shared a parent company, the financial benefits of that relationship ultimately went to CVS as a whole.

In a note, RBC's Hendrix also raised questions about how the split could impact Aetna's Medicare Advantage quality ratings, which determine whether health plans qualify for bonus payments from the government. Retail pharmacists have played a role in engaging Aetna members, and a separation could complicate that relationship.

But Joyner told analysts Wednesday that the synergy between the two businesses would be "preserved in commercial agreements between the two organizations," adding that "we don't need the integration of an operating structure."

Joyner said CVS will be working on those potential agreements over the next 15 to 18 months.

Preferred pharmacy networks are common across the industry, and separate ownership does not prevent Caremark from continuing to favor CVS pharmacies through negotiated arrangements, Cherny said. The difference is that under separate ownership, the profits from those arrangements would no longer accrue to a single company, he noted.

“The issue is not whether the companies can continue working together in some capacity, but whether any future contractual arrangements preserve the SAME competitive advantages and flexibility as today under common ownership,” Cherny said in an afternoon note on Wednesday.

Still, both businesses face challenges on their own.

Tanquilut cautioned that some of CVS's recent retail pharmacy gains have come as Walgreens and other competitors have closed stores, raising questions about whether the growth can be sustained. The business also remains exposed to consumer pressure and competition from online pharmacies.

Caremark, meanwhile, faces continued regulatory scrutiny and potential headwinds from changes to the federal 340B drug discount program, which requires drugmakers to offer discounted medications to eligible hospitals and other healthcare providers serving low-income and vulnerable patients.

CVS participates in the program by providing pharmacy services to those organizations, but tweaks to manufacturer data-sharing policies and potential reimbursement changes could weigh on that business in 2027, Tanquilut said.

Aetna, meanwhile, is still working through a multiyear plan to rebuild its margins, as it looks to improve the profitability of its Medicare Advantage business.

Ultimately, CVS is betting that the value of letting investors assess its businesses separately will outweigh the benefits of keeping them under one roof. The split will test whether each can thrive independently.

#399. Nvidia’s crisis has corporate governance experts reaching for the popcorn

Nvidia’s stock sank 5.47% on Tuesday, following a 4% drop on Monday, a loss of about 9.5% in two days, even though there wasn’t much news out of the company. CEO Jensen Huang has reportedly been trying to conduct business as usual after admitting to an extramarital affair with Alice Huang, whom U.S. intelligence agencies believe was spying for China. The biggest development: the board hired the law firm Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz to conduct an internal investigation.

What comes next is mostly a guessing game, even for the people who study how boards are supposed to handle crises. Two corporate governance professors, Ann Lipton of the University of Colorado and Todd Henderson of the University of Chicago, say there is no real precedent for a CEO caught up in a suspected honeypot. And while they have views on what the investigation should ask, like everyone else, they’re watching to see how it plays out.

“There have actually been affair cases and sexual harassment cases,” Lipton said, but was quick to add that she doesn’t know of previous cases that also involve potential national security compromises. Henderson, whose research interests include corporations, securities regulation, and law and economics, said much depends on facts few people have yet, including, most importantly, what, if anything, Alice Huang got out of Jensen Huang. (Secrets about Nvidia’s fabs or chip designs would matter to the Justice Department. Ordinary corporate information probably would not.)

Lipton described the investigation led by Nvidia’s independent board members, with their own outside counsel, as perhaps the most “standard” aspect of the whole affair so far. She also observed that an investigation will be “exactly as thorough” as the directors running it want it to be, which is also standard. Henderson made a related point about incentives, saying that Washington law firms that conduct internal investigations “know who pays their bills.” He didn’t suggest that’s happening here.

Lipton said she sees three lines of inquiry, including whether the law was broken, whether Huang violated Nvidia’s internal policies, and whether the board was misled, including how forthcoming he was and when he was on notice. It’s notable, as Wired reported Monday, that Huang hired a PR crisis firm known for handling sex scandals a full year ago. Of course, the driver could have been about the affair alone. It’s unclear when Huang learned Alice Huang was a suspected spy. The board said in a Sunday statement about the matter that Nvidia has “has no reason to believe that either national security or NVIDIA secrets were compromised.”

Henderson similarly stressed the importance of what Huang knew and when. He described two scenarios. In one, Huang hires a PR firm because an extramarital affair is embarrassing, without knowing who the woman is. In the other, he knows she is a suspected Chinese spy. “I think that’s very different,” he said. “If I were a board member and the CEO of a company with very sensitive technology was in that situation, I would be furious if they didn’t tell me.” Not telling the board that you have cancer “or that you’ve been sleeping with a Chinese spy,” said Henderson, “seems to me to be a fireable offense.”

Either way, Lipton expects shareholder suits, and she said two kinds are possible. Securities fraud, which she said is “only about lying,” is the most obvious, and as we reported yesterday, Nvidia is already facing a proposed class action lawsuit that alleges Nvidia and Huang misled investors by concealing Huang’s relationship with Alice Huang while describing dealings with her former employer as routine. “You can have as many affairs with Chinese spies as you like,” Lipton said. “You just can’t lie about it under the federal securities laws.”

A mismanagement claim under Delaware law, where Nvidia is incorporated, is much harder. Employees do “stupid or reckless or damaging things all the time,” she said, and courts don’t let shareholders sue each time. Shareholders bringing such a claim on the company’s behalf, known as a derivative suit, must first show the board is incapable of handling the problem, usually by proving directors are implicated or so beholden to the CEO that they can’t act independently.

Nvidia’s 10-member board has a track record of loyalty and deference to Huang, as outlined by a New York Times piece Tuesday morning. Still, the investigation those “independent” directors have announced with outside counsel makes that very hard. Lipton cited, for example, a shareholder suit over alleged mismanagement at McDonald’s several years ago, after its chief people officer was accused of harassment and its CEO had a relationship with a subordinate. Ultimately, the court found no reason the board couldn’t deal with the misconduct on its own.

Even if none of this ends up threatening Huang’s job, the episode raises other governance questions that don’t look great in retrospect. Nvidia is run with a famously flat structure, with more than 60 direct reports to Huang, and the company doesn’t appear to have a succession plan, which Lipton called “a danger for any company.”

Huang’s children also work at Nvidia. His son Spencer is a director of product management, his daughter Madison is a senior director of product marketing, and his son-in-law, Nico Caprez, works in business development. Some employees believe he is grooming successors, and asked last month by Bloomberg whether Madison is in line for the job, Huang didn’t shoot down the idea, saying instead that it’s not something he’d wish on her.

Lipton called hiring the kids poor corporate hygiene, even though founders have long done it. (Kimbal Musk is on Tesla’s board because he’s Elon Musk’s brother, she noted.) Directors are supposed to act only for stockholders, but founders get more rope as long as the company is thriving.

Henderson similarly calls himself a strong “anti-nepotism person,” sharing that when his father worked at Westinghouse’s nuclear research lab, there was an absolute ban on hiring employees’ children. “Like, fine for them to be working there,” he said of Huang’s children working at Nvidia. But “the idea that one of his kids would succeed him” at a publicly traded company the size of Nvidia “seems crazy,” he added.

What Nvidia may have gotten more right, from a governance standpoint, is what it doesn’t have, which is dual-class shares. Huang reportedly owns about 3.6%, so he doesn’t control the vote. That makes him “easier to dislodge,” Lipton said, even though he is deeply integrated into the company in other ways.

Neither professor is rooting for or against Huang, each made clear. The simple truth is that with voting control on top of Huang’s holdings, the board would have even less flexibility. Without it, the board has a few more options.

#371. Consumers are spending on physical gifts even as travel tops wish lists, Visa survey finds

Consumers are hoping for more travel and experiences this holiday season, but physical gifts continue to win the lion's share of their wallets, according to a new report from Visa on Thursday.

The report found that more than half of survey respondents are putting travel and live events at the top of their wish lists. Still, only 15% of those respondents are prioritizing spending on experiences for others, while 27% of them plan to favor physical gifts.

That gap between what shoppers want and what they plan to buy is largely due to higher travel costs, lower consumer sentiment and an emphasis on value, according to Michael Brown, Visa's principal U.S. economist.

"There is some interesting trade-offs there that we're seeing on the part of consumers," Brown told CNBC. "If I'm traveling more, I'm probably going to be gifting those physical goods less, so this is really the broader context."

The survey was conducted among roughly 4,660 U.S. adults ages 18 and older, designed to be representative of the broader population of consumers.

Brown said budget pressure also factors into traveling for the holidays, as Visa expects more people to celebrate the holidays "a little bit closer to home" than in the past given rising airfares and general inflation. In September, consumer prices rose 3.7% annually, driven higher by surging energy costs.

Airlines have hiked the price of plane tickets this year as their fuel costs have risen by billions of dollars due to the war in the Middle East. And even if fuel prices drop, executives have said fares are likely to stay high, with fares up more than 22% in September compared with a year earlier, according to the consumer price index.

Those trends mean consumers will likely be more intentional about where they're spending, especially as inflation drives up prices across the board.

"Consumers are aspirational. They want those experiences, they want to travel, they want to be out in the world," Brown said. "However, the economic reality is with prices rising, with economic uncertainty, they need to balance the reality of today's economic environment with those aspirations."

The report also found that only households with an income above $200,000 spent more on trips departing between Nov. 1 and early next year compared with 2025 into 2026. On the other hand, many other income groups pulled back on their travel spending.

"If you think about that segment, a lot of them own equities, and the equity market runup over the last several months has created a lot of positivity among that specific affluent demographic, and I think that's one of the factors that continues to motivate even aggregate macro spend today," Brown said.

Meanwhile, less affluent shoppers are placing an emphasis on value. Visa's consumer survey from October showed that roughly one-third of consumers with an income below $100,000 are going to switch to lower-cost brands or stores when shopping for holiday gifts this season.

Though Visa is projecting a 4.5% year-over-year increase in holiday sales growth, much of that figure is due to inflation. Without those price increases, sales are expected to rise just 1.1% this year, down from the 1.3% increase during the 2025 holiday season.

Ultimately, Brown said the survey findings may be encouraging for the broader retail scene this holiday season. With less emphasis on travel and more projected spending on physical gifts, it could boost the sector at a time when spending is under pressure.

At the same time, he said that consumers may just be expecting to spend more this holiday season because they've become accustomed to the higher prices.

"But, of course, consumers are internalizing higher prices of things, and I think that's why they're reporting higher anticipated spend this year," Brown said. "I think these numbers just need to be looked at through the lens of the inflation and particularly consumers' perception of where inflation is going to go. I think that weighed heavily on consumer psychology this season."
