#462. Measles is surging in the U.S. — and outbreaks can cost millions

Measles outbreaks aren't just a public health threat. They can also be a financial burden on the U.S. — and the cost could only deepen as the once-eliminated disease spreads more widely.

The U.S. is expected to lose its official measles elimination status following a review by the Pan American Health Organization this week, after cases ratcheted to recent highs this year.

More than 5,100 measles cases have been reported nationwide in 2026 as of early November, marking the country's worst outbreak in more than three decades. Eight deaths associated with the disease have been confirmed by states so far in 2026: six in Pennsylvania, one in New York and one in Wisconsin.

Based on this year's case count, a new tool developed by researchers at Johns Hopkins University estimates an economic cost of nearly $83 million for the outbreaks. That total includes $56 million in public health response costs, almost $25 million in lost productivity and more than $1.8 million in direct medical expenses.

The tool also estimates that 2025 measles outbreaks in the U.S. — which involved 2,289 confirmed cases nationwide – carried an economic cost of more than $37 million.

The costs extend beyond treating people who become sick, accounting for the resources health departments spend investigating and containing outbreaks, as well as wages lost when people have to miss work. The tool uses more recent, state-specific cost and financing data than an initial study by the researchers, which relied on historical outbreak costs.

The estimates from the tool are not a definitive accounting of what the outbreaks actually cost. But it illustrates the hefty bill tied to the spread of a disease that can be prevented through vaccination, and the potential economic consequences of falling childhood immunization rates. CNBC previously reported on how the return of measles in the U.S. exposes weaknesses in the nation's public health system that raise concerns about the return of other eliminated diseases.

"We're starting to see a whole bunch of people who decided not to get vaccinated as kids or have never gotten exposed to measles because they benefited from herd immunity, who now make it into adulthood and risk potentially getting quite seriously ill and requiring very significant medical expenses, or dying at the hospital," Bryan Patenaude, a health economist at the International Vaccine Access Center at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health, who helped develop the tool, said in an interview.

Measles outbreaks can trigger a costly public health response, even when many people who are exposed never become sick.

When a case is identified, public health officials must investigate the infection, identify and notify people who may have been exposed, coordinate with hospitals and schools, and sometimes organize vaccination campaigns to prevent further spread. Those efforts can divert staff and resources from other public health priorities.

Costs could differ heavily depending on the state.

Johns Hopkins' data includes national averages alongside state-specific inputs, including inpatient hospital costs and wages from government data or trusted health policy sources. In New York, for example, the average inpatient hospital stay is roughly $4,200 a day, while the minimum wage is $16 per hour.

The tool estimates that the 48 cases reported by New York in 2025 carried an estimated $1.1 million in costs, including nearly $749,000 in public health responses, $333,000 in productivity losses and roughly $24,500 in direct medical expenses.

Meanwhile, the 803 cases in Texas, the epicenter of the largest U.S. measles outbreak in 2025, carried estimated costs of nearly $13 million, according to the tool. That includes $9 million in public health costs, $4 million in productivity losses and around $288,000 in direct medical expenses.

The tool builds on a study published by Patenaude and other researchers in August, which examined the costs of measles outbreaks in the U.S. The researchers reviewed published studies and government reports to compile historical estimates of medical expenses and public health response costs across 18 states.

The paper estimated that outbreaks carried a historical average cost of nearly $59,000 per case, just from the public health response. The researchers also estimated how costs scale with outbreak size, separating fixed up-front response expenses from the additional costs associated with each new case.

"A lot of this is incurred up front, and that's because of the human labor that's involved in contact tracing and all of the different actors that sort of need to be mobilized to deal with the fact that there's an outbreak," Patenaude told CNBC. "It's a largely labor-driven cost."

That model estimated roughly $298,000 in fixed costs and about $16,300 for each additional case. But it relied on historical data, including a particularly large 2019 outbreak in New York City, where contact-tracing labor costs were high. The researchers also excluded productivity losses from that model because most of the historical studies they reviewed did not consistently account for them.

The economic burden of measles extends beyond the costs of treating infected patients and organizing a public health response.

People who are exposed to measles may have to quarantine, even if they never develop symptoms. Parents can also be forced to miss work to care for sick or exposed children, while workers who contract the disease may lose wages during their recovery.

A 2025 study by Joey Mattingly, a pharmacist and health economist at the University of Utah, used published epidemiological and cost data to model the potential economic burden of a hypothetical 1,500-case outbreak. His analysis included direct medical expenses, public health spending and lost productivity, and examined how the estimates changed under different assumptions, including hospitalization rates.

The study estimated that the hypothetical outbreak could cost about $90 million, including roughly $26 million in productivity losses.

Those indirect costs can add up quickly because measles is highly contagious and a single infected person can expose dozens of others. Those contacts may need to quarantine or miss work while health officials determine whether they have been infected, creating costs even when they never develop the disease.

Mattingly estimated that each person who is quarantined could amount to roughly $300 in lost productivity alone.

If a single measles case exposes 40 to 50 people who then need to quarantine, that could translate into an estimated $12,000 to $15,000 in lost productivity, even if none of those contacts become sick.

That ripple effect is one reason Mattingly argues that the economic costs of outbreaks can quickly outweigh the investment required to vaccinate people against measles.

"Even if you don't get the infection, the contact costs you," he said. "And so it's just showing that there's this multiplication or exponential growth on the economic side that happens."

The financial burden can also fall unevenly across communities. While the costs of responding to an outbreak may be shared among local, state and federal agencies, Mattingly said the impact is often felt directly by local health departments.

"It's not like your health department is sitting around waiting for measles outbreaks," he said. "They're working, doing all these other things, and imagine having to just drop what you're doing because there's been a huge outbreak in the community."

Patenaude said the findings could help state and local health departments better anticipate the resources needed to contain outbreaks — particularly as measles cases continue to rise.

"It's not super expensive to treat and deal with a single case of measles compared to a single case of cancer," he said. "But if you have these outbreaks popping up and you're having to constantly divert resources that could be spent elsewhere by state and local health departments on measles, then you're going to wind up spending a lot of money very quickly."

#458. As decision model use skyrockets, is customization the next frontier?

In October, more than one in four requests on Vercel’s AI model router went to decision models, not LLMs — an enormous share of work for a kind of computerized intelligence that was invented only a month earlier.

Decision models are LLMs that don’t produce output tokens; instead, they’re tuned to use their general intelligence to quickly and cheaply decide between preset options, while registering its level of confidence in the choice. They’re useful in production environments, where fast decisions can be more important than the reasoning provided by a slower and more expensive frontier model.

TypeSafe became a $7.5 billion company after releasing its Jev family of models, which continues to dominate usage (at least where we can see metrics from services like OpenRouter, which handled more than 1.5 billion queries to TypeSafe’s models last week).

However, it’s easy to make a decision model, and dozens were released in the wake of Jev. One Amazon engineer made one in a weekend, and soon every hyperscaler had its own option. On Tuesday, Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella said that his company is rolling out its decision model, Microsoft-Decision-1.1, for wide use on Azure immediately.

The action is happening on both ends of the ecosystem: Sqwish, a U.K.-based AI startup behind a decision model called kettei fat, announced a $3 million pre-seed round today. Co-founders Ushnish Sengupta and Federica Freddi, who met as Cambridge grad students, had been developing small LLMs when Jev arrived on the scene, and they realized their architectures could be repurposed as a powerful decision model.

Data appears to be the key to a successful model in this category — TypeSafe CEO Diogo Almeida told TechCrunch that the hardest part of building Jev was finely calibrating its judgment using statistically-adjusted synthetic data. Sqwish, too, has been working with synthetic data, but its unique offering is built-in post-training tools that use a customer’s own data to improve the model’s judgment.

“What makes us not a commodity, I think, is the fact that we are offering our customers the same power…they can keep uploading their production data, and just the same way that we trained our models, their models can also keep getting better at the tasks that they care about,” CEO Ushnish Sengupta told TechCrunch.

Attempting to rank decision models is difficult; Sqwish’s “kettei fat” model has performed well on some benchmarks, but most builders of the technology say that the real proof is in the pudding: It’s generally simple for developers to sub in one model for another and see how it does in a specific context.

“The benchmark is more of an invitation to try the model out, and then the continual tuning is the reason why they should stick to us,” Sengupta said.

The Microsoft-Decision-1.1 model is also the result of the company carefully balancing across cost, latency, intelligence and calibration. Achint Srivastava, the engineering VP who led the project, agreed that too much focus on benchmarks is a mistake.

“What we see is that ‘benchmaxed’ models fall flat under real enterprise usage very quickly,” Srivastava wrote in an email. “Our testing underscores just how challenging it is to deliver accuracy, reliability, speed and cost efficiency in a single model. Microsoft-Decision-1.1 and Jev are among the few that perform strongly across all four dimensions, even when evaluated on ‘held out’ or unseen benchmarks reflective of real use cases.”

For startups like Sqwish, competition with hyperscalers and frontier labs is an opportunity. Freddi predicts that future systems will see so-called System One intelligence, like decision models, working in concert with System Two intelligence, like frontier LLMs.

“Even in that future, System One is maybe a $10 billion, $50 billion [opportunity], and OpenAI is doing this IPO with you know $1 trillion, $2 trillion market value in mind,” she told TechCrunch. “This is all we care about; whereas for them, this is just a side project.”

#445. JetBlue unveils new first-class seats to bring back profits

JetBlue Airways unveiled its new domestic first-class seats Wednesday as more of the country's carriers bet on the premium travel boom to make them profitable again.

JetBlue announced it would offer a domestic first class — BlueFirst — in December 2024 and plans to start flying aircraft with the new seats and full on-board product next month. The carrier showed off the new cabin at its home hub at John F. Kennedy International Airport on Wednesday. Retrofits will continue during 2027, CEO Joanna Geraghty told CNBC late last month.

JetBlue will have 12 of the seats on its Airbus A320 fleet and 30 extra-legroom seats, down from 42 of those options previously. Because the airplanes don't have ovens, JetBlue said it is offering an array of cold food options like sesame noodles, pastrami sandwiches and tortellini salad, though they vary by route.

The rollout comes as the New York airline tries to stabilize after last posting a profit in 2019. Last month, JetBlue executives sought to calm investors after shares in the airline hit a record low following a report that bondholders had contracted restructuring advisors.

"We have no intention, no plans to file for bankruptcy. We are not engaged with any restructuring company," Geraghty told CNBC in an interview on Oct. 27.

Geraghty told CNBC's Phil LeBeau on Wednesday that JetBlue has "met with a few of the creditors over the last couple of weeks as part of ... normal investor engagement." She said the company made it clear to bondholders that JetBlue doesn't need to reorganize.

The airline's shares are up more than 40% this month, more than other passenger carriers, as of midday Wednesday, though they are still off about 11% so far this year.

JetBlue is betting that resilient travel demand, especially for roomier seats will help fuel its turnaround plan, which aims to get the carrier to an operating profit next year and $1 in adjusted earnings per share in 2028.

Airline executives have said they expect fares to stay elevated next year as they grapple with higher costs, including but not limited to fuel, such as labor and airport fees. Geraghty said Wednesday that even if fuel prices moderate next year, "I don't think fares are going to fall as much as people expect."

For example, a ticket in the new recliner first-class seats between Kennedy airport and Fort Lauderdale-Hollywood International Airport in Florida is going for $847 roundtrip in the first week of March while standard economy is $446.

On Tuesday, budget carrier Frontier Airlines unveiled its new first-class seats, which will take up the first two rows, and said it would outfit its Airbus fleet with premium seating that has extra legroom and in-seat power through row eight. The airline will take out a row of seating on its planes to make the pricier ones fit, Chief Marketing Officer Bobby Schroeter told CNBC in an interview Monday.

JetBlue last month reported that it ended the third quarter with $2.2 billion in liquidity and that its unencumbered asset base is now $7 billion, including $2.8 billion in aircraft and engines.

JetBlue had total debt of $8.4 billion as of Sept. 30, the carrier said in its earnings filing.

Geraghty told CNBC last month there is a "fundamental disconnect between the narrative in the bond market and what our financial results actually demonstrate."

When asked whether JetBlue's eye on keeping costs down includes furloughs, layoffs or buyouts, Geraghty last month said "absolutely not."

#438. Waymo suspends freeway rides nationwide after California DMV order

Waymo robotaxis are no longer allowed to operate on California freeways after the state’s Department of Motor Vehicles restricted the Alphabet-owned company’s operations following several incidents that raised safety concerns.

California’s DMV, which regulates the testing and deployment of autonomous vehicles in the state, issued the operational restrictions on Waymo on November 11. However, the restrictions weren’t made public until Tuesday. San Francisco Business Times was the first to report the order.

The DMV confirmed the new restrictions to TechCrunch, stating that the department took action after three Waymo autonomous vehicles entered an active construction closure on U.S. Route 101 near Burlingame, and another separate incident involving California Highway Patrol vehicles on Highway 280 in San Francisco.

Waymo has complied and is preventing its robotaxis from accessing freeways in any of the markets it operates in, including those outside of California. Waymo currently has commercial operations in 15 U.S. cities.

The DMV said Waymo’s freeway operations pose an imminent hazard to public safety.

“The Department determined that Waymo’s driverless freeway operations pose an imminent hazard to public safety due to recurring incursions by its vehicles into active construction zones where construction workers, vehicles and equipment are present, despite a prior restriction and corrective actions implemented following similar incidents that occurred on May 18, 2026,” according to an emailed statement sent to TechCrunch.

The DMV said the restrictions will remain in place until Waymo can demonstrate that it has taken “effective corrective actions” to address the deficiencies.

Waymo spokesperson Dana Gallagher told TechCrunch the company has temporarily restricted freeway operations across its other operating markets to evaluate its performance.

“Safety is at the foundation of everything we do,” Gallagher said in a statement. “Following recent freeway construction zone encounters on U.S. 101 in California that did not involve any injuries or collisions, the California DMV has implemented an operating restriction on freeway operations in the state. In alignment with our safety-first principles, we have also temporarily restricted freeway operations across our other operating markets to evaluate our performance. Our ride-hailing service continues to operate safely on surface streets, serving over half a million trips each week. We are working with the California DMV and other state officials diligently and responsibly to resume freeway operations.”

Waymo began offering freeway rides in late 2025, a commercial milestone that has been central to its expansion in major metro areas. Freeway access allows Waymo to connect riders to airports and other destinations more efficiently, slashing travel times by avoiding slower surface streets.

That has been particularly important in the San Francisco Bay Area because Waymo’s service area stretches from the city south to Silicon Valley. Freeway access has dramatically shortened trips across the Peninsula that previously took 45 minutes or more than an hour.

In May, Waymo abruptly stopped operating on highways. The company didn’t cite a specific reason at the time, but several social media posts showed Waymo robotaxis driving through traffic cones and struggling to navigate construction zones.

Waymo robotaxis returned to freeways in July after the company said it updated the self-driving software system to improve performance around freeway construction zones.

#399. China dominates battery graphite, but this startup could break the monopoly

Electric vehicle sales in the U.S. may have leveled off, but the market for batteries inside them isn’t slowing down, quintupling in the last five years. Many of those are made in the U.S., but they’re largely dependent on China for key ingredients, including graphite.

Graphite is a key ingredient in lithium-ion batteries, where it forms the anode, the part that stores the battery’s charge. Chinese companies control about three-quarters of the graphite anode supply chain and 97% of synthetic (human-made) graphite anode production, according to Benchmark Mineral Intelligence. One five-year-old startup, Molten Industries, is working to break that dominance.

Molten Industries has started operating a demonstration plant in Oakland meant to prove the technology works, the company exclusively told TechCrunch. The plant is will pave the way for U.S.-made graphite to win a share of the market.

“The new plant will be producing graphite that’s at cost parity with China plus tariffs,” Caleb Boyd, co-founder and CTO of Molten Industries, told TechCrunch. In other words, once U.S. import taxes are added to Chinese graphite, Molten’s price would match it. He said future plants should match China’s cost even without tariffs, which he called a built-in advantage of the technology.

Molten’s Oakland plant will make about 300 metric tons of graphite annually, which is enough for 300 megawatt-hours of batteries. That’s enough for about 4,300 EVs. The bulk of the output will go to undisclosed customers that are testing it in their battery cells.

The next facility, which Molten has yet to announce, will take the same template used in the Oakland plant and replicate it several times over, said Boyd.

The startup’s technology began in a Silicon Valley garage. It uses electrical resistive heating, which passes electricity through a material to heat it, to split methane (the main ingredient in natural gas) into pure carbon and hydrogen. “You can think of it almost like a massive toaster,” Boyd said. The company says the system uses three times less electricity than existing methods, and it runs on natural gas, which is cheap and plentiful in the U.S.

“We think it’s very hard to compete with China if you’re just copying their processes and trying to compete on cost,” said Kevin Bush, who co-founded Molten Industries with Boyd and is the company’s CEO. “So we are using a resource in the United States that’s abundant in natural gas, and we’ve brought some innovation to actually be able to drive down the cost of graphite production globally.”

Here’s how the chemistry works: When methane (CH4) is heated without oxygen, each molecule breaks into one carbon atom and four hydrogen atoms. The hydrogen atoms pair up into hydrogen gas (H2), which Molten sells at market prices to a customer next door. The carbon is then processed to meet battery makers’ requirements.

The basic idea of using heat to separate methane has been around for over a century, but early attempts to commercialize it ran into trouble. Carbon built up and clogged the reactor, and Boyd said no company before Molten had been able to produce high-quality graphite this way.

Solving that problem changed the company’s direction. Boyd and Bush — both materials science PhDs from Stanford — originally set out to make clean hydrogen from natural gas. Most hydrogen today comes from steam reforming, a cheap but polluting process. The two wanted to reap the benefits of a low-cost feedstock like natural gas without the pollution, and they expected to sell the leftover carbon as a byproduct.

But as they experimented with ways to keep the reactor clean, “we actually started making this really high quality graphite,” Boyd said, which “changed the entire economics of what we were doing.” Molten went from a hydrogen company that happened to sell graphite to a battery-grade graphite company that happens to sell hydrogen.

The company says both businesses could bring in billions of dollars. If Molten supplied the entire U.S. battery market, it could also provide about a third of the hydrogen used in the country, the company claims. Other uses for high-quality graphite, like thermal batteries that store energy as heat, could expand both markets further.

The new factory, Boyd said, “is really a milestone, not just for the company, we think, but globally.”

#396. Feds arrest top official of healthcare company, alleging illegal drug importation

U.S. authorities are accusing the two top officials of a Canadian company of operating a long-running scheme to illegally import prescription drugs into the U.S., according to court records filed Tuesday.

John Thomas Howard, treasurer and a director of the company, CANARX, was arrested Sunday on smuggling and conspiracy charges, according to the records.

Bob Howard, his brother, who is president and a director of CANARX, is also wanted in the federal investigation, according to an affidavit from Dustin Dobbs, a special agent with the Food and Drug Administration's Office of Criminal Investigations. He has not yet been taken into custody.

Besides conspiracy and smuggling, the arrest warrant said John Thomas Howard was charged with introducing misbranded drugs into interstate commerce and illegal drug importation. No plea has been filed.

CANARX was featured in a CNBC investigation last year that revealed how the company and others known as alternative funding programs, or AFPs, are becoming more pervasive around the U.S. as drug costs skyrocket. The investigation found these companies have penetrated the country's healthcare system by offering high-cost specialty drugs at more affordable prices through private and public employers. In some cases, employers are requiring their staff to use an AFP.

AFPs often obtain the medications overseas and ship them directly to patients, a practice that federal authorities have said is illegal and poses safety risks. CNBC traced the trail of drugs from abroad to the U.S. via a supply chain that the pharmaceutical companies producing the medications have not authorized.

On its website, CANARX, founded in 2002 and based in Windsor, Ontario, Canada, described its business as the first international prescription provider for the U.S. and the safest such program in the industry. The company said it sources its medications from Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia and New Zealand.

But the affidavit said that the FDA's investigation found "there is probable cause to believe that (CANARX) illegally imported or caused the illegal importation of prescription drugs into the United States …."

The affidavit does not specify which medications the agency believes were illegally imported. The criminal complaint covers the period from Feb. 22, 2017, through April 15, 2026.

The company's general counsel, Joseph Morris, told CNBC in a statement: "The Howards deny all the charges against them, will defend vigorously, and expect to be vindicated in court."

The company has been under FDA scrutiny for several years. In 2023, the agency seized medication being shipped by CANARX that it says was potentially misbranded and counterfeit, according to an email sent to a patient that was obtained by CNBC.

And in 2019, the FDA sent a warning letter to the company, saying it was operating "in a manner that substitutes the FDA-approved drugs prescribed by the U.S. healthcare provider with unapproved drugs." Morris, in response to that warning, argued CANARX was not misleading patients, but agreed for the company to stop importing drugs that need to be administered by a doctor.

In an interview as part of the CNBC investigation, Morris said last year that the medicines shipped to U.S. patients come from highly regulated countries. He said CANARX was operating legally.

The central question for AFPs is the legality of importing prescription drugs into the U.S. for personal use. The FDA allows Americans to import medications for individual use in some cases if the drug is not available domestically. That is the basis on which the AFPs built their models for shipping medicines directly to patients.

But an FDA spokesperson told CNBC last year that "in most circumstances, a person cannot import prescription drugs from other countries into the U.S. ... as substitutes for FDA-approved drugs."

In a separate civil lawsuit that is pending, Gilead Sciences accused CANARX and other companies of illegally importing Biktarvy, its medication to treat HIV.

In a statement to CNBC on Wednesday, a Gilead spokesperson said the company "has taken strong legal action to protect patients and the integrity of the U.S. medicines supply chain. We welcome the government's efforts to hold accountable those whose conduct may put patients at risk."

CANARX has not responded to the Gilead lawsuit.

The technology also shows where the extracted information came from in the original document, so businesses can check the results rather than take the AI’s output at face value.

“The problem with LLMs is they’re inherently probabilistic,” said Unsiloed co-founder and CEO Aman Mishra (pictured above, right) in an interview. “There is going to be some deviation induced. There is going to be some hallucination involved, and that has a massive negative impact downstream.”

Mishra co-founded Unsiloed with Adnan Abbas (pictured above, left), his former classmate at the Indian Institute of Technology (IIT) Kharagpur. The two joined Y Combinator’s Fall 2025 batch after spending some time developing the technology.

Unsiloed is already working with customers in finance, legal services, and healthcare, Mishra told TechCrunch. Mishra told TechCrunch that Unsiloed’s advantage lies in how it processes complex documents. “In one of the streams you have the text tokens, in the other stream you have the exact image tokens,” Mishra said. “This is something that we have spent a lot of time architecting.”

The technology then directs more computing power toward areas such as figures, numbers, and charts. Unsiloed also uses a specialized decoder that takes industry-specific terminology into account, helping it distinguish between terms that can mean different things in different business contexts.

Supporting those claims, Mishra pointed to a test involving 542 financial documents supplied by an enterprise customer. Unsiloed achieved 99.78% accuracy across roughly 84,000 extracted data points, per a technical brief shared by the startup. The test compared Unsiloed with major AI models, Microsoft’s Azure Document Intelligence, and the customer’s in-house extraction tool. The results have, however, not been independently verified.

Currently, Unsiloed has a headcount of seven people. The startup, Mishra said, plans to use the fresh funding to hire more researchers and engineers, expand its sales efforts and develop proprietary datasets to improve its technology.

#335. Retailers are turning to entertainment to win shoppers’ attention

Legacy retailer Gap Inc. made an unconventional hire earlier this year: a chief entertainment officer.

It was a newly created role as the company tapped former Paramount executive Pam Kaufman to helm its entertainment, content and licensing platform. Gap has since rolled out major campaigns, partnering with bands like Just Your Type and building on the success of last year's ads with girl group Katseye.

"Beloved brands have value beyond the products they sell. Consumers identify with them, trust them and want to be part of their world," Kaufman told CNBC in a statement. "The key is authenticity: Every extension has to reflect what the brand stands for and give its fans a meaningful new way to experience it."

She said it's part of a broader goal to emphasize what Gap calls "fashiontainment," a strategy to blend retail and entertainment to better align with consumers. Gap is part of a growing cohort of retailers investing in entertainment and experiences both in-store and through exclusive online content to drive engagement.

"Brands are stories, and we are the storytellers," CEO Richard Dickson said at the Goldman Sachs Global Consumer and Retail conference in September. "I think you could see that come to life when you look at some of the work that we've done, putting our brands in the center of culture and cultural conversation."

In another blending of retail and media, Mattel made waves a few years ago for its "Barbie" movie, building on the intellectual property of a decades-old fan favorite and drawing in massive box office success. At the time, Dickson was president and chief operating officer of the company, witnessing firsthand the sectors' intersection before departing for Gap.

"I think the post-Covid consumer attitude around shopping certainly evolved a lot," said Bryan Eshelman, who leads the retail practice at consulting firm AlixPartners. "It has gone from shopping as a fulfillment task to shopping, in many instances, more as an entertainment form."

Between experiences in physical stores and media campaigns featuring exclusive content, Eshelman said, retailers are getting more creative about the ways they're driving engagement and marketing.

"Community, at the end of the day, is one of the key ways to drive loyalty, so I think retailers of all types are trying to tap into some sense of community, some sense of lifestyle, some sense of belonging that's beyond the product that they offer," he said.

Branded entertainment has been around for a long time. Procter & Gamble made headlines in the 1930s for helping to coin the term "soap opera," and companies have for decades created media campaigns to draw in customers.

But with the rise of social media, content creation and short-form offerings, experts said audiences are becoming hungrier than ever for something new and original.

The retail and entertainment intersection is happening at a time when intellectual property is becoming more valuable than ever, according to experts.

As consumers encounter more marketing than ever, the companies that will win the battle for attention are the ones that can "cut through all of the noise," said Alex Michael, senior managing director at investment firm LionTree.

"The hardest thing to find and to get is audience these days," Michael told CNBC. "IP, in that sense, is the most valuable thing out there, and only becoming more valuable because it's attached to audience."

At the same time, he said, the way IP is distributed is broadening to include video, audio, short-form, long-form and live event content. Consumers are also redefining their loyalty to brands, which means companies have to "play much earlier in the consumer journey" than they have in years past to secure their trust, he said.

The trend is also likely helped by executives who have worked in both retail and entertainment: Gap's Kaufman came from Paramount, for example, and Dickson and Skydance co-CEO Ynon Kreiz held top roles at Mattel.

"The gray areas have never been more pronounced as to what is classically defined as retail or media or entertainment or live events," Michael said. "People who have cross-sector expertise are increasingly more valuable because you can't be defined by a single vertical, especially as things change so quickly."

Still, there's room for newer players, especially with the studio landscape rapidly changing, said Sean Akaks, the CEO of SonderCo, an agency that partners brands with celebrities and entertainment properties.

"I think it's a really interesting time for nontraditional players to be making content because you're seeing this massive consolidation of these legacy entertainment companies," he said. "I think there are just more open lanes for independent creators and also brands and anyone outside the traditional studio system to create content."

That's where retailers might tap creators, Akaks said, because they can use their platforms to reach consumers directly.

"I do think there's a world where we're going to see creators and brands sort of co-creating IP that maybe they're owning together," he said.

Once those companies get consumers into the store, they're also leaning into experiential retail, making shopping a more immersive experience than it otherwise would be, like Dick's Sporting Goods offering interactive activities and simulators in its stores.

According to data from AlixPartners, companies investing in experiential retail such as Coach and Ralph Lauren saw close to 10% year-over-year increases in consumers' average basket size.

"The idea that retailers would potentially invest in some distributed electronic content to help drive traffic, and then when you get there, it's experiential — it really goes into the kinds of flywheels that entertainment companies are also trying to build and grow," said Jeff Goldstein, co-lead of the media and entertainment practice at AlixPartners.

Yet, the entertainment that retail brands are developing is only one piece in a larger puzzle to retain customers and attract new ones. Translating that into tangible sales growth isn't necessarily a given.

"The basics of having great product in stock at a transparent price that feels like value is not swept away by animatronics," AlixPartners' Eshelman said. "The fundamentals are still there, and if you're going to have a good retail proposition, you need to have that balance. You can't mask bad product at a bad price with no inventory with glitz and glamour."

This holiday season, big-ticket items are expected to be in short supply with diesel prices near records, trucking rates close to a post-pandemic level and carrying costs hitting multidecade highs. All of that is forcing many retailers to rethink their holiday inventory strategy.

"We are going to have some of the lowest inventory levels on some of these big-ticket items we have seen in history," said Deborah Weinswig, CEO of retail analytics firm Coresight Research.

Weinswig said retailers she is working with are holding less inventory of big-screen TVs, grills, large consumer appliances like washers and dryers, and other items that take either big amounts of space or cost a lot of money to hold.

"It's freight getting it from there to here; it's carrying costs; then it's just the trucking to get it into the stores," Weinswig said. "We're talking three different pieces right here and each is up significantly year on year."

The surge pricing for supply chain services is being caused by domestic and international policy.

According to Evercore ISI, the rates for general trucking have increased more than 47% year over year during the week of Nov. 2.

Industry experts say a large factor has been a crackdown by the federal government on non-citizen truck drivers and non-U.S.-based truck drivers operating in the U.S. That has created a capacity crunch that has raised rates and is expected to continue to create upward pressure.

At the same time, fuel surcharges are spiking.

Most transportation providers charge customers an extra fuel surcharge to offset changes in the cost of diesel fuel. That surcharge has spiked 64% year over year, according to Evercore ISI data, as diesel prices have hit new record highs due in part to the U.S.-Israel war with Iran.

And inventory holding costs — or the total expense of storing unsold goods, which could include the costs for capital, service, storage and other risk factors — are closely tied to the yield on the 10-year Treasury note, which exceeded 5% in October and remains at multiyear highs in November.

Collectively, the higher costs could mean bigger discounts for shoppers willing to wait, according to Bill Catania, CEO of logistics technology company OneRail, which powers same-day delivery for companies such as FedEx and Lowe's.

"You're going to see more discount and price promotions as we get closer to the holiday to liquidate inventory, so they're not having to transfer it or dispose of it or position it some other way," Catania said.

Retailers are also facing a conundrum in their holiday inventory strategy when it comes to shipping costs versus opportunity costs. In other words, is it worth it to stock up now, or wait it out in hopes that shipping rates will come down some but potentially miss out on having the right product mix?

It's leading to a wait-and-see approach for stocking shelves after early holiday sales in October, executives said.

UPS CEO Carol Tomé said on the company's earnings call on Oct. 27 that it's "just starting to get peak season forecasts from our customers."

Similarly, Derek Leathers, CEO of Werner Enterprises, a trucker for big-box stores and major retailers, said retailers are cautious.

"When rates are higher and the cost of capital is more expensive, you are going to have a much more intense focus on not having excess inventory," Leathers said.

Still, 2026 holiday sales are expected to top $1 trillion as inflation drives growth and customer spending is expected to remain healthy, according to Bain & Company's annual forecast. Leathers said even with the surge in trucking and shipping rates, he's seeing that retailers are willing to pay up to stock up.

"The bigger concern right now is wanting to make sure they have their capacity locked in. There is way more dialogue around the assurance of having a truck or whatever mode that needs to be secured," Leathers said. "Not saying they are price insensitive, because they are certainly always concerned about price. But you can't sell it if it's not on the shelf."

ShipMatrix, a data analytics firm for the logistics industry, forecasts 2.4 billion packages will be delivered during the 2026 holiday peak, a 4% increase year over year.

It's an increase that will likely lead retailers to increasingly use gig delivery, especially later in the holiday peak. Gig workers allow retailers and delivery companies to adjust to changes in order flow and accommodate surges of last-minute shopping without spending time and money onboarding new team members.

"Gig economy delivery is great. You know, it fulfills fast, but there's better unit economics out there," Catania said.

A 2024 PwC study forecast that the cost of drone delivery would "drop by more than 70% over the next 10 years, which will not only match traditional last-mile delivery costs, but surpass them and create an inflection point for the industry."

"The expectation now is becoming so much more instant," Regev said.

#272. Novo says Wegovy injection lowers BMI and obesity rates in children in trial

Novo on Monday released full results from a late-stage trial showing its blockbuster Wegovy injection helped children ages 6 to under 12 with obesity shed weight, significantly lowering their body mass index.

Wegovy reduced BMI by an estimated 19.7 percentage points on average compared with a placebo at 68 weeks, with BMI falling 17.4% among children receiving the drug and rising 2.2% among those receiving a placebo. Those results assumed patients stayed on treatment during the trial.

When evaluating all children in the study, including those who discontinued treatment, Wegovy lowered BMI by an estimated average of 16.2 percentage points more than the placebo at 68 weeks. All 167 kids in the study received lifestyle modifications throughout the trial, including a ​reduced-calorie diet and increased physical activity.

The detailed results mark an encouraging step forward in treating childhood obesity — a chronic disease that can carry serious cardiovascular, metabolic and psychosocial consequences but has had few treatment options for younger children. When left unaddressed, childhood obesity can extend into adulthood, bringing earlier and more severe health complications.

"This is a very significant disease burden in this very young patient population," said Dr. Andrea Traina, Novo's medical director lead of obesity.

In 2025, the disease affected an estimated roughly 177 million children and teens ages 5 to 19 worldwide, according to the World Obesity Federation. That figure is projected to rise to 228 million by 2040.

While Wegovy is already approved to treat obesity in children ages 12 and older, there are currently no approved medications for those under 12. The latest results offer evidence that Wegovy can potentially fill that treatment gap, safely and effectively reducing the severity of the disease in children as young as 6.

In Novo's trial, 85% of children started with moderate to severe obesity, which corresponds to BMI thresholds of 35 and above and 40 or higher, respectively. Nearly half already had at least one obesity-related complication, such as high blood pressure, high cholesterol or sleep apnea.

But more than 40% of children treated with Wegovy were no longer classified as having obesity after 68 weeks of the trial, assuming patients stayed on treatment. Novo initially shared that result in September.

"We are driving towards control of this chronic disease in that patient population when they are still so young and at such high risk for more advanced and accelerated disease progression because they developed it so early," Traina said.

Novo said Wegovy's overall safety and tolerability among children — how well patients managed the drug — were consistent with those seen in ​trials ⁠with adults. The company added that it observed no safety concerns related to growth or pubertal development in the kids.

Still, the two-year study does not establish whether treating obesity earlier can change its long-term trajectory, or what treatment and maintenance might look like as children grow into adulthood, Traina said.

"That is something that is still an evidence gap," she said, pointing to the potential of future research.
