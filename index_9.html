I never thought I’d get excited about replacing my microwave, but the Ninja Crispi Microwave and Air Fryer has made a pretty convincing case for ditching two appliances and making room for one. Combining a microwave with a built-in air fryer, it’s designed to speed up everyday cooking, give leftovers their crunch back and, perhaps best of all, free up some valuable kitchen counter space.

Priced at $449.99, it’s certainly not a budget-friendly microwave. But this is no ordinary machine for heating leftover soup. Ninja has paired a 1,000W microwave with a 1,700W air fryer system, powered by its FusionCrisp technology, which combines the speed of microwave cooking with the golden, crispy results you’d typically expect from an air fryer.

Think leftover pizza, chicken tenders, fries, and all those foods that tend to turn sad and soggy in a traditional microwave. Instead, the idea is to get food that’s piping hot and juicy on the inside, with a deliciously crisp, golden finish on the outside. In other words, no more choosing between speed and texture.

Of course, microwave and air fryer combinations aren’t entirely new. Brands such as Smeg have already explored the concept, while plenty of modern microwaves come with a range of additional cooking functions.

What makes the Ninja Crispi Microwave particularly interesting, however, is its FusionCrisp technology, which is designed to bring both cooking methods together rather than simply offering two separate functions. It comes with a 5.5-quart glass cooking dish and a nonstick crisper plate, which sits on top when you want to air fry and crisp up your food.

Another part that makes it stand out is that there isn’t a rotating plate inside, opening up a lot more space to cook more dishes at once.

It’s not just about reheating leftovers. For example, if you want to make a pasta bake, the microwave can cook the pasta, while the air fryer crisps up the cheese on top.

There’s one thing to consider before buying it, though. Measuring 20.3 x 17.56 x 14.1 inches, the microwave is hardly compact. It takes up a fair amount of counter space, so while it could free up room if you’re replacing a separate microwave and air fryer, it’s not necessarily the ideal solution for a small apartment kitchen.

Then there’s the $449.99 price tag. That’s a considerable amount to spend on a microwave, particularly when a basic model can handle everyday reheating for a fraction of the cost. But if you’re someone who regularly reaches for both a microwave and an air fryer, the combination could make a lot more sense.

#455. Omen AI adds liquid know-how in $16 million Aquatic Labs acquisition

Buy One Founder Summit Ticket, Get a Second for Free. Register now.

Founder Summit begins Nov. 4, 9:00 a.m. ET. Get your pass savings & deals before it starts. REGISTER HERE.

U.S. data centers use 17 to 19 billion gallons of water to cool their hot-running chips each year — and that number is expected to triple in the next five years, according to The Water Foundation.

That’s an expensive and politically tricky proposition for investors throwing billions of dollars at the challenge of scaling up AI. One way to use less water is to do a better job monitoring the cooling fluids, a task that currently involves sending samples away to a laboratory for tests in a multi-day process.

Omen AI was founded in 2024 to solve that problem. The company is building a real-time sensor to determine whether valves were failing, or if bacteria growth in the system threatened to choke off water flow. After raising a $31 million Series A in June, Omen is now acquiring competitor Aquatic Labs in a $16 million cash-and-stock deal.

The deal came together after Omen founder Zach Laberge was introduced to Aquatic Labs’ CEO Allan Adams by an investor in the filtration space. Both startups were building real-time water sensors with complementary technology: Omen’s sensors looked for elements in the water, while the Aquatic Labs sensor tests its alkalinity.

Putting those two technologies in the same sensor creates a “much richer picture of the water and the fluid,” Adams told TechCrunch, while uniting their vision of automating what had previously been a slow, manual process.

The combination unites two classic species of founder: Laberge dropped out of high school to pursue his first company, which did similar fluid analysis for large construction machines that depend on hydraulics. Adams was an academic, trained as a physicist, who spent much of his career developing advanced ocean sensing technology as an MIT professor and Woods Hole Oceanographic Institute researcher before starting his company.

Now, with Adams as the company’s chief science officer, the combined teams are working to integrate both technologies into one sensor, which they expect to have on the market by early next year. Omen’s initial sensors will be monitoring chips pulling in 250 to 300 MW of power by the end of the year, worth about $5 to $7 billion, Laberge told TechCrunch, with the hope of being installed on the equivalent of 1 GW of chips by the end of 2027.

The added value is in uptime: If the fluid system gets out of whack, data center operators typically choose to dump the water rather than risk damage to their expensive equipment. That means shutting down operations until the fluid system is restored, which costs a lot of money. Monitoring lets data center operators use the same fluid for longer, and minimize the costs of a clean up.

Adams hopes to see their technologies have an impact outside of data centers as well, telling TechCrunch that “water goes into data centers, and then it comes out and gets dumped into municipal water, and you want that whole process to happen in a way that’s as safe and responsible as possible — and that means you got to measure stuff.”

AI companies once estimated as much as $4 trillion would be spent on AI infrastructure by 2030, but some investors have grown skeptical of the buildout, sending hyperscaler stocks plummeting in recent days. Omen’s team isn’t worried about shifting sentiment though, since their work comes after a data center has been built.

“We’re working customers on stuff they’re building today or have already built, and so it doesn’t really affect us because all of those projects were financed 12 months ago,” Laberge said. “Our view is, if there’s tightness, this is still going to be the biggest market in human history, even if you sliced it in half.”

#449. 42AI comes out of stealth with $62.5M and a world model that can ‘predict human behavior’

Buy One Founder Summit Ticket, Get a Second for Free. Register now.

Founder Summit begins Nov. 4, 9:00 a.m. ET. Get your pass savings & deals before it starts. REGISTER HERE.

Here’s a question straight out of sci-fi: What if AI could predict what you were going to do before you decided to, even in a completely new situation?

Israeli startup 42AI says it is developing a “behavioral world model” that can do just that, and it has just come out of stealth with $62.5 million in seed funding. The company is initially running its predictive system at a handful of financial brokerages, where it says the model can help predict how individual traders will respond to volatile conditions.

“Behavior is not deterministic; it’s constantly probabilistic,” 42AI’s CEO and co-founder Amit Gal-Or told TechCrunch when I asked him to justify his company’s tall claim. He argues that people have underlying psychological tendencies that influence their decisions even when their circumstances change, and that’s what the startup is trying to measure and convert into data that the model can understand.

“A week from now, you might be a different person, but your tendencies will always play out in certain areas to a probability or distribution,” his co-founder and CTO Ehud Ezra added. And that, the company argues, is what the model does: show the probability of a person making a certain decision.

42AI says the model tries to figure out people’s internal motivations instead of extrapolating patterns based on their previous actions. To do that, 42AI examines how people react to seemingly unrelated events. For an investor or trader, for example, such events might include completing KYC processes, onboarding, support calls, money transfers, or trading stocks.

“When the person is talking to support or sales in the initial process […] we’re looking at the sentiment, we’re looking at the voice, we’re looking at a lot of interactions that they’re doing, how many times do they call, and so forth,” added Ezra. “And all of those data points are going into the model. Later on, you have the interactions: how you buy and sell, and when, and we’re looking at the market at that stage.”

“So we’re trying to look at how they’re looking at the world. In a world model, every individual is the world,” he added.

The company then converts those observations into mathematical representations of traits, which are then fed into a transformer-based world model that lays out probabilities for possible decisions under different circumstances. (The company uses a separate language model to generate explanations for its predictions.)

Ezra said the model is trained to preserve a person’s underlying tendencies during simulations. So if someone is usually risk-averse, but the model suddenly treats them as a risk-taker, “the target is off,” he said, explaining how the model’s training rewards are structured.

42AI spent three years developing this approach, combining psychological research with neuroscience and genetics, as well as advice from experts in psychology and PTSD, and Dan Shechtman, who was awarded the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Gal-Or said the team first studied thousands of people via psych assessments and behavioral scenarios, before adding government and biobank datasets covering more than 2 million people. Its customer datasets have since expanded to cover almost 30 million people, he said.

In live deployments, the model analyzes customers’ existing records, including transactions and support calls. Gal-Or pointed out the system doesn’t need personally identifiable information, arguing that “the model never knows who the person is.” However, it seems the company still creates individual behavioral profiles from linked customer activity.

In predicting market trades, 42AI claims its model achieves 98% accuracy, but Gal-Or clarified that this is more a measure of whether trading flows fall within a predicted range, not if each trade was predicted correctly.

He also said the model consistently achieves an F1 score of 0.8 to 0.85 for predicting trading direction and volume. (F1 score is a metric in machine learning that measures how well a classification model can balance correct predictions against false alarms and missed events.)

And it’s not like the model can work everywhere, given it’s still a machine in a box. “There’s a natural ceiling because you really don’t have access to everything that a person experiences,” Gal-Or said. Ezra explained that the system’s accuracy also suffers when it misses important situational context, like if someone chooses to change their trading choices based on advice from a friend or a colleague.

Gal-Or said 42AI is working with 10 large brokerages, who are using it to manage liquidity, margin requirements, and hedging exposure. The company is onboarding more customers, he said, and is generating more than “seven figures” in revenue, though he wouldn’t specify a number.

Customers pay the startup a flat fee, or a percentage of the value the platform helps them gain. He claimed one brokerage generated approximately $30 million in additional value through dynamic hedging.

The $62.5 million seed funding came from the founders and Infinity Group over three years, and wasn’t entirely raised from external investors, Gal-Or said. Infinity was founded by his father, Amir Gal-Or, who is also 42AI’s chairman and a co-founder. 42AI now plans to raise more money.

Beyond finance, 42AI is now exploring applications for its model in gaming, fraud prevention, and cybersecurity, and one day wants to use it to help robots and AI agents understand people. But that may be a ways off yet: Ezra acknowledged that the model needs to be trained on rules and context for each new domain.

And like with all AI tools, there’s a potentially troubling side to 42AI’s technology: If a brokerage can anticipate what its customers may do, what’s to stop someone from using that knowledge to their own benefit? Ezra admitted that this was “a valid concern” when I raised it, and the founders pointed to regulations, monitoring, and an internal ethics committee, though they didn’t explain what, if anything, would stop their customers from using the technology to gain an advantage over the very people it’s profiling.

Amit Gal-Or, however, was confident that the technology could help address such risks. “We are very proud that actually we are part of the solution; we are the solution to this problem,” he said.

RSI is the point at which AI grows so good at training itself that it spawns a dramatic acceleration of AI capabilities. To the AI doomers, RSI represents the cataclysm from which there is no return, in which AI builds itself into something powerful enough that it slips beyond human control and may be indifferent to the damage it causes while achieving its goals.

To AI enthusiasts, RSI, or some version of AI reliably using itself to improve, represents the technology’s promise, where it becomes a tool of unimaginable productivity, scientific discovery, and more.

Anthropic seems to believe that RSI is both a promise and a peril. It says in the S-1 that it uses AI in its R&D, but it cautions: “Using AI to help build more capable AI could also pose a considerable safety risk, particularly if it sharply accelerates the pace of progress. This is one of the areas covered by our Responsible Scaling Policy, which sets out our plans to monitor and mitigate the risks as we approach what has been termed ‘recursive self-improvement.’”

When the term RSI became popular earlier this year, many believed it was a buzzword or, at best, a goal too hard to achieve, define, or prove.

In addition, our increasing use of AI systems to accelerate our own research and development may cause the capabilities of our models to advance more rapidly than our ability to develop, validate, and implement corresponding safety, alignment, and security measures. As a result, we have in the past not identified, and may in the future not identify, a significant acceleration in our rate of development until after it has occurred.

For example, our internal estimates and our expectations regarding future models, rather than our current models, indicate that we may imminently meet our Responsible Scaling Policy’s definition of “dramatically accelerated progress attributable to AI.” We have not designated any of our current models as exceeding this threshold, and we continue to assess the underlying data.

However, Anthropic also said in the filing that none of its models have crossed this RSI threshold yet and that it is promising to keep humans in the R&D process for the foreseeable future. “We do not anticipate being able to safely use Claude to fully autonomously design and build successive generations of frontier models without meaningful human supervision or understanding, if and when that becomes technically possible. Accordingly, we are considering updates to our Responsible Scaling Policy to keep humans in control of frontier model development at Anthropic.”

Skeptics might point out that an S-1 functions as both disclosure and marketing, and that claims of being close to a model so powerful that it can build itself could fall into the latter category.

Nevertheless, there’s no question that Claude’s capabilities, especially in terms of coding, math, and cybersecurity, have grow in leaps and bounds this year. If Anthropic thinks the possibility of RSI is close, maybe we should believe it and act — perhaps govern — accordingly.

Whether developed by OpenAI or Anthropic or built on open source alternatives, current AI models share a critical limitation: They don’t learn from their mistakes without further training.

Businesses adapting these models for specialized fields, such as law and finance, typically get around this by fine-tuning general models. However, that often requires hiring internal research teams or relying on expensive consultants, commonly known as forward-deployed engineers (FDEs).

Trajectory, a startup founded about a year ago by three Stanford classmates with experience at Google DeepMind and Apple, wants to automate that process, allowing AI models to learn from feedback and continuously improve at specialized tasks.

The 18-person company just announced that it raised $45 million in a Series A round at a $300 million valuation, led by Sequoia, with participation from Nvidia, Bessemer Venture Partners, and Bain Capital Ventures.

Trajectory co-founder Ronak Malde compared foundational models to a very smart PhD student who’s always starting a new job. “But what the economy values is someone who worked on the job for 30 years,” he told TechCrunch.

The startup aims to solve this problem through an approach known as continual learning. Businesses using Trajectory can start with an open source AI model and connect the platform to their applications, allowing it to collect feedback on how the model performs. Trajectory then uses that information to retrain the model, helping it improve at specialized tasks over time.

With Trajectory, lawyers, doctors, and other specialists can improve the models near automatically themselves, Malde said. That capability could make deploying specialized AI faster and cheaper.

Companies like Harvey and Rogo are already using Trajectory’s platform to post-train open source models, helping them perform more like experienced lawyers or financial analysts by learning from previous tasks, such as reviewing a particular type of legal contract or valuing a company using a specific methodology. Trajectory’s other customers include Decagon, Mercor, and Nvidia. The startup is now making its post-training platform publicly available.

The people using Trajectory decide “what good looks like for their organization and can turn that into improvements for their model,” Malde said.

Trajectory’s approach to continual learning shares some similarities with recursive self-improvement (RSI), the idea that AI systems can improve their own capabilities. But co-founder Michael Elabd argues that keeping humans involved in the training process makes Trajectory’s approach safer, since autonomous self-improvement could lead to unintended or even dangerous outcomes.

Trajectory isn’t the only startup trying to automate post-training. It considers Thinking Machines Lab, founded by former OpenAI CTO Mira Murati and the company behind the fine-tuning platform Tinker, a direct competitor, along with Applied Compute, which was valued at $3.25 billion last month, and Prime Intellect, which helps companies build and train their own AI agents.

Malde believes the startup’s biggest advantage is its team. He was among the handful of researchers Google DeepMind acqui-hired from Windsurf. Elabd also worked at DeepMind, while Trajectory’s third co-founder, Arjun Karanam, previously worked on generative foundation models at Apple. The startup has also recruited researchers and product designers from OpenAI, Stripe, and Netflix.

Why does design matter so much to a company focused on AI training? Karanam says the startup has developed an interface that makes post-training more accessible to its nontechnical customers.

#375. Fewer people are flying as airfare soars. Here's why airlines aren't worried

Fewer people are flying this year, but with airfare this high and climbing, airline executives say that's just fine.

At the New York Hilton in Midtown Manhattan on Friday night, the aviation industry held its annual Wings Club gala, honoring Ed Bastian, CEO of profit-leader Delta Air Lines.

North American airline CEOs and other executives at the black-tie bash told CNBC that demand trends aren't worrying them, but persistently high, profit-crimping fuel prices are.

The number of people screened at U.S. checkpoints is on track for its first annual decline since 2020. Major U.S. carriers have said capturing higher fares matters more than fully filling planes or flying record numbers of travelers.

"We're actively prioritizing yield versus load factor and really happy as we see it. It fits right in with the premium investments that we're making," American Airlines Chief Commercial Officer Nat Pieper said on the company's earnings call Thursday.

American's load factor, a gauge of how full planes are flying, fell to 83.4% in the third quarter from 86% in the same quarter of 2025.

Plane ticket prices have skyrocketed as airlines grapple with a spike in jet fuel prices, their biggest expense after labor, and pass more of those costs on to customers.

Fares in September were up more than 22% from a year earlier, according to the latest inflation read. Fare tracker Hopper said it expects this holiday season to come with the most expensive ticket prices in years.

The average Southwest Airlines fare in the third quarter, for example, was $231.69, up close to $50 from a year earlier. That doesn't include Southwest's new fees, like its extra charges for checked bags or a seat closer to the front of the plane.

The airline's revenue passenger count, however, fell 3.2% from a year ago to about 33.5 million people.

Still, both fares and the passenger counts exceeded Southwest's expectations.

"Passenger fares came in about $3 above what our forecast was and the more important thing is that passenger volumes also exceeded our expectations," Southwest CEO Bob Jordan said on an earnings call Thursday.

Since April — when airlines began warning in earnest about profits taking a hit from the spike in jet fuel — through Thursday, about 9.3 million fewer people were screened at airport checkpoints than in the same period of 2025, according to the Transportation Security Administration. That's down less than 2% from last year, but it follows years of growth.

With jet fuel prices more than double year-ago levels — as seen in U.S. Gulf Coast spot prices at $4.63 a gallon, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration — airlines have focused on recovering as much of the fuel spike as possible.

Despite some drops, airline executives said during their earnings calls that overall travel demand is robust, especially for profitable premium cabins like business class.

And not every carrier is seeing a decline. United Airlines flew 2.4% more passengers in the third quarter than a year ago and said its unit revenue rose 16%, the biggest increase since the pandemic.

"I've been pleasantly surprised at the industry's ability this year to raise yields and pass through much of the increase in fuel prices," CEO Scott Kirby said on the company's earnings call Wednesday. "So it's possible that airlines will continue to focus on yields at the expense of load factor and that alone could get us to double-digit margins next year."

Most airlines expect fuel costs to keep biting, but executives say they're confident in their pricing power.

United, for example, said it expects to pay an average of $4.80 per gallon for fuel in the fourth quarter, up from $3.90 per gallon in the third.

American, United, Delta and Southwest all cut their 2026 profit forecasts this month when they reported earnings. American is the only one of the four that expects to post a loss this year.

American and others have also reduced growth plans or warned the industry could dial back its capacity expansion goals, which could keep fares elevated. Reduced capacity can make bookable seats scarcer, driving up prices even more if demand holds.

The carriers said demand has been particularly strong for premium seats, which command hundreds, if not thousands of dollars, more than coach tickets.

"Today, we find ourselves yet at another inflection point for our industry and the world. ... Geopolitics affecting our business, driving the price of jet fuel to historic highs," Bastian said in his award-acceptance speech, adding that artificial intelligence makes it hard to predict months or weeks ahead.

One factor that could also affect air travel is the demise of Spirit Airlines, which went out of business in early May.

Restaurant stocks have split sharply this year. And it could be tied to how many brands they own.

Meanwhile, stocks like McDonald's, Chipotle, Cava, Wendy's, Shake Shack and Domino's have all dropped this year, with several down double-digit percentages. Those companies all have one core chain to their names.

Those share moves underscore what some analysts see as the benefits of a diversified restaurant business, especially at a time when any misstep can make cash-strapped consumers go elsewhere when they dine out.

One company that could be watching is Inspire Brands, the owner of chains like Dunkin', Buffalo Wild Wings and Jimmy John's, which is moving toward going public.

A business built around one main restaurant concept depends more heavily on that brand holding consumers' attention, whereas a company with a portfolio has other potential sources of sales if one brand loses momentum.

That cushion can be critical, but only if another chain is strong enough to pick up the slack, according to some Wall Street analysts.

"Darden is probably the best case of the scale and size and diversity of business," said Nick Setyan, a restaurant analyst at Mizuho Securities. "If one business isn't doing well, the other ones are."

Setyan said that dynamic has played out between Darden's key brands — Olive Garden and LongHorn Steakhouse — over recent quarters. LongHorn's growth has outpaced Olive Garden's for months, and in the latest reported quarter, LongHorn's comparable sales rose 6.2% compared with Olive Garden's 1.1%.

#336. Why popular airplanes are getting scrapped years early

GOODYEAR, Ariz. — An Airbus A320neo, one of the world's most popular planes, rolled off the Mobile, Alabama, factory line in July 2022, painted bright yellow for its next destination: Spirit Airlines.

It was built to fly for decades. But this May, less than four years later, it was stripped for parts worth millions and crushed, the steel teeth of an excavator tearing through its fuselage at a facility on the outskirts of Phoenix.

More aircraft are getting dismantled years, if not decades, earlier than expected, industry executives told CNBC. It is the result of a shortage of parts, none more precious and expensive than aircraft engines, that has exasperated airlines and driven up costs for years.

Even after the engines are removed, firms are looking for parts as varied as landing gear and antennae to sell.

"It's the first time in my career that involved purchasing an aircraft that's less than five years since date of manufacture," said Bill Thompson, vice president who leads aircraft and engine acquisition for the Americas at aviation asset management and trading firm EirTrade Aviation, which bought the less than 4-year-old Airbus A320neo earlier this year.

He said the company paid "single-digit" millions each for that airplane and a similarly aged one, and it expects to make a profit from selling the more than 1,000 parts it took off them before they were crushed.

"I don't know if you're going to sell the coffee makers but you never know. Sometimes you do," he said.

Twenty-one newest-generation aircraft were torn down from June 2025 through this past August, according to aviation data and advisory firm IBA Group. The scrapped planes were 6.4 years old on average, and included mostly Airbus but also some Boeing planes. That compares with an average retirement age for narrow-body airplanes of 21.7 years, IBA said.

"Anytime that you end up with any period of market softness in aircraft availability, engine availability, it's going to cause investors to look at other options," said Simon Goodson, CEO of AerFin, a company that buys, sells, repairs and leases aircraft and parts. "We always say to our aircraft and engine traders, 'Please don't fall in love with the metal.'"

Hundreds of aircraft are retired each year as manufacturers ramp up production for airlines, so older planes are swapped out for newer, more fuel-efficient models.

But aside from retiring aging planes, some owners don't want to wait around for replacement parts or repairs on younger jets.

"A year might not sound that long, but does three or four years?" said Dave Querio, CEO of Ascent Aviation Services, a company that repairs, modifies, stores and disassembles aircraft, based in Marana, Arizona, outside of Tucson. "That's the issue for the aircraft that came in in '23, '24 and '25."

In some cases, owners can reap more for the parts than they can selling the plane or paying storage costs and waiting for it to get back in the air again.

Querio said he hasn't seen "the volume of younger aircraft being torn down as I'm seeing today."

A major reason for the young retirements is the long wait and rising prices for jet engines.

One problem is that cutting-edge engines that burn hotter and use less fuel are coming up for maintenance far earlier than airlines expected.

The maintenance requests have kept engine repair and overhaul facilities backed up, from those owned by manufacturing giants GE Aerospace and RTX unit Pratt & Whitney to smaller, independent ones. Airlines and leasing firms have been hunting for spare parts and engines anywhere they can find them, like here in Goodyear, Arizona.

The trend has added more fuel to the already hot secondhand parts market. An engine fan blade could cost $50,000 or even more than $100,000 for bigger jets, Goodson said. Engines that power some Airbus A320neo aircraft have 20 of those blades. A disc for the engine, meanwhile, could cost between $1 million and $2 million.

An engine recall because of a manufacturing defect has backed up repair shops and grounded hundreds of Airbus planes around the world. Spirit Airlines bore the brunt of that problem in the United States, years before the airline collapsed.

The engine is made by Pratt & Whitney, which issued the recall in 2023 after discovering contaminated powdered metal used in making some parts. Those engines are worth close to $17 million this year at the high end, up 60% from the upper end of the range in 2019, according to IBA.

Airlines are removing some engines off retired planes to keep current aircraft flying while they wait for engines to come back from the shop.

"So you're just playing Legos and moving engines on and off aircraft," said Mike Stengel, a partner at AeroDynamic Advisory, an aerospace consulting firm.

The problems are driving up the cost to buy or rent one of the popular engines to record levels.

Renting one is ranging from about $160,000 to $180,000 a month, according to IBA data. A competing engine for the A320neo family made by CFM International, a joint venture of GE Aerospace and France's Safran, is far from a bargain, renting in a similar range of $130,000 to $180,000 a month.

The problems with young retirements predate Spirit's collapse.

The 4-year-old Airbus plane that was torn down outside Phoenix in May is a key example. The aircraft was put in storage because of the engine recall, and it stayed until it was torn apart.

But the company's demise has brought more of its Airbus fleet to the market, forcing owners to figure out if it's worth the wait for engines or other parts.

CNBC visited AerSale's facility in Goodyear on a scorching day in late June, less than two months after Spirit collapsed, where dozens of the idle yellow planes were stored. It was so soon after the airline's demise that a former Detroit-based Spirit captain, Glenn Zecchin, popped in and asked about opportunities to ferry Spirit's aircraft to the desert, and to peek in on his former airline's planes.

"I knew the airplanes were there, and I wanted to see what happened," said Zecchin, who is now flying corporate jets.

A jet parked at the storage facility and maintenance hangar still had Spirit in-flight menus and safety cards in the bungee cord seatbacks. Pilot manuals were still in the cockpit.

Not all of these aircraft will be destroyed. Some companies that owned Spirit's planes before bankruptcy will just sell their parts, but some may keep the planes in storage. Other aircraft may go on to fly for other airlines, with potential carriers ripping out Spirit's tight 28-inch seat pitch for something roomier.

But as long as they're not flying, it means paying storage fees, which vary by aircraft type. Some planes in desert facilities — selected because the arid climate can preserve the aircraft better and avoid corrosion — can sit for decades.

Others won't make it back into the air if there's enough demand for the parts and too long of a wait to get the plane flying again.

"After you get rid of all the valuable equipment and sell it back into the aftermarket, what's really left is just a metal carcass that you will literally take a crane and crush," said AeroDynamic's Stengel. Then the owner can "sell it to recyclers that will make of it what they will, maybe into beer cans or something outside of aerospace."

Ascent's Querio said some owners opt to take that route.

"It's a little melancholy to watch it at times," he said. "But sometimes, it's kind of a thrill to ... see how fast an aircraft can be crunched up and sent off to the smelter."
