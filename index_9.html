#467. Retailers are turning to entertainment to win shoppers’ attention

Legacy retailer Gap Inc. made an unconventional hire earlier this year: a chief entertainment officer.

It was a newly created role as the company tapped former Paramount executive Pam Kaufman to helm its entertainment, content and licensing platform. Gap has since rolled out major campaigns, partnering with bands like Just Your Type and building on the success of last year's ads with girl group Katseye.

"Beloved brands have value beyond the products they sell. Consumers identify with them, trust them and want to be part of their world," Kaufman told CNBC in a statement. "The key is authenticity: Every extension has to reflect what the brand stands for and give its fans a meaningful new way to experience it."

She said it's part of a broader goal to emphasize what Gap calls "fashiontainment," a strategy to blend retail and entertainment to better align with consumers. Gap is part of a growing cohort of retailers investing in entertainment and experiences both in-store and through exclusive online content to drive engagement.

"Brands are stories, and we are the storytellers," CEO Richard Dickson said at the Goldman Sachs Global Consumer and Retail conference in September. "I think you could see that come to life when you look at some of the work that we've done, putting our brands in the center of culture and cultural conversation."

In another blending of retail and media, Mattel made waves a few years ago for its "Barbie" movie, building on the intellectual property of a decades-old fan favorite and drawing in massive box office success. At the time, Dickson was president and chief operating officer of the company, witnessing firsthand the sectors' intersection before departing for Gap.

"I think the post-Covid consumer attitude around shopping certainly evolved a lot," said Bryan Eshelman, who leads the retail practice at consulting firm AlixPartners. "It has gone from shopping as a fulfillment task to shopping, in many instances, more as an entertainment form."

Between experiences in physical stores and media campaigns featuring exclusive content, Eshelman said, retailers are getting more creative about the ways they're driving engagement and marketing.

"Community, at the end of the day, is one of the key ways to drive loyalty, so I think retailers of all types are trying to tap into some sense of community, some sense of lifestyle, some sense of belonging that's beyond the product that they offer," he said.

Branded entertainment has been around for a long time. Procter & Gamble made headlines in the 1930s for helping to coin the term "soap opera," and companies have for decades created media campaigns to draw in customers.

But with the rise of social media, content creation and short-form offerings, experts said audiences are becoming hungrier than ever for something new and original.

The retail and entertainment intersection is happening at a time when intellectual property is becoming more valuable than ever, according to experts.

As consumers encounter more marketing than ever, the companies that will win the battle for attention are the ones that can "cut through all of the noise," said Alex Michael, senior managing director at investment firm LionTree.

"The hardest thing to find and to get is audience these days," Michael told CNBC. "IP, in that sense, is the most valuable thing out there, and only becoming more valuable because it's attached to audience."

At the same time, he said, the way IP is distributed is broadening to include video, audio, short-form, long-form and live event content. Consumers are also redefining their loyalty to brands, which means companies have to "play much earlier in the consumer journey" than they have in years past to secure their trust, he said.

The trend is also likely helped by executives who have worked in both retail and entertainment: Gap's Kaufman came from Paramount, for example, and Dickson and Skydance co-CEO Ynon Kreiz held top roles at Mattel.

"The gray areas have never been more pronounced as to what is classically defined as retail or media or entertainment or live events," Michael said. "People who have cross-sector expertise are increasingly more valuable because you can't be defined by a single vertical, especially as things change so quickly."

Still, there's room for newer players, especially with the studio landscape rapidly changing, said Sean Akaks, the CEO of SonderCo, an agency that partners brands with celebrities and entertainment properties.

"I think it's a really interesting time for nontraditional players to be making content because you're seeing this massive consolidation of these legacy entertainment companies," he said. "I think there are just more open lanes for independent creators and also brands and anyone outside the traditional studio system to create content."

That's where retailers might tap creators, Akaks said, because they can use their platforms to reach consumers directly.

"I do think there's a world where we're going to see creators and brands sort of co-creating IP that maybe they're owning together," he said.

Once those companies get consumers into the store, they're also leaning into experiential retail, making shopping a more immersive experience than it otherwise would be, like Dick's Sporting Goods offering interactive activities and simulators in its stores.

According to data from AlixPartners, companies investing in experiential retail such as Coach and Ralph Lauren saw close to 10% year-over-year increases in consumers' average basket size.

"The idea that retailers would potentially invest in some distributed electronic content to help drive traffic, and then when you get there, it's experiential — it really goes into the kinds of flywheels that entertainment companies are also trying to build and grow," said Jeff Goldstein, co-lead of the media and entertainment practice at AlixPartners.

Yet, the entertainment that retail brands are developing is only one piece in a larger puzzle to retain customers and attract new ones. Translating that into tangible sales growth isn't necessarily a given.

"The basics of having great product in stock at a transparent price that feels like value is not swept away by animatronics," AlixPartners' Eshelman said. "The fundamentals are still there, and if you're going to have a good retail proposition, you need to have that balance. You can't mask bad product at a bad price with no inventory with glitz and glamour."

#459. Anthropic’s 7 founders cashed out nearly $2B in record-breaking IPO

Anthropic became a public company on Thursday, raising a total of $81.3 billion, including $1.95 billion for its seven founders. As we previously reported, each of them ponied up about 3.4 million shares apiece to sell in the IPO.

At the upgraded IPO price of $83 per share, that means they each pocketed about $280 million before underwriting fees and taxes. It looked like they had calculated the number of shares they planned to sell to give them each around $250 million if shares hit $74. That was the midpoint price from Anthropic’s original price range. So that’s a cool $30-ish million more for each of them than perhaps they were gunning for.

Anthropic implied in its IPO filings that the founders aren’t entirely in this for the money though. The company said that each of the founders has pledged to dedicate 80% of their personal equity in the company to philanthropy.

However, if any of them struggle to live on their quarter of a billion, they can scale back their charitable commitments. The company points out that these commitments are “voluntary, revocable expressions of each co-founder’s philanthropic intent and do not constitute legally binding obligations.”

With the IPO in the record books, there were a few other interesting numbers worth highlighting.

$104.8 billion. That’s really the amount Anthropic raised for its coffers (with a possibility of $117 billion, if the bankers buy all of their allotment of shares). In addition to selling its Class A common stock, it also sold a previously announced $26 billion worth of a special non-voting Class N stock to some of the biggest tech names in the industry.

Nvidia bought $10 billion of these Class N shares, Google bought $10 billion, and AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Microsoft, and TSMC divvied up the rest.

Just for comparison: When Facebook had its then record-breaking IPO in 2012, it raised $16 billion on IPO day.

28% stake to Amazon and Google combined. All told, at the higher IPO share price, Google wound up owning 13.4% of the company. Amazon also now has a big stake in Anthropic because when it previously invested in the AI lab as a startup, it was granted promissory notes that could be converted to equity. It converted a small percentage of them as part of the IPO, leaving it with a 14.6% stake. Had it converted all of them, Amazon could have owned nearly 24%.

The two large cloud providers now own 28% of Anthropic, the AI lab says.

More than $1 trillion. As mind-blowing as it is to raise nearly $105 billion, giving Anthropic nearly $230 billion of working capital available (along with capital it already had on hand), the company’s need for future capital is still far greater.

Anthropic needs about $70.7 billion just to cover its compute and infrastructure spending commitments in the next 12 months as of the quarter ended September 30, 2026. It needs $451.6 billion to cover these commitments before the end of 2030. And it has racked up hundreds of billions of other commitments that are either conditional (and may not happen) or haven’t been priced yet.

All told, the company has disclosed a ballpark of $1.05 trillion to $1.08 trillion in infrastructure commitments in its foreseeable future. This was a huge IPO. But it is a pricey business to run.

#455. Apple may be developing its own AI agent that runs your Mac

Apple appears to be working on its own AI agent to conduct computer work on behalf of users — including using apps, accessing websites, and coding, among other things.

The news was originally reported by Bloomberg, which spotted a job listing the iPhone maker recently posted for a senior engineering manager to oversee the creation of an “AI agent that can use a computer.”

“Imagine an AI agent that can use a computer the way a capable person does,” the posting says. “It browses the web, works across apps, runs commands, writes and debugs code, and calls services, and it chooses the right tool for each step. It does all this privately and with the polish people expect from Apple.”

The posting additionally notes that the agents should be able to move “fluidly between tools” and “carry out multi-step tasks across apps, browsers, command lines, code, and APIs.”

TechCrunch reached out to Apple for more information but did not receive a response.

Agents capable of computer work have become one of the hottest commodities in the tech industry over the past year. Major labs like Anthropic and OpenAI have launched such services, while other major tech brands — like Meta, which recently launched its Muse agent line — have also moved into the space. It makes sense that Apple — which is trying to expand its AI offerings in the new John Ternus era — would want to follow suit.

Bloomberg additionally notes that, at the same time that Apple is working on its own digital assistant, the company is making it significantly harder for third-party agents to gain total control over its computers.

The outlet notes that the version of its upcoming macOS 27.3 will deploy warnings discouraging users from granting full access to third-party agents. In October, Apple issued a notification to developers stating that the company planned to “introduce additional controls to ensure that users who genuinely wish to grant an app this extraordinary level of access can only do so with very explicit user action.”

Bloomberg writes that while the iPhone maker “has eyed introducing the service as soon as the end of next year,” progress on its in-house agent “remains in the early stages.”

#432. A transcription firm used by police, prosecutors, and tech giants exposed customer data online

A transcription company that caters to law enforcement, government departments, and Silicon Valley tech giants has resolved a security lapse after leaving a large amount of internal data, including customer transcriptions and their source materials, exposed to the web.

The data security lapse at San Francisco-based Tigerfish exposed transcriptions of police interviews, body-cam footage, and customers’ raw audio and video footage. The exposed data also included invoices of the company’s customers, which include police departments and prosecutors’ offices.

According to Anurag Sen, a good faith security researcher who found the data, the files were located on an Amazon-hosted (S3) cloud server that was openly listed and accessible to anyone via an easy-to-guess web address — no passwords needed.

The security lapse underscores how simple flaws or misconfigurations can result in sensitive information getting exposed online, especially as AI makes it easier to uncover weaknesses that can put companies and their data at risk.

Sen discovered the exposed data online in October and alerted the company to the issue, but did not hear back. Sen later provided details to TechCrunch in an effort to flag the incident with the company for resolution.

Sen, 30, is no stranger to TechCrunch. For the past decade, he has searched for sensitive datasets exposed online with the goal of notifying the companies and organizations that their information is open to the internet. Like many security researchers, much of his efforts are driven by a duty to secure the internet, and he describes his work as an “unpaid air-traffic controller.”

From his island home in Indonesia, Sen has uncovered sensitive U.S. military emails spilling from a compartmentalized Department of Defense cloud server; databases exposing reams of customer records; the inner workings of a country’s national license plate surveillance system; and public systems that inadvertently sent out streams of SMS codes used by people around the world to log into their online accounts.

Given Sen’s propensity for finding often highly confidential information online, we verified the source of the leak and the data, and contacted Tigerfish.

Soon after making contact, Tigerfish secured its exposed S3 cloud bucket.

“We’ve blocked all public access to the bucket you identified, including directory listings and individual files,” Tigerfish president, Adam Goldberg, said in an email. “We’ve also verified that all of our other S3 buckets are blocked to public access.”

“We’re continuing to review and strengthen our storage security more broadly,” added Goldberg.

The company said that Sen’s email disclosing the data leak was caught in its spam filter.

It’s unclear if the company has notified its customers. Tigerfish did not respond to a follow-up email.

Discoveries of exposed datasets have consumed a large part of Sen’s early cybersecurity career so far. He scours the web daily using automated scanners and specialist search engines to identify databases, cloud storage, or other sensitive information that likely shouldn’t be left unprotected online.

The modern web runs on absolute trust, he said, and operates under the notion that the world’s data is kept secure. But he described how his findings reveal the reality of how “fragile” the internet is behind the scenes.

Despite the recent focus on how AI is accelerating people’s ability to discover vulnerabilities, Sen said that many of the data leaks and exposures that he finds come down to basic human errors.

“An engineer spinning up a test cloud database on a Friday, disabling authentication to test an API, and forgetting to shut the door before heading into the weekend,” was just one example, he recounted.

Sen’s findings over the years highlight the ongoing problems caused by such security mistakes, just as the new wave of vibe-coding and AI-generated development is creating new security problems due to insecure and unchecked code deployed to the web.

Similar to Amazon’s recent efforts to make it difficult to make S3 buckets public without going through clear multistep warnings, newer vibe-coding platforms like Lovable and Supabase have also made changes to their platforms to prevent user mistakes.

“Everyone often fears shadowy hackers,” Sen said. But he argues that the real crisis is leaving sensitive systems and databases “wide open to the public internet without a password.”

#421. Big-ticket holiday items may be harder to find as shipping costs rise

This holiday season, big-ticket items are expected to be in short supply with diesel prices near records, trucking rates close to a post-pandemic level and carrying costs hitting multidecade highs. All of that is forcing many retailers to rethink their holiday inventory strategy.

"We are going to have some of the lowest inventory levels on some of these big-ticket items we have seen in history," said Deborah Weinswig, CEO of retail analytics firm Coresight Research.

Weinswig said retailers she is working with are holding less inventory of big-screen TVs, grills, large consumer appliances like washers and dryers, and other items that take either big amounts of space or cost a lot of money to hold.

"It's freight getting it from there to here; it's carrying costs; then it's just the trucking to get it into the stores," Weinswig said. "We're talking three different pieces right here and each is up significantly year on year."

The surge pricing for supply chain services is being caused by domestic and international policy.

According to Evercore ISI, the rates for general trucking have increased more than 47% year over year during the week of Nov. 2.

Industry experts say a large factor has been a crackdown by the federal government on non-citizen truck drivers and non-U.S.-based truck drivers operating in the U.S. That has created a capacity crunch that has raised rates and is expected to continue to create upward pressure.

At the same time, fuel surcharges are spiking.

Most transportation providers charge customers an extra fuel surcharge to offset changes in the cost of diesel fuel. That surcharge has spiked 64% year over year, according to Evercore ISI data, as diesel prices have hit new record highs due in part to the U.S.-Israel war with Iran.

And inventory holding costs — or the total expense of storing unsold goods, which could include the costs for capital, service, storage and other risk factors — are closely tied to the yield on the 10-year Treasury note, which exceeded 5% in October and remains at multiyear highs in November.

Collectively, the higher costs could mean bigger discounts for shoppers willing to wait, according to Bill Catania, CEO of logistics technology company OneRail, which powers same-day delivery for companies such as FedEx and Lowe's.

"You're going to see more discount and price promotions as we get closer to the holiday to liquidate inventory, so they're not having to transfer it or dispose of it or position it some other way," Catania said.

Retailers are also facing a conundrum in their holiday inventory strategy when it comes to shipping costs versus opportunity costs. In other words, is it worth it to stock up now, or wait it out in hopes that shipping rates will come down some but potentially miss out on having the right product mix?

It's leading to a wait-and-see approach for stocking shelves after early holiday sales in October, executives said.

UPS CEO Carol Tomé said on the company's earnings call on Oct. 27 that it's "just starting to get peak season forecasts from our customers."

Similarly, Derek Leathers, CEO of Werner Enterprises, a trucker for big-box stores and major retailers, said retailers are cautious.

"When rates are higher and the cost of capital is more expensive, you are going to have a much more intense focus on not having excess inventory," Leathers said.

Still, 2026 holiday sales are expected to top $1 trillion as inflation drives growth and customer spending is expected to remain healthy, according to Bain & Company's annual forecast. Leathers said even with the surge in trucking and shipping rates, he's seeing that retailers are willing to pay up to stock up.

"The bigger concern right now is wanting to make sure they have their capacity locked in. There is way more dialogue around the assurance of having a truck or whatever mode that needs to be secured," Leathers said. "Not saying they are price insensitive, because they are certainly always concerned about price. But you can't sell it if it's not on the shelf."

ShipMatrix, a data analytics firm for the logistics industry, forecasts 2.4 billion packages will be delivered during the 2026 holiday peak, a 4% increase year over year.

It's an increase that will likely lead retailers to increasingly use gig delivery, especially later in the holiday peak. Gig workers allow retailers and delivery companies to adjust to changes in order flow and accommodate surges of last-minute shopping without spending time and money onboarding new team members.

"Gig economy delivery is great. You know, it fulfills fast, but there's better unit economics out there," Catania said.

Industry experts said they also expect to see more retailers use drones for last-mile delivery for smaller items.

A 2024 PwC study forecast that the cost of drone delivery would "drop by more than 70% over the next 10 years, which will not only match traditional last-mile delivery costs, but surpass them and create an inflection point for the industry."

"The expectation now is becoming so much more instant," Regev said.

#416. Super-hot rock geothermal could end data center energy woes

Geothermal startups are in a race to see who can tap the deepest, hottest rocks. The winners could power the next phase of the grid, data centers and all.

The broader markets got their first taste of enhanced geothermal earlier this year when Fervo Energy went public. But this week was all about super-hot rock geothermal.

Geothermal has become a darling among investors, tech companies, and even the Trump administration. The basic technology has been around for decades, but advances in drilling techniques borrowed from the oil and gas industry has supercharged it. The U.S. has plenty of potential, too, with up to 57 terawatts of generating capacity, more than enough to power the next wave of AI data centers.

An emerging class of geothermal energy, known as super-hot rock, could help the industry unlock much of that potential.

The secret to super-hot rock geothermal is what happens to water at extreme depths. As it gets closer to the superheated core of the Earth, both temperature and pressure rise significantly. When they get high enough, ordinary water turns into a supercritical fluid — a sort of gaseous-liquid state that can carry tremendous amounts of energy. A single super-hot rock well could generate 15 megawatts of electricity or more.

Super-hot rock geothermal is inching closer to commercial reality. Quaise Energy said on Tuesday that it had landed Google as a customer. The hyperscaler said it would pay more than $500 million to secure 50 megawatts of power from a Quaise project in central Oregon, which will tap into searing heat three miles deep.

Google is paying a premium for the amount of power Quaise will deliver, but that’s in line with the company’s strategy of getting in early with potential suppliers. For instance, it was an investor in and an early customer of Fervo.

Meanwhile, on Monday, Khosla-backed Mazama Energy announced it had figured out how to keep its drill heads cool as they tap into super-hot rocks. And last week, XGS Energy was given a grant by the Department of Energy to develop and refine materials, well designs, and models to improve reliability at depths where super-hot rock exists.

XGS is dipping its toes in the super-hot waters, while Mazama and Quaise have made it their core focus, though they’re taking very different approaches.

Quaise’s technology sounds like science fiction: Starting at about 2.5 miles deep, the company will swap out its conventional drill bit for one powered by a gyrotron, bombarding rock with millimeter-wave radiation and turning it into ash. The hope is that the technology can make drilling under super-hot conditions cost-effective, something that’s challenging with traditional metal drill bits, which wear out faster at those temperatures.

Mazama, on the other hand, thinks that traditional drill bits will work just fine if they can be kept cool.

The startup’s newly announced technology, Carbon Chill, uses carbon dioxide in place of water-based drilling “mud” to keep the bit cool. At those depths and temperatures, the carbon dioxide turns supercritical, allowing it to hold significant amounts of energy. Just above the drill bit, the CO2 flows through a valve that looks similar to a rocket nozzle. As the gas flows through the valve and expands on the other end, the expansion rapidly cools it, allowing it to absorb significant amounts of heat. The CO2 also removes drill cuttings on its trip to the surface, where it’s filtered and reused.

In both cases, Quaise and Mazama are betting that their technology could leapfrog today’s state-of-the-art commercial geothermal power plants. By going deeper and hotter, they’re hoping to get substantially more power out of each well. Given that drilling accounts for about a quarter to a third of a geothermal power plant’s total costs, drilling fewer wells could make a project more competitive.

Investors have been betting that the technologies will soon prove themselves. Quaise has raised more than $270 million from investors, including Prelude Ventures, Collaborative Fund, and Engine Ventures, with Vinod Khosla stepping in as an angel investor, according to PitchBook. Mazama has raised $171 million from investors, including Khosla Ventures, Doerr Capital, and Bill Gates.

As with any new technology, there’s some risk that the new techniques won’t pay off. But early results suggest they will — Mazama drilled its second well 80% faster than its first, and its CO2-cooling tech cut drilling time another 20%, co-founder and CEO Sriram Vasantharajan told TechCrunch.

That, plus Google’s vote of confidence in Quaise, suggests the race to super-hot rock geothermal is, well, heating up.

#405. Experts warn: Nearly everyone in AI is susceptible to a honey-trap scheme right now

Even as the fall-out for Nvidia over Jensen Huang’s alleged honey-trap scandal is still unfolding, Silicon Valley has yet to reckon with a new reality: many more people among them are at risk.

“Nobody briefs founders on counterintelligence,” Aaron Brown, a former senior CIA officer and current founder of the software company Lumbra, told TechCrunch.

That’s because historically honey traps have been associated with politicians, the military, and national intelligence — the people “behind clearances and a workforce trained to expect an approach,” from spies and other attackers.

But now many of the most valuable secrets “sit inside companies building chips, models, and infrastructure,” Brown says, adding that a spy “wants what the CEO can touch. The roadmap, the export decisions, the customer list, who gets the technology. The CEO is the shortest path to all of it.”

Huang has been accused of falling for just such a honey trap by having an affair with Alice Huang. She’s a former executive of a Singapore neocloud who’s been accused of being a Chinese spy and of smuggling billions of dollars’ worth of restricted Nvidia chips into China.

After the Huang allegations broke, jokes flooded the internet about which tech CEO will be next, but it’s not just CEOs and founders who are at risk. The WSJ reported that OpenAI started warning its employees about outsiders trying to cozy up to them in search of company secrets. Anyone with access to sensitive information — from board members to executive assistants — can be the target of a honey trap.

And typically, the attackers aren’t lovers. Friendship is actually the oldest honey-trap scheme around, Brown said.

Honey traps gain access to their targets as investors, customers, recruiters, and consultancies. Their requests look legitimate on paper and don’t need their targets to fall in love. “They need you to feel comfortable talking,” Brown said. “They last longer because nobody’s looking.”

What’s interesting, too, is the irony. So many tech founders are moving to automate and digitize so much of human life and yet remain vulnerable to the oldest bug around, a person who preys on the human need to be understood by others.

“Why fight through a firewall when you can get close to the person who can open doors, influence decisions, or make requests nobody wants to question?” Perry Carpenter, a criminologist and chief deception strategist at KnowBe4, told TechCrunch.

Once targets realize they have been compromised, the threat can escalate: attackers now have a means of blackmail.

Another irony: AI has made honey-trapping much easier and cheaper. Planning a scheme used to take months or years of research, crafting a persona, knowing the target, and executing a plan. These days, it’s easy to use AI to research a target, generate fake social profiles, fake backgrounds, even fake voice calls.

“AI can help sustain an entire fictional backstory,” Carpenter said. “The technology helps set the stage, but the deception ultimately plays out through human relationships.”

The AI industry “became a national security industry faster than its culture caught up,” Brown said.

“Part of being honey-trapped is not knowing,” he added. “It doesn’t feel like manipulation. It feels genuine, even lucky.”

Carpenter said a good first step is looking for unusual patterns. Note, for instance, “coincidental encounters that happen a little too often,” he said. There are also obvious signs, like persistent curiosity about work and colleagues, or requesting secrecy about meeting up, and pressing for introductions or information.

Bigger red flags include if someone’s requests are getting bigger, pushing the boundaries of what you usually would be uncomfortable doing. “A simple gut check,” Brown said. “Would this person still be around if you lost your job tomorrow?”

It also helps to try and understand when emotions might be influencing a decision. Carpenter suggests putting up a clear boundary between one’s personal and professional life. Within companies, it also helps to “eliminate executive exceptionalism,” he said, the idea that the rules don’t apply to people in power at the company.

Brown added that company culture plays another role, too: Are employees comfortable with raising concerns, challenging unusual requests, and escalating possible threats? “One of the most dangerous security cultures is one where nobody feels comfortable questioning the CEO,” Carpenter said.

“Founders need to take risks, and they need to explore business opportunities,” Brown said. “Paranoia doesn’t help. Recognizing you’re a target does.”

On a Reddit page dedicated to discussing Wikipedia, someone posted that they had found what seemed very likely to be Kreiss-Tomkins’ account — a page for a user named Jonathan.s.kt from Sitka, Alaska who went to Yale, liked mountaineering, and made 13,358 Wikipedia edits between 2005 and 2011. The governor-elect is also from Alaska, went to Yale, and climbs mountains.

Wikipedia editors are an impressive community: They keep one of the most important sources of information online (mostly) clean and accurate. This only makes Kreiss-Tomkins look better as a legislator. Check this guy out! A dedicated public servant to the global constituency of Wikipedia! But a moderator on the Wikipedia subreddit pointed out that we cannot be 100% certain that this account is his.

“The odds that somebody would spend six years creating articles about Sitka, Alaska, Yale, and uploading PD photos of mountains, all to deceive people into thinking they were a disenchanted Howard Dean volunteer who was from Sitka, attended Yale, and was a mountaineer, is effectively zero. Plus they uploaded a photo of themself that looks like every other photo of JKT, minus the bald part,” one commenter replied.

Fair enough. If someone created a Wikipedia account pretending to be some guy in Alaska, made over 13,000 Wikipedia edits, and then waited two decades until that guy became the governor of America’s 49th state in order to sabotage him by making him look like a conscientious denizen of the web, kudos to them. But I’m a journalist. I have literal nightmares about getting stuff wrong. If you’re not careful, it’s easy to get duped.

So I emailed Kreiss-Tomkins, who is probably fielding many more important messages right now about policy, and asked if this is, in fact, his Wikipedia account.

So, there you have it, people of Alaska — your next governor is a nerd.

#393. Is selling an AI version of your face to this microdrama startup the future of Hollywood?

For the most part, the content offered by the microdrama startup Shortical is indistinguishable from the rest of its industry. The company produces soapy, melodramatic storylines — almost always risqué romances — that, in bite-sized two-minute episodes, compel viewers to stay glued to their phones so as not to miss the latest twist in a tawdry, cliff-hanger-happy saga.

The primary way that Shortical — which was founded last January — makes money is through subscriptions. Typically, the first 7-10 episodes of a micro-drama are free. After that, if the user wants to finish the series, they have to pay a small monthly fee which unlocks access to all of the site’s content.

Over the past year, the company has churned out a gush of popular live-action romances like “Crossing the Line with My Neighbor,” “Accidentally Married My Boss,” and “Spoiled by My Billionaire Sugar Daddy.”

Lately, however, the company’s homepage is covered in AI-generated content. The reason isn’t exactly hard to figure out: the production overhead for AI content is substantially less than live-action. The profit potential, meanwhile, is substantially higher, if you can draw an audience.

Its latest microdrama premiered in Los Angeles last week. The series, called “Inevitable,” stars Aki Avni, an Israeli film and television star who has been visible in American TV shows for years (he had a stint on Fox’s 9/11-era thriller “24” years ago) but is now trying something largely unheard of in modern Hollywood: licensing his face to Shortical for the sake of its AI content.

The project, which is wholly AI, used Avni’s face to create a digital avatar of him. In “Inevitable,” he plays himself – in a very meta plot in which he — the actor Aki Avni — licenses his face to a film studio, after which thriller-like antics ensue.

This is Shortical’s first foray into such a licensing deal, but it likely won’t be their last.

In a phone interview, Avni told TechCrunch that he found the project both nerveracking and exciting. “Look, it’s a new era for artists,” the actor said. “AI is a new kind of art. It’s a new format. It’s always great to be the pioneer in something.”

Guy Shimoni, the CEO of Shortical, emphasized the creative potential of the new tool. “One of the things that AI does is it enables smaller teams to be able to produce these movie-scale productions,” Shimoni said. “When we were doing mobile-first live action, we were limited by budget, but now we can serve them with epic fantasies. We have kingdoms and dragons. We have superheroes. We have werewolves.”

But AI-generated romances involving werewolves — who, exactly, is watching this kind of thing?

Apparently, a lot of people. While China boasts the biggest microdrama audience globally, Shimoni says that most of his platform’s viewers are actually in the U.S. The industry’s viewer demographic skews heavily female. Many viewers may watch them to wind down at night, getting minor dopamine hits as they drift off to sleep. Many of the AI-generated programs that already exist on the company’s website appear to have millions of views.

Now, Shortical is betting on the creation of a new generation of creators — those who, like Avni, are willing to surrender their likenesses for compensation. “LICENSE YOUR FACE” Shortical’s website advertises, explaining that interested parties can submit their social media handles to the company, which will then decide whether to offer them “a contract setting out the terms” of using their persona in the company’s AI-generated content.

A company rep told TechCrunch that this “offering is still in its early days and hasn’t yet resulted in any signed contracts.” They added: “The series with Aki is the first time Shortical has used a real person’s likeness, and it was done through a direct agreement with him rather than through the platform. Shortical hopes the series will draw more attention to face licensing and plans to promote the offering more actively following this launch.”

Much of this trend has already taken hold in China where the microdrama industry originated, where AI-generated content is big, and where face-licensing is already a trend. Although the trend may not be a particularly profitable one for creators. A recent report in Rest of World found that Microdrama firms were paying people “between US$15 to US$700 to license their facial likenesses.”

How much money is Shortical offering for its face-licensing deals? The company isn’t saying. As for if Shortical will offer royalty-based compensation for creators — like the residual system in television, “It’s a case by case [basis],” Shimoni answers vaguely.

A Shortical representative further explained to TechCrunch that the company didn’t want to “share a specific figure” for Avni’s particular contract. However, the person said, he came “on as a partner in the project, and his compensation was in line with industry standards for a comparable non-AI acting role.”

For other actors, however, the deals may be quite different. Plus, many creatives are understandably unsettled by signs of automation creeping into their industry. Until recently, microdramas seemed like a safe, entry-level gateway into the entertainment industry, where young actors could gain experience on sets and hone their craft. Now, companies are downsizing and producing content that doesn’t even require real actors.

Jennifer Cooper, a longtime fan of the industry and founder of the consultancy firm Vertical Drama Love, notes that there’s a lot of distrust of AI — both from audiences and within the microdrama industry itself.

“Everyone’s been impacted by AI,” she said. “A lot of us have either lost work to AI or can see that it’s devastated this industry — [taking microdramas] from something that was a rare thing in the world, that was in growth, and providing work, particularly for young people…[and now] you know, it’s been decimated.”

Questions of consent with AI likenesses have also dogged the industry (some creators claim they have had their likenesses stolen), which is something that Shortical is obviously trying to address with its new face-licensing endeavor.

Yet these licensing deals could also help to gig-ify the industry, creating a scenario in which creators pay a distributor for the chance to platform their content (or their face), then receive payouts based on the audience engagement they’re allowed to generate. Shortical recently partnered with a company called Payouts.com, which describes itself as a “payout infrastructure platform powering creator and gig economy disbursements at scale.” The plan is to create a system by which creatives can seamlessly interact with Shortical’s platform and get paid.

Cooper believes the push towards AI in the industry is driven by broader financial trends. Most of the microdrama business is led by startups, and VCs love to hear a growth game plan involving automation. “For anyone to get investment at the moment in microdramas, they’ve got to mention the word AI,” she says. “It’s like AI equals gold in everyone’s eyes.”

Despite all the investment in this space, Cooper seems skeptical that there is actually a huge audience for this kind of content. “Some of it does get views. A lot of it doesn’t,” she said, of the AI content. “I think it’s being pushed on an audience more than there is an audience demand or wish for it.”

#390. 'We slept here': Flight delays linger as airlines recover from ground stop, but schedule improves Monday

U.S. flights delays eased on Monday as airlines scrambled to get back on track after the Federal Aviation Administration grounded departures nationwide for more than four hours on Friday.

Nearly 300 U.S. flights were canceled and close to 1,000 were delayed Monday, a fraction of Sunday's disruptions, according to FlightAware data.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy over the weekend tied the issue that prompted officials to call for the ground stop to a router in southern Arkansas and work to update copper wires to a fiber-optic system overseen by L3Harris Technologies. FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford said the upgrades were part of "routine" maintenance to switch over to fiber optic.

Officials ordered the ground stop before 4 p.m. ET on Friday and it lasted until after 8 p.m. ET, marking the longest halt to U.S. flight departures since 9/11.

Hundreds of thousands of travelers were stranded and about 18,000 U.S. flights were canceled from Friday through Monday morning, according to FlightAware.

Over that period, American Airlines had the most cancellations with 5,367 flights, close to 22% of its schedule, followed by Delta Air Lines with 3,434 cancellations, or 17% of its schedule, according to aviation data firm Cirium. Southwest Airlines and United Airlines each canceled more than 16% of their schedules. Bad weather on Thursday and Friday had also contributed to disruptions before the ground stop.

On Sunday, disruptions lingered but were starting to improve.

"We slept here," said Beth Martin, who was trying to travel home to Birmingham, Alabama, from New York's LaGuardia Airport on Sunday at the end of her daughter's birthday trip. The two were booked for Saturday night, and when delay announcements started rolling in from Delta, they decided to make good use of the extra time: After strolling through Central Park, they went window shopping on Fifth Avenue.

But when the flight was canceled, the standby list for a backup flight looked grim and their original midtown hotel was charging about $700 for an additional night, the two slept at LaGuardia on Saturday. She said they found wheelchairs to sleep in outside security.

Airlines waived change fees and fare differences and offered pilots and flight attendants extra pay to ease the delays.

Customers told CNBC they were booked on several flights before getting on board.

She tried five different flights before getting on one to Houston, then flew four hours out of her way to get back to Minneapolis after her Saturday flight was canceled. Fritz said she had so many boarding passes that the Transportation Security Administration wouldn't let her through security at first.

"It's been bananas," she told CNBC at the gate. Fritz said she made the best of it by shopping and getting a haircut, but she's still disappointed.

"I'm missing the first hour of the Vikings game," she said.

Martin said her trip home with her daughter will take even longer. Delta booked them to Memphis, and her husband will drive the four hours from Birmingham to pick them up.

Officials are investigating the root cause that led to what the FAA described as "instability" in data feeds. Duffy said Sunday that two routers, the original one detected as a problem in southern Arkansas, and another in Amarillo, Texas, are in an L3Harris lab in Florida.

"We are supporting the FAA in its investigation to understand the conditions that led to the Nov. 13 ground stop of our nation's air travel," L3Harris said in a statement Saturday afternoon.

President Donald Trump said Monday he didn't "take any responsibility" for the ground stop.

Shortly after the ground stop, Duffy said he was asking Congress for as much as $50 billion for upgrades to the aging U.S. air traffic control system. A day later, Democratic lawmakers pushed back, citing unspent money from existing funding, which includes Congress' approval last year of $12.5 billion for airport and air traffic control improvements over five years.

"Our committee, on behalf of the flying public, will hold the administration accountable for what happened to make sure this chaos does not happen again," said Reps. Rick Larsen, D.-Wash., ranking member of the House Committee on Transportation and Infrastructure, and Andre Carson, D.-Ind., ranking member of the aviation subcommittee, in a joint statement.

#389. Here are the top AI agents that can live in your text messages

Rather than downloading another app, a growing number of agents can simply be texted like an ordinary person.

You text it what you need, and it can remember context, connect to the apps and services you already use, and complete tasks on your behalf. That can mean scheduling an appointment, organizing a calendar, researching a trip, sending an email, making a reservation, shopping online, or reminding you about something days later.

While Instinct is one of the buzziest AI agents at present, thanks to its $10 billion valuation after its latest funding round of $1 billion, there are many others also making a play for this space.

Below are the most notable options so far, from general-purpose personal assistants to agents designed for families, travel, and work.

Ask Proxy’s biggest differentiator is that it “thinks like a woman,” meaning it aims to anticipate your needs before you even ask, the company explains on its website.

It works like a chatbot you can text like a friend, whether you need gift ideas, help planning an event, or recommendations for things to do that weekend. You can ask it to add important dates to your calendar, set reminders, or help with everyday decisions. You can also send it photos, like an empty table, to figure out how to decorate the table for a dinner party.

Unlike a typical chatbot, Ask Proxy also takes a proactive approach. It can reach out when it notices something you might forget or when it comes across a new activity, restaurant, or experience you might enjoy.

There’s also a work-focused version, which the company explains “gets employees back to work faster, while keeping personal data off company systems.”

The personal version costs $1 per day. For businesses, pricing starts at $15 per employee per month for large companies and $23 per employee per month for small teams with fewer than 49 employees.

Ask Proxy works over SMS. You can text “start” to 820-732-2625.

Caddy is an AI assistant that turns the information scattered across your phone into things you can actually act on. It lives in iMessage for iPhone users and RCS for Android users, so there’s no separate app or inbox to constantly check.

For instance, an email contains an appointment that needs to be added to your calendar or a friend sends a list of things to pick up. Instead of worrying about all the details getting buried, Caddy connects to your calendars and conversations, then identifies things that may require action. It can add events to your calendar, set reminders, keep track of follow-ups, and even do research for you.

Caddy has been available in public beta since April 2026.

Comma is designed to manage tasks across both work and everyday life. Comma carries tasks through to completion, checking its own work and determining when a task is finished.

Completed work is surfaced for review when a decision or approval is needed. It also proactively notifies users and recommends the next steps.

It can work across a user’s browser, computer, and files, while remaining accessible remotely through messaging apps such as Signal, Telegram, and WeChat. Comma maintains context and memory across devices and conversations, and can deliver daily briefings, flag urgent matters, and summarize recorded calls.

Fambot is an AI-powered “chief of staff” for families, designed to bring together the many moving parts of family life, including school communications, sports activities, meal planning, calendars, and other day-to-day responsibilities, and turn them into an organized plan.

The service is designed to work across both an app and text messages. Fambot is available on iOS devices, Android devices, and the web, and families can also interact with it directly over SMS. Every night, Fambot automatically sends a summary of the following day, including upcoming events, to-dos, and details such as school uniform or packing requirements. Parents can reply to those messages to ask questions, check information, or make changes to their calendar.

Fambot currently connects to Gmail, Google Calendar, Outlook, and WhatsApp, with support for Apple Calendar planned for the future.

The company launched in beta in early September 2026 and has raised $3.5 million in pre-seed funding. The service is currently free during the beta period, but Fambot expects to eventually charge roughly the equivalent of a Netflix subscription.

Folk is designed to be an AI assistant you can reach through iMessage, WhatsApp, and Telegram. It can remember personal context, manage reminders, research topics, track flights, handle email, and perform real-world tasks such as restaurant reservations.

One distinction is that Folk runs on its own private cloud computer. It can also run code and execute multi-step tasks rather than merely responding conversationally.

Folk launched its beta in May 2026, and it’s available for free but also offers a Pro subscription for $8.33/month if you want unlimited tasks running in the background.

Haven distinguishes itself by presenting the concept of a “digital twin” rather than merely serving as a text-based AI assistant.

According to the company, Haven understands and adapts to your preferences, routines, and decision-making styles, creating a personalized memory graph based on your interactions. As the company describes, “A personal assistant knows what you asked it to do. Haven knows what you would do and how you would do it.”

Notably, the company envisions future features that customize your experiences across devices, like TV and car.

Currently, Haven is available for free in beta on iPhone and Android devices, and the web. You can also reach Haven by calling or texting (435) 260-6500.

Instinct first made headlines after raising $350 million at a $2.5 billion valuation. In September 2026, it raised $1 billion, valuing it at $10 billion.

The always-available agent can take actions on your behalf, rather than simply answer questions. Users can communicate with Instinct by text or voice and connect it to the apps and services they already use, such as email, calendar, Google Workspace, and more. The company says early users have used Instinct for tasks ranging from planning trips and buying groceries to booking tickets and canceling subscriptions.

Powered by Hermes Agent, Iris is designed to let users delegate tasks to an AI assistant directly through iMessage. Users can connect the apps they use and hand over a task with a simple message. The agent then works through the required steps, while retaining relevant context from previous interactions. It’s also designed to follow up with users proactively rather than waiting for another request.

Martin is a general-purpose personal assistant reachable through SMS as well as phone, WhatsApp, email, Slack, and its own iOS app.

It can help manage calendars, email, reminders, tasks, notes, and communications. It can also act on behalf of users by sending messages or making phone calls, while providing daily summaries of what needs attention.

It’s a bit on the pricier side, with subscriptions starting at $21 per month.

Maverick describes itself as an “AI companion who lives in your texts,” acting as a personal assistant that helps manage everyday tasks directly from your messages.

It can fix typos, cancel subscriptions, schedule meetings from voice notes, and help group chats settle on a date for an event. One notable feature is that Maverick asks for your permission before taking action.

The platform also offers hundreds of integrations, including Discord, Gmail, and Slack, allowing it to connect with the apps you already use.

It’s currently in beta and free to use on iMessage. There’s also a Pro version for €27 per month (or approximately $30.25 USD), which includes 500 tasks a month,  20 connected apps, scheduled tasks, and memory.

Miso takes the personal-assistant concept and applies it specifically to travel.

It operates through iMessage and combines AI trip planning with support from a dedicated travel team. Users can arrange flights through messaging while Miso takes into account their travel preferences, loyalty points, and other priorities. Miso also gives travelers access to trip details and flight updates, along with personalized recommendations.

Ohai is another family assistant focused on helping households manage the constant stream of information and tasks involved in everyday family life.

Users can send messages, forward emails, or make voice requests, and the assistant can transform them into schedules, reminders, and plans. It can also coordinate calendars, household chores, and meals across multiple members of a family.

It includes a free basic option, but also offers different subscriptions based on family size, starting at $9.99 per month.

Orbits also has an AI assistant that is focused on the household.

It works through both the Orbits app and text messaging, and can bring together calendars, lists, and conversations while also carrying out tasks for you. That can include searching the web, making purchases, booking restaurants, changing appointments, and requesting quotes for household services.

Additionally, Orbits can remind family members about chores, communicate with service providers, and help coordinate responsibilities beyond the immediate household.

Orbits is backed by investors like Andreessen Horowitz (a16z) Speedrun fund, N49P, and Garage Capital.

Users can interact with Pally as they would with a friend while giving it access to the apps they already rely on. The assistant can connect information spread across services such as WhatsApp, Gmail, calendars, and Google Drive, helping users keep track of plans, receipts, dates, files, and other details.

There’s a free plan available. It works over iMessage, RCS, and Telegram.

Poke launched in March 2026 as a general-purpose assistant that you can reach through text messaging. It can handle tasks involving calendars, planning, health and fitness, smart-home controls, photos, and other everyday activities.

Poke became particularly notable in June 2026 when Apple approved Poke as the first AI agent on the Apple Messages for Business platform. A month later, parent company The Interaction Company of California was acquired by AI coding startup Cognition in a deal valued in the low nine figures.

Rene is an AI personal assistant designed to be a user’s “best contact” rather than another productivity agent. It’s text-based and can turn natural-language conversations into tasks, reminders, summaries, and other follow-up actions. Rene also maintains context across conversations, allowing it to remember details and surface things that may need attention later.

Additionally, it has a browser, can write code, shop for you, as well as create slides and images.

Rene is currently available on iMessage, Telegram, and WhatsApp.

Instead of waiting for users to open an app, Skye is an “agentic homescreen” for the iPhone that analyzes information from connected services and surfaces what it thinks is relevant through contextual cards, suggestions, and daily briefings. The assistant can generate audio briefings, prepare information ahead of meetings, surface reminders, and identify potentially useful information.

The company, Signull Labs, raised about $3.6 million in seed funding in 2025. It entered private testing and beta in April 2026, and hasn’t yet announced a public release date.

Stanley is an AI content assistant aimed at content creators rather than general personal productivity. Available through iMessage, Telegram, and the web, it acts as a kind of AI head of content, helping users come up with ideas, turn them into posts, and maintain a consistent publishing schedule.

For instance, Stanley can connect to a user’s Instagram account, using that context to suggest content based on what the user has actually been doing. Users can also send voice notes, which Stanley turns into scripts, captions, and other social assets. It includes a content calendar and can schedule posts, with the broader goal of making content creation a more continuous, automated process rather than something users have to sit down and plan from scratch.

Szn is designed to coordinate tasks and follow-ups through a single conversation thread. Users can send text messages, voice notes, photos, or forwarded emails, and the assistant takes action on their behalf, including making calls, sending emails, and managing follow-ups.

According to the company’s website, Szn has served more than 7,500 businesses across 16 countries, completed over 10,000 jobs, and handled 95 hours of calls.

Tab allows users to delegate everyday tasks through familiar messaging platforms. Users can send requests to the assistant via iMessage or WhatsApp, ranging from ordering groceries to finding birthday gifts, and Tab handles the process on their behalf.

The company emerged from stealth in early October with a valuation of $300 million. Its investors include SV Angel, Valar Ventures, and American Spirit.

The biggest differentiator with Tomo’s personal assistant for iMessage is that, instead of being a private assistant that only you interact with, Tomo can become part of group conversations.

Also, like other agents, it’s designed to help with goals, accountability, scheduling, and everyday organization. It also has the ability to send reminders, search the web, work with calendars and email, watch videos, and edit photos.

The basic plan starts at $19.99 per month.

Town is aimed more at professional life. It gives users a “Townie,” an AI assistant designed to learn how they work and handle recurring professional tasks. It can work with email, calendars, documents, Slack, and other platforms.

In June 2026, the company raised a $55 million Series A led by Andreessen Horowitz and Forerunner.

The company plans to offer the app for free initially and has stated that it will never include advertising.

Wajo’s first consumer agent, Fo, can call businesses, send emails, join group chats, make bookings and purchases, and coordinate with people to complete everyday tasks.

A key part of Wajo’s approach is giving Fo its own identity. The agent has its own email address, phone number, and payment card, allowing it to interact with businesses without requiring users to hand over their own credentials.

Notably, when Fo encounters something it cannot handle on its own, Wajo says it can bring in a human assistant to finish the task.

Fo launched publicly in September 2026 with a free tier, while heavier workloads and custom agents are available through a paid Pro offering.

#383. Robert Pattinson cast as next James Bond

Robert Pattinson has been cast as the next James Bond in the upcoming film from director Denis Villeneuve. It will be the 26th James Bond film and the first since Amazon MGM took over the iconic franchise.

Pattinson will be the seventh actor to play the role. Monday's announcement — which came in part via an Instagram post — ends a long period of speculation about who would take over as "007" after Daniel Craig.

"James Bond is one of the most legendary characters in cinema, and to be entrusted with 007 is the honour of a lifetime," Pattinson said in a statement. "I couldn't be more excited about this partnership and the work ahead with this fantastic team."

Pattinson has appeared in several big-ticket films this year, including Christopher Nolan's "The Odyssey" and psychological thriller "Primetime," and he'll soon appear in "Dune: Part Three," also directed by Villeneuve. The upcoming Bond film will have a script by Steven Knight and will be produced by Amy Pascal, known for her work on the Spider-Man franchise, and David Heyman, who produced all eight Harry Potter films.

"We were surrounded by extraordinary talent, but there can only be one 007," Villeneuve said. "I am overjoyed to welcome Robert Pattinson as our new James Bond. He has the timeless soul of 007 and brings a spirit that is completely new. I cannot wait to begin."

The film is still untitled, and Amazon MGM hasn't announced a projected release date.
