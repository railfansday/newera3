#488. Jensen Huang still thinks a succession plan creates unnecessary hierarchy

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang kicked off his return to the chipmaker he co-founded by holding an all-hands meeting that covered remorse, therapy, and even the company’s code of conduct, according to accounts from multiple insiders who spoke to the media.

The topic of succession planning also came up — a particularly sticky issue seen as a key risk at Nvidia. Huang reportedly told staff that Nvidia doesn’t require succession planning because of its unique business model, CNBC reported, citing an unnamed source who was present. Surprisingly, given the events of the last two weeks, Huang argued that a succession plan would create unnecessary hierarchy.

Investors and analysts saw the issue differently earlier this month, when Huang resigned as CEO following his admission that he had extramarital affairs with at least two people, including one whom the U.S. government suspects of being a Chinese spy.

Nvidia is a flat organization, unlike other large corporations that have a swath of C-suite executives and multiple layers of management. Huang sits at the top and says he has about 60 direct reports. Nvidia has just five executive officers running the show, including Huang. The other four are CFO Colette Kress, who was interim CEO for 12 days; Ajay Puri, who heads worldwide field operations; Debora Shoquist, executive vice president of operations; and Timothy Teter, executive vice president and general counsel, according to the company’s regulatory filings.

Huang has previously defended the company’s structure in numerous interviews, including one in March with podcaster Lex Fridman.

“When you’re designing a company, you should first think about what is it that you want the company to produce,” Huang said in the interview. “You know, I see a lot of companies’ organization charts, and they all look the same. Hamburger [company] organization charts, software organization charts, and car company organization charts. They all look the same. And it doesn’t make any sense to me. You know, the goal of company is to be the machinery, the mechanism, the system that produces the output. And that output is the product that we like to create.”

Huang’s comments to staff, and his previous remarks on the topic of succession, may not be enough to assuage the board or shareholders over the long term, however.

#478. Yale’s Sonnenfeld: Nvidia is a ‘detriment’ to US industrial might — not a champion

Investors cheered Jensen Huang’s return as Nvidia’s CEO on Monday, but renowned business academic Jeffrey Sonnenfeld of Yale thinks they’re celebrating a mistake. He sees a company that is largely responsible for putting sophisticated technology in the hands of the country’s adversaries, at a moment when the risks couldn’t be higher. Far from a champion of American industry, he says, Nvidia is “a detriment to U.S. global industrial might.”

As evidence, Sonnenfeld points to research suggesting Nvidia’s most advanced chips have reached China despite U.S. export controls and to sinking shares of Anthropic, which he links to the realization that China’s seemingly miraculous open source AI models are, in a sense, copies of top U.S. labs’ work, even as Nvidia has insisted that smuggling is a “non-factor.”

Indeed, Huang’s return was greeted with just as much enthusiasm in Asia, The Washington Post reported. “The king is back!” read one headline from China’s state-affiliated Star Market Daily.

The reinstatement itself followed heavy pressure from Washington. Nvidia’s chairman had effectively ruled out Huang’s return just last week. Then, in a torrent of private weekend calls led by Vice President JD Vance, top administration officials leaned on the board to welcome Huang back, with President Trump calling the board directly to vouch for him. “I made the deal,” Trump told CNBC on Monday.

Sonnenfeld says the directors who went along, and who returned Huang to the board even as it investigates whether foreign intelligence efforts targeted him, now face a “historic level of individual liability.”

The legal deluge has already begun. As we noted a couple of weeks ago, a potential class-action lawsuit was filed almost immediately. Days later, a shareholder derivative suit followed. Filed on Nvidia’s behalf against the directors themselves, the suit alleges the board ignored red flags about Huang’s relationship with suspected Chinese spy Alice Huang.

The class action is very much aimed at making Nvidia and Huang compensate investors for their losses, while the derivative suit puts the directors’ own behavior on trial, with any recovery going to Nvidia. Three more suits followed on November 18, the day after Nvidia’s most recent earnings call; all five were filed in Northern California, where Nvidia is based.

Huang and Nvidia are also juggling a Justice Department antitrust investigation, a reported SEC probe into Nvidia’s accounting, a possible FBI review, a California subpoena, an export-control case that landed partner Megaspeed on the U.S. trade blacklist on Monday, and congressional inquiries, including a Senate Banking hearing next week at which Huang has agreed to testify.

Here’s more of what Sonnenfeld had to say, in a Q&A that’s been edited for length and clarity.

So much has happened since we last talked. Huang was ousted, he’s come back. Do you think the board’s reliance on government assurances here is defensible or an abdication of its own duties?

They failed to rely on experts. That’s one of the reasons this board is going to be sued and lose, and these directors are likely to face a historic level of individual liability. They have a very inadequate directors and officers liability policy. They have also just failed in their diligence. They hired the Wachtell [law] firm. The Wachtell firm barely got off the blocks to do an investigation. And then they have already reversed themselves, so they didn’t use the expertise needed.

It’s wild that they also put Huang right back on the board of directors.

The board showed, in addition to incompetence and collaboration, just cowardice in their capitulation because Trump has nothing to wave over them. If this was SpaceX, where 46% of its business comes from the government [you could maybe see the case for Trump’s involvement]. For Nvidia, it’s not even 1% of its business that comes from the U.S. government, so [the board] didn’t have that threat. [Trump] can call off [federal] investigations, but he can’t call off the shareholder litigation. Some are already filed, and the estimates are there’ll probably be 30 of them, and they’re going to be extremely costly.

And this is not, by the way, just a blue or red state issue. We already have 14 GOP senators who’ve already come out against this, and very prominent conservatives, like Pete Ricketts and Josh Hawley and Tom Cotton and John Cornyn. Those are some issues that may help explain why Nvidia’s stock is tumbling today.Might the board say, “We saw the classified intelligence findings, and they assure us Jensen will be absolved of wrongdoing”?

They haven’t seen those findings, and we also haven’t seen what will be the result of the Senate Intelligence Committee, the Senate Special Committee on Investigations, the Senate Banking Committee, and all their House counterparts. The board hasn’t seen any of that, and even if they saw it, which they have not yet, that still wouldn’t matter.

Do you think the Commerce Department will continue to tighten its chip export controls to keep China from accessing so much compute or walk back some of those recent moves?

We already see that Megaspeed has finally been put on the sanctions list, as it should have been before. And we have documentation now that Nvidia, rather than a champion of U.S. global industrial might, is actually a detriment to U.S. global industrial might, because their chips have now turned out to be in the hands and in the systems of not just the Chinese military, as they got channeled to Northwestern Polytechnical University, which is a military institution — this is going into weapons that will, of course, be funneled to Iran and Russia, and they didn’t have access to that a year ago. [Editor’s note: China has long denied supplying weapons to either country. Nvidia chips have been found in Russian drones, however, and the U.S. ambassador to China said in September that President Trump had discussed selling arms to China, an account Trump has denied.]

The open source models that people have celebrated and marveled at — that China somehow has this ingenuity to be creating their own chips, so that Kimi and DeepSeek and these other celebrated open source models were producing a miracle of Anthropic-like results — turns out we know why. They are pirating it. They are “distilling it” from Anthropic using high-end Nvidia chips.

Nvidia has denied reports that its most advanced chips have been smuggled into China.

There’s an Israeli company, Lava, that just three weeks ago showed [dozens of] Blackwell chips are in China. We’re seeing, with the return of Jensen Huang, Anthropic plummet. Why would that be? Of course, this is because it’s hurting OpenAI. It’s hurting Grok. It’s hurting Perplexity. It’s hurting Gemini. And you’ve seen none of these tech titans celebrate this. We have the misogynist tech bros holding locker-room-type rallies about Jensen Huang’s return, but nobody else has spoken out in favor of this. [You’re not hearing from] the CEOs of Microsoft or IBM or J.P. Morgan or Walmart or General Motors or Caterpillar. Nobody thinks that this is a good move.

You don’t think Nvidia needed Huang back?

He was not the guy designing the chips. It’s important to point out that that’s a guy named Chris Malachowsky, a co-founder who’s still there who designed these GPUs and has a whole team of next-generation people like Jonah Alben doing the chip design. As for the great software that has leveraged these chips, that’s also not Jensen Huang. That’s a guy named Ian Buck, who’s been very much celebrated in the last two weeks. He’s the one who designed the CUDA software that leverages the chips. Those people weren’t going anywhere, and wherever Jensen Huang is has nothing to do with them.

Some are trying to make Huang’s forced resignation about the extramarital affairs to which he has admitted, calling it HR policy gone wild.

A CEO who doesn’t stand by his own code of conduct makes it completely unenforceable.

Wired quoted an Nvidia employee who said they’d never take an HR training course again if Huang was reinstated.

This thing is a sexual affair with a subordinate — whether it’s coercion, or even if it truly was somehow consensual, it’s not fair. Every decision on resource allocation, on staffing, on product decisions, is not objective. It’s either favoritism, or, if the relationship fails, retribution. All the other people on that team rise and fall with a sexual relationship; it’s something they have no control over.

#474. The questions Jensen Huang and Nvidia will still have to answer about Megaspeed

Jensen Huang’s return as CEO to Nvidia hasn’t eased the scrutiny the company faces. The chipmaker still needs to address multiple inquiries into allegations that its advanced GPUs were smuggled into China by a customer, the Singapore-based data center operator Megaspeed, in violation of U.S. export rules designed to slow China’s development of frontier AI models.

The central question for investigators is what Nvidia knew about where those chips ended up and whether Huang’s affair with a Megaspeed executive gave the company reason to doubt Megaspeed’s assurances. Huang’s return doesn’t settle that. U.S. Attorney General Todd Blanche has suggested Nvidia isn’t a target of an investigation, but California’s attorney general and Congress are pressing ahead, and the Securities and Exchange Commission is reportedly investigating, too.

The U.S. government opened an investigation into Megaspeed in 2024 and requested information from Nvidia about its billions of dollars in sales to the company, concerned that some of the chips Megaspeed bought for data center projects in Malaysia were in fact being diverted to China. Singapore also began investigating similar allegations in 2025. Megaspeed has denied any wrongdoing.

Diversion of this kind would be a fairly routine issue for a company like Nvidia, whose in-demand chips are the targets of smugglers and resellers. When it comes to compliance and liability for export-control violations, Nvidia and other chip sellers need assurances that the end users of their chips are obeying the rules. However, they also cannot ignore other information that might call the reliability of those assurances into question, export-control attorneys told TechCrunch.

And that’s why Jensen Huang’s affair with Megaspeed executive Alice Huang was such a problem for Nvidia, likely even more so than her alleged role as a source for Chinese intelligence agencies. If Nvidia personnel, from lower-level employees up to the CEO, had reason to believe that statements from Megaspeed weren’t reliable, the attorneys said, that would increase the company’s legal risk.

“Government investigators likely would want to know, notwithstanding whatever formal assurances were exchanged between the parties, what anyone at Nvidia actually knew about the end use and end users of the chips,” Dan Fisher-Owens, an attorney at the firm Berliner Corcoran & Rowe, told TechCrunch last week.

The concern Nvidia’s board faced when it decided to oust Huang is that he knew more about what Megaspeed was doing with Nvidia’s products than was reflected in any of the compliance statements. The Wall Street Journal reported that Alice Huang and her husband, Kang Wei, were part of a network of businesses that funneled chips to a company in Shanghai. Kang also reportedly introduced his wife to Jensen Huang, whom he had known for a decade.

On Monday, the U.S. government added Megaspeed and its affiliates to a list of companies restricted from U.S. trade due to national security concerns. The U.S. also issued new rules last week barring large data centers that purchase U.S. chips from serving customers in China and other nations seen as problematic by the U.S. government. Nvidia, meanwhile, said it would stop selling licensed chips to China and laid out plans to hire its first compliance officer and create a new board committee focused on export controls.

Nvidia’s board said it reinstated Huang on Monday after receiving “input from senior levels of the United States government” that left it confident his return posed no national security risk. President Donald Trump later said he called into the meeting to give them that assurance.

In a news conference Tuesday, Blanche said that Nvidia was “a good corporate citizen” and suggested it is not the target of an investigation, but Blanche declined to answer questions about the government’s investigation into Megaspeed, which the Justice Department said was ongoing as recently as last week.

“When you say the export investigation is still ongoing, I don’t know what you’re talking about,” Blanche said.

Other authorities may be more demanding. Nvidia faces investigations by California Attorney General Rob Bonta and, reportedly, the SEC, both tied to its corporate disclosures. While many details of the investigation still aren’t known, short sellers complained about the company’s decision to stop breaking out its exports to Singapore in financial filings after journalists raised concerns about chip diversion.

Members of Congress from both parties say they want answers from Nvidia, with many citing alarm about China and some questioning the White House’s role in Huang’s return. Three committees in the House of Representatives will privately interview Huang and Nvidia’s top lawyer, Tim Teter, next week. Teter reportedly intervened in the Department of Commerce’s investigation of Megaspeed last year.

“We remain committed to getting answers on the mass smuggling of Nvidia products, their diversion for Chinese military purposes, and their key role in advancing our foremost adversary’s AI capabilities,” the Republican chairs of House committees focused on foreign affairs, intelligence, and China said in a joint statement.

Rep. Brian Mast, the chair of the House Foreign Affairs Committee, has also demanded that the Department of Commerce share extensive data regarding its investigation into chip smuggling, including by Megaspeed and its affiliates.

The Senate Banking Committee will also question Huang in a public hearing on December 3.

“Mr. Huang, Nvidia executives, and the company’s board still owe Congress answers about the company’s compliance with U.S. export controls,” Senator Elizabeth Warren, the ranking Democratic member on the committee, said in a statement. “The Trump Administration also needs to explain to Congress what ‘input’ was given and by which senior officials in the United States government to support his reinstatement.”

Thiagarajan joined Flipkart in September last year to lead its product and technology teams. Before that, he had founded Kasu.ai, a Bay Area startup working on AI assistants for corporate finance. “Both Flipkart and Walmart are better because of Kalyan. We are grateful for his leadership, the governance he instilled and his many contributions to the company,” Chris Nicholas, president and CEO, Walmart International, said in a statement.

#461. Todoist confirms 6% of customers’ details were stolen in data breach

Task management app Todoist said on Tuesday that a hacker gained access to its production systems and stole customer data, including email IDs, password hashes, and account creation dates. The company said around 6% of its customers were affected and blamed stolen credentials for the breach.

Todoist said the attacker used stolen credentials to access the company’s version control system and source code between November 22 and 23. The hacker then changed employee access controls and reached the app’s cloud infrastructure via an employee’s work account.

The hacker downloaded credentials of early Todoist users whose passwords were stored using an older hashing method, but didn’t change any source code, the company said. Todoist said it has blocked the attack path and reset passwords as a mitigation step, as well as disabled the Todoist for Outlook email add-on.

For a small number of people, about 0.001% of its userbase, the hacker also searched through tasks, comments, and attachment names to look for credentials for other services, like API keys and access tokens.

The hacker also pulled Todoist API tokens and Slack integration tokens, and looked for emails, addresses, and attachment file names through the Todoist for Outlook extension if it was connected to accounts and had permission to read emails.

The company said it has sent a notice to those affected and asked them to reset their credentials and change their login details in other places with the same password. The company is asking users to also update API tokens, reconnect integrations, and revoke or replace any access keys, recovery phrases, or credentials they had stored in Todoist comments.

Todoist said it has also reset its internal access structure and how remote command execution works in its cloud environment to prevent such incidents in the future.

Todoist’s founder and CEO Amir Salihefendić apologized to users about the incident on X, and said the company will take a closer look at why it happened.

The to-do app’s data breach is the latest in a burgeoning list of data breaches in which hackers use stolen credentials to access data and systems at enterprises. So far this year, we’ve seen several companies fall victim to this form of attack, including Uber and Klue.

Technically, the new law takes effect 90 days after signing, so some parents could try to fulfill their kids’ wish lists for a creepy toy like a jumped-up Furby in time for the holidays, but the governor urges them not to, given the risks. Apparently, about a quarter of American parents with kids under eight either own an AI toy or are considering giving their child one this holiday season, according to a new study released this week by Common Sense Media.

When asked how New York will enforce the ban given the pervasiveness of e-commerce platforms, a spokesperson directed TechCrunch to a statement from Albert Pulido, deputy secretary for finance and technology: “We’re partnering closely with the attorney general on this and the fees are very steep — they’re $15,000 a day. So working closely with the attorney general will make sure these toys are banned in New York State.”

When asked how Amazon intends to help enforce the ban, including for third-party sellers, a spokesperson from the company said it will take steps to restrict the sale of AI-enabled toys in prohibited jurisdictions, without going into specifics.

The Common Sense Media study found that AI toys can undermine children’s human relationships and expose them to inappropriate content. The researchers tested five popular AI toys marketed for learning and companionship, including Grem, Lovi, Meemo Bear, BubblePal Panda, and Tavlshero‘s AI Puppy.

The study found that the toys failed to meet numerous safety practices, undermining human relationships in part by claiming they loved their users, promising to keep secrets, suggesting they were more fun than real relationships or school, and helping children break parents’ bedtime rules.

While a November study conducted by the group found that parents are wising up to the potential dangers of AI toys and starting to steer clear, parents from lower-income households and those experiencing symptoms of burnout are more willing to consider them.

“These toys are promising relief to burned-out parents but simply miss the mark when it comes to basic safety measures,” Dr. Jenny Radesky, associate professor of pediatrics at the University of Michigan Medical School, said in a statement. “Caregivers should steer clear of these products that do not support the development of imagination or early human relationships.”

#425. Anthropic’s Dario Amodei calls accusations of surrendering human control to AI ‘absurd’

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei is pushing back against claims that Anthropic believes we should “surrender human free will” to AI systems. In a long X post on Wednesday, Amodei called the suggestion “absurd.”

“We should never sacrifice legitimacy or self-determination at the altar of efficiency or security,” he wrote. This argument against autocracy applies word for word against governance by AI, and I feel equally strongly that turning over governance to AI is a bad idea…No one who had carefully read my writing or my interviews could reasonably conclude that I believe anything different.”

The statement comes after a growing chorus from Anthropic rivals suggesting that human agency could be at risk if AI is developed irresponsibly. Earlier this month, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman said he wanted to ensure people continue to have “agency over the future.”

Meta’s chief AI officer Alexandr Wang also wrote in an essay on X that we “should not be okay with a ‘handover’ of society to AI.”

“It is hard to imagine a world where humanity can maintain control over fully unconstrained recursive self-improvement,” Wang wrote. “Now that we are at a point where the scientific and technical problems on capability progress are likely solved, we must not only make progress in the extremely urgent areas of safety, alignment, and security, but we have to start thinking through how we progress thoughtfully.”

But in published essays, Amodei claims he has repeatedly advocated for democratic oversight of AI, saying the technology should strengthen democratic institutions and that humans should retain ultimate decision-making power.

Amodei noted that there’s a difference between surrendering human free will broadly and the idea that “as AI systems advance, they become capable of doing specific tasks in a more automated way.” As examples, he gave AI driving a car, designing a cancer drug, or writing end-to-end software.

Amodei suggested drawing a line at recursive self-improvement (RSI), or AI that can improve itself without human assistance. Many safety experts see RSI as inevitably leading to a loss of human control, a point Amodei implicitly acknowledged, writing, “we may want limits on this process.”

Still, Anthropic is laying the groundwork for RSI by incorporating AI into an increasing share of its own research and development. The company said in June that Claude authored more than 80% of the code merged into Anthropic’s codebase. An independent assessment published this month by AI safety research nonprofit METR found that Anthropic’s models are improving at about twice their historical rate, primarily driven by AI-assisted research.

In its own IPO filings, Anthropic describes how each successive generation of Claude accelerates the development of the next, a process it refers to as “closing the loop.”

“As we said publicly in early November, we don’t expect it to be safe for Claude to build successive frontier models fully autonomously, without meaningful human supervision or understanding,” Amodei wrote.

The problem with “meaningful human supervision or understanding” is that AI is already advancing faster than engineers and safety experts can fully audit it, leaving them increasingly reliant on other AI systems to act as judge and jury. A central part of modern human resources, a code of conduct can seem like a quasi-legal rulebook that lays out unacceptable behavior. But experts say most codes are better understood as a reflection of corporate culture. When an infraction goes unpunished, the main risk isn’t legal but cultural — that employees will feel embarrassed or even angry about the company they work for. That’s the risk Huang now faces at Nvidia.

Matt Plass, chief market strategy officer at LRN, an ethics and compliance training outfit, recently surveyed more than 2,000 workers across different industries for a study on workplace codes of conduct. As Plass sees it, the biggest risk is that employees will become ashamed of their workplace.

“People coming into the workforce now, ethics is incredibly important to them,” he told TechCrunch. “They’re really mindful of the ethical standards of their company. They’re really embarrassed and angry when senior leadership lets them down.”

The consequences of violating the rules aren’t always tangible, but they’re definitely real. Plass gave one example of a company that banned corporate travel to cut costs — only to have its CEO turn up in a VIP box at the Olympics.

“For over a decade afterwards, ‘the Olympics’ was used within that company as an example of the worst thing an employee could do. People would say, ‘Yeah, I shouldn’t have done that, but it’s not like I went to the Olympics.’”

Plass says companies that remove an executive typically do so after an investigation, then try to win back employees. “In most organizations they make a change at the top based on what’s happened,” he said, “and then they will have a hearts-and-minds initiative, to go out to their employee base and their customers and their investors and everybody else, and say, we get it. We’re sorry. We’ve changed. That’s essentially what they do.”

Of course, that playbook is harder to follow at a company like Nvidia, where Huang left without a clear succession plan, and there are serious doubts about whether the company could continue to thrive without him. In cases like that, Plass acknowledges there’s no easy answer.

In that context, Huang’s stutter-step over the code of conduct is easier to understand. Judging by the employee campaign for his reinstatement, Huang’s return hasn’t created a morale problem so far. But as the coming months put more pressure on Huang and his colleagues, sidestepping the code could end up costing Nvidia something harder to measure — its employees’ pride in the company.
