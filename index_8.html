#534. Nvidia clarifies when it will — and won’t — keep financing neoclouds

One reason 197,000 people, including Elon Musk, signed a petition over the weekend calling on Nvidia to reinstate the company’s ousted CEO Jensen Huang is that he wasn’t just championing the AI boom. He was using Nvidia’s enormous profits to help finance it.

And Nvidia has a lot of money to spend. The company reported on Tuesday net income of $64.9 billion for the third quarter, which was up from $59.7 billion the prior quarter.

But Nvidia’s continued success depends on having enough customers building and expanding data centers to keep buying its AI chips and systems. Under Huang, the company aggressively invested in AI startups and neoclouds and, more recently, began guaranteeing massive loans to finance new data centers. The biggest deal yet was the $105 billion backstop for OpenAI’s PORTS-Pike data center campus in Ohio, announced in August. In exchange for that loan guarantee, OpenAI promised that the campus would exclusively use Nvidia’s equipment, the company said.

Huang’s abrupt departure last week raised questions about whether Nvidia would continue bankrolling its customers at the same pace. There were reports that CFO Colette Kress, now interim CEO, had tried to push back on Huang’s increasingly ambitious dealmaking. She reportedly reduced the backstop guarantee for the OpenAI project from $250 billion down to $105 billion.

Under Kress, Nvidia clarified in its quarterly CFO commentary that guaranteeing a loan doesn’t mean the company is providing the financing itself. The guarantee only takes effect once the customer has secured the financing needed to build the facility and install Nvidia’s products. If the project fails to meet those conditions, the guarantee “doesn’t exist” she said.

“There is the raising of capital that they may need through equity and or debt, in order to build out any of the buildings, get them ready for use, but also the ability to procure the compute, install the compute, and get ready. What we are indicating in our statement there is that is when our guarantee starts. So if all of that begins and unfortunately they are delayed or they have a challenge in terms of building that, our guarantee doesn’t exist anymore,” Kress said to analysts on Tuesday during the quarterly conference call.

She pointed to OpenAI PORTS-Pike campus as an example of where such terms apply. When asked, she also said this wasn’t a change in policy, but simply a clarification of existing terms. However, these disclosures have never been public before.

Kress also made clear that Nvidia wasn’t bailing on all the ways it was financing the AI boom, especially new data centers. But, she said, Nvidia was going to be far more selective in the future.

“The availability of land, power, shell is such a critical part of the whole structure in terms of building out those data centers. So they may need the assistance. That’s a great asset for anyone to hold. So we will look at that as probably one of our top priorities,” she said. At the same time, she insisted that Nvidia was “taking a very strong look in terms of the types of companies that we would want to help in terms of the financing.”

One of the ongoing criticisms Huang faced was his love of circular deals: invest in his customers so they would turn around and buy his products. Kress is now trying to strike a delicate balance. She isn’t saying she will pull the rug out from under Nvidia’s customers. After all, the company’s investments undoubtedly spurred the AI boom that has been so lucrative for Nvidia. But she’s also signaling that the days of Huang’s freewheeling dealmaking may be over.

#525. Sony’s updated turntable is an easy entry into the world of vinyl

Record players and other retro tech are making a strong comeback, and I’ve recently rejoined the world of vinyl collecting. Lately, I’ve noticed myself turning to Spotify less frequently, so it felt like the perfect time to start using a record player again.

Choosing a turntable can be quite overwhelming, especially for beginners. That’s where Sony’s new PS-LX3BT turntable comes in.

Since 2019, Sony’s PS-LX310BT has been a go-to choice for vinyl newbies. Now that it has been discontinued, the PS-LX3BT emerges as its successor, keeping the same beginner-friendly approach while adding a few modern upgrades.

Currently priced at $399.99, the PS-LX3BT is designed for people who want to enjoy vinyl without getting too caught up in the technical side of things. It’s also a good option for those looking to upgrade from their first inexpensive turntable without having to learn how to set up or customize a more complicated audio system.

One of its biggest selling points is how easy it is to use. The PS-LX3BT features automatic playback, so you simply press start and the tonearm lifts, moves into position, and drops onto the first track for you. There’s no need to manually position the needle or worry about getting everything just right. It’s a small convenience, but one that makes the experience much less intimidating for newcomers.

Sony has also made a few welcome changes compared with its predecessor. The PS-LX3BT now uses USB-C for power and supports Qualcomm’s aptX Adaptive Bluetooth codec, allowing it to transmit audio at up to 24-bit/96kHz. It also has a traditional phono output alongside Bluetooth, so you can choose between a wired speaker setup and listening wirelessly.

One slightly strange design decision, however, is the repositioning of the start, stop, and tonearm buttons. On the older model, these were placed outside the dust cover. Now they’re underneath it, meaning you’ll have to lift the cover just to press start. It’s not a huge inconvenience, but still kind of annoying.

The turntable also offers USB connectivity for digitizing your vinyl collection. Connect it to a computer, and you can convert your records into digital audio files, which is useful if you want to preserve older records or listen to your collection on the go.

My biggest disappointment is the lack of a built-in speaker. It would have been nice to have everything included for someone just getting started. You’ll need to buy speakers (and possibly an amplifier) or compatible Bluetooth headphones separately, so keep that added cost in mind.

If you’re searching for a more affordable and beginner-friendly automatic record player, there are cheaper options, such as this $179 option from Audio-Technica. But overall, Sony’s PS-LX3BT is a solid choice for anyone ready to dive into the world of vinyl for the first time.

#517. Sam Altman warns against a future where AI, not humans, calls the shots

OpenAI co-founder and CEO Sam Altman believes that people should stay in charge of the future when it comes to how AI technology impacts the world and everyone’s lives. Speaking at The New York Times DealBook Summit on Wednesday, the AI executive referred to discussions in Silicon Valley about whether there will be a gradual handover to AI — meaning AI will eventually become the decision-making authority across every major industry and institution.

“One thing we’ve been talking a lot about recently is there’s been this thing in Silicon Valley that seems almost ridiculous to have to fight against, but we both believe very deeply that people need to stay in control of the future, and there’s increasing talk about, well, there is either going to be or there should be this sort of gradual handover to AI. And I — we both — agree that that’s a terrible idea,” said Altman, who was sharing the stage with OpenAI co-founder and president Greg Brockman.

The hand-off theory stems from the idea that as AI models become increasingly more powerful, they will learn to improve themselves — a concept known as “recursive self-improvement” or RSI. As existing AI technology builds newer, smarter generations, those will eventually become a superintelligence that humans can no longer control, the belief goes.

Altman explained that he and Brockman only disagree over how to best accomplish the goal of keeping humans in control, or what sort of challenges we should expect to face along the way. However, Altman characterized this as more of a debate between two people who share the same larger views.

“The question of how to actually prevent [handover] from happening — how to actually ensure that we will, given all of the various pressures and given what other companies may try to do — that we are going to actually ensure that people stay in control of the future in a very deep way, and we don’t have this either accidental or intentional handover — I think we can both agree on that,” Altman said.

Brockman added that, as the models improve, there’s an increasing responsibility for alignment, safety, and security, which may require changes in how things are currently done.

“Monitoring is a good example of something where I think we are going to need new … technology,” he said.

Today, AI researchers monitor the model’s chain of thought, where the AI writes out what it’s thinking as it works through problems and performs tasks. But in Brockman’s view, that may not always be the best way to monitor more advanced AI that’s being built now.

“As the models have gotten more capable, they can do more within just the forward path without having to actually write out that thinking, which means we need a new approach to monitoring,” Brockman said. “We’re going to need to deliver that, and I think that there will be points where we need to really think about, as the capabilities get to a certain point, chain-of-thought monitorability is degraded,” he continued, adding that all the problems were technical ones to be solved.

OpenAI had committed to increased chain-of-thought monitoring after the incident where an OpenAI model escaped containment and hacked into the AI model repository Hugging Face. In Wednesday’s interview, Brockman noted that the company told 25% of its production engineers to stop building things and work on securing the AI technology after the hack.

But despite the high-profile breach, he argued that forward progress was still critical to fight bad actors.

“I think to realize that if you look to the level of capabilities of models in the world, and you see cyber models that are coming from that are now … in the hands of threat actors everywhere, that I think that it’s really important that defenders use the most capable models to defend,” he said.

#509. More banks say customers using AI agents are on their own — even if they get hacked

Citibank, the third largest commercial bank in the United States, changed its terms of service this week, telling customers who use third-party AI tools for personal finance that anything that goes wrong is their problem — even if they get hacked.

AI agents, ranging from frontier lab models to purpose-built personal finance tools, can be given the credentials to your bank account and other online financial portals, with the idea that they can analyze spending and investment decisions, offer advice, and even handle transactions and bill payments.

But financial institutions aren’t thrilled with the unreliable AI actors in their systems, and are telling customers that they won’t be held responsible for reversing an intentional or unintentional transaction made by automated assistants.

“As third-party AI tools become more widely available, we want customers to understand the risks associated with sharing banking credentials or authorizing those tools to access their accounts,” a Citi spokesperson told TechCrunch. “We recently updated our Online User Agreement to provide greater clarity about those risks and to reinforce that customers who choose to grant account access to third-party AI agents are responsible for activities conducted through those agents.”

JPMorgan and Bank of America, the two largest banks in the country, announced similar policies last month. And PNC, the eighth largest bank, changed its terms today.

Citi’s update goes beyond telling customers that they are responsible for anything their agents may do; it also informs them that if the AI tool leaks their credentials or is hacked, the customer will be responsible for anything hackers do with their account. Banking giant Citizens similarly emailed its customers this week, warning that they are also on the hook for anything their AI assistant does, according to an email seen by TechCrunch.

As AI startups look for more revenue, breaking into personal finance presents an attractive opportunity for companies to add value for customers and wet their beaks in the stream of fees generated by the sector. But if consumers are already leery of sharing their email or chat records with an AI agent, bank and brokerage accounts offer a new trust challenge.

Anthropic’s approach is telling: The personal finance tool it launched earlier this month is explicitly billed as “read-only” and “can’t move, manage, or invest your money.”

Beyond cybersecurity concerns, banks also view efforts to enter their franchise as a competitive threat. Markets shuddered briefly in reaction to the prospect of AI tools making it easier for consumers to avoid unnecessary fees or low-interest deposit accounts that pad bank margins. But if AI agents become an important way to interact with financial decisions, banks will fight to control that pathway. Citi’s terms note that AI agents it provides can be used by customers without worrying about additional liability.

The reaction by banks is part of a broader trend among businesses of all stripes: AI labs promise that their agents will revolutionize the way people engage with a variety of service providers, then the service providers say “hold on a minute.” We’ve seen it in everything from car-hailing to food service, and how these relationships play out will help determine which AI investments pay off.

#494. Sam Altman says his life is so weird that he’s unfazed by Andrew Garfield portraying him in upcoming movie

Hollywood has found plenty of material and cinematic inspiration from a string of Silicon Valley scandals and power struggles in recent years.

“You Can See Everything,” the documentary about Elizabeth Holmes, smashed box office expectations, and Netflix’s limited series “The Altruists” comes out Thursday to tell the story of Sam Bankman-Fried and the downfall of FTX.

As the founder and CEO of OpenAI, one of the most influential companies of the century, Sam Altman isn’t going to escape the Hollywood treatment, either.

In “Artificial,” a biographical comedy-drama opening in theaters on Christmas, the lauded actor Andrew Garfield will portray Altman in a film that chronicles OpenAI’s rise and the internal turmoil that erupted when he was briefly ousted in 2023.

“If you had told me 20 years ago that someone was going to make a movie about me, I think that would have struck me as … that would have been a very weird thing,” Altman said onstage at the New York Times DealBook Summit on Wednesday. “Now it doesn’t feel like it’s in like the top 10 weirdest things about my life.”

Garfield is best known for his role as Spider-Man, but his big break came in 2010, when he played Facebook co-founder Eduardo Saverin in “The Social Network” alongside Jesse Eisenberg as Mark Zuckerberg. (That film also had a sequel of sorts this year called “The Social Reckoning,” but it bombed at the box office.)

So, if Altman isn’t fazed by the looming release of “Artificial,” what’s actually the weirdest thing in his life?

“The fact that we are ushering in this technology that we have all of these very real concerns about, and that we have, we find ourselves in a role of such significance and such responsibility to make that go well,” Altman said. “That’s a crazy situation to find yourself in.”

Sure, that is pretty weird, Sam. It’s also weird that you use ChatGPT for parenting advice and don’t respect olive oil. But I’d wager to say it’s even weirder that there’s an upcoming film about the time your colleagues briefly ousted you because they were concerned that you can’t be trusted.

Say what you want about Mark Zuckerberg (I certainly have), but at least he’s honest about what he thinks about “The Social Network.”

“It was weird, man,” Zuckerberg reflected on a podcast last year. “They got all these very specific details of what I was wearing, or these specific things correct, but then the whole narrative arc around my motivations and all this stuff were like, completely wrong … The whole arc is like, I’m somehow motivated by trying to find a girlfriend, [but] I was dating Priscilla [Chan] before I started Facebook.”

Zuckerberg said he knew his employees would be tempted to see the movie, so he organized a company outing when it was released, even though the film hardly paints a flattering portrait of him.

Zuckerberg even had enough of a sense of humor to buy a prop from the film at auction. He owns an Ardsley High School T-shirt that Eisenberg wore in the movie and even wore it himself in a video with YouTubers Colin and Samir.

Eisenberg hasn’t been particularly complimentary of Zuckerberg, saying that he doesn’t want to think of himself as “associated with somebody like that.” And while “Artificial” hasn’t hit theaters yet, Garfield doesn’t seem to have a much more favorable opinion of the tech founder he portrays.

“I don’t know Sam Altman. I’ve never met him,” Garfield told Esquire. “But I think anyone who takes on that level of responsibility for a company or technology that you know is going to do a hell of a lot of damage to the soul of humanity [must have] a certain ability to dissociate.”

#483. Palmer Luckey warns AI-powered cyberattacks are outpacing defenses: ‘It’s time to start running’

AI-powered cyberattacks are moving faster than outdated technology systems can defend against them, Palmer Luckey warned at The New York Times DealBook Summit on Wednesday. Addressing the recent spate of tech outages, the Oculus founder and Anduril Industries co-founder argued that older systems are struggling to keep pace with the new hacking capabilities enabled by AI and warned it was time to quicken the pace of securing our defenses.

“We’re seeing the natural effects of a lot of these advanced AI tools allowing for the creation of various exploits,” he said.

While government officials largely attributed the latest set of technical issues to companies rushing to patch their systems to protect against the new vulnerabilities AI has discovered, Luckey focused on the underlying security challenges facing organizations using outdated technology.

Luckey explained that U.S. adversaries are currently exploiting the mismatch between new attack capabilities and our defense capabilities. These attacks are being carried out for both strategic and financial reasons, and North Korea was particularly involved, he added.

The interviewer, Andrew Ross Sorkin, noted that U.S. Vice President J.D. Vance had canceled his appearance at the Times’ conference this week because of the recent tech outages, saying he now had to attend meetings about the matter.

Given the situation, Sorkin asked Luckey how worried we should be about all this.

“It depends on how well prepared you are,” was Luckey’s answer. (He then ominously warned, “You guys are going to find out what’s going on soon enough.”)

The tech executive argued that AI could ultimately give defenders the upper hand, but they’d have to move more quickly.

“In the long run, the advantages are going to accrue to the side that is most effectively using AI for defense,” Luckey said. “With the people who say that everything can be hacked — they are right, for now. That actually may be the case with certain new techniques that are coming along. But as of right now, there’s a mismatch between the attackers who are very fast-moving and using technology and old, outdated systems that are not able to adopt technology as quickly as it is developed,” Luckey continued.

“And I think … people are going to have to pick up the pace,” he said. “It’s time to start running.”

#479. Nvidia created a weird bit of intrigue by not filing a routine SEC report

During Nvidia’s quarterly call on Tuesday, CFO-turned-interim-CEO Colette Kress dropped this little oddity: She said Nvidia wouldn’t be immediately filing its quarterly results report, known as a 10-Q, with the SEC. “We plan to file our 10-Q in the coming weeks.” And she offered no reason why.

The delay was widely noticed. Among the many comments on X about it, economic and investment analyst Edward Dowd called it a “red flag.” Nvidia has typically filed the 10-Q the same day it reports results.

The 10-Q offers far more detail about a company’s operations than an earnings press release, including sections that disclose “legal proceedings,” which include lawsuits that could affect the company; and “risk factors,” which cover other potentially business-impairing items.

The announcement of the delay was so unusual that UBS analyst Timothy Arcuri asked about it after the public earnings call. Nvidia told him the delay was related to whether the company would need to disclose additional information about its ongoing investigation into Huang in the 10-Q.

“The company announced that the 10-Q will be filed ‘in the coming weeks’ – later than usual. When queried on our callback, management indicated this was related to the ongoing investigation and whether any additional risk factors would need to be added to the filing,” Arcuri wrote in his research note. (Arcuri still rates the stock a “buy” by the way, although he did lower his price target to $220 from $240. The stock is now trading around $150).

Notably, on the same day that Nvidia reported earnings, The Wall Street Journal reported that the SEC is investigating Nvidia over whether the company’s disclosures misled investors.

So the mystery thickens: What will Nvidia disclose? We’ll have to wait and see. (Nvidia has not yet responded to our request for comment.)

The delay suggests the company may have more to say about its mounting legal troubles. Nvidia is now facing multiple federal investigations over allegations that its partners were selling restricted Nvidia AI chips to China. So far, the scrutiny has come from a number of congressional committees; the Department of Justice looking into Megaspeed, the company run by a woman with whom Huang had an affair and who is also suspected of being a Chinese spy; and now, reportedly, the SEC.

Additionally, Nvidia is conducting its own internal investigation into Huang after he admitted to having an affair with Alice Huang (the Megaspeed executive), and allegations surfaced that he also had affairs with other Nvidia employees.

There’s little doubt that Wall Street will scrutinize that 10-Q once it’s finally filed. The intrigue brings to mind the old saying: “Doctors bury their mistakes. Lawyers put theirs in prison. And accountants hide theirs in the footnotes.”
