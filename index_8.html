Importantly, volume is ratcheting higher in the aftermath of each event.

#634. Robert Pattinson cast as next James Bond

Robert Pattinson has been cast as the next James Bond in the upcoming film from director Denis Villeneuve. It will be the 26th James Bond film and the first since Amazon MGM took over the iconic franchise.

Pattinson will be the seventh actor to play the role. Monday's announcement — which came in part via an Instagram post — ends a long period of speculation about who would take over as "007" after Daniel Craig.

"James Bond is one of the most legendary characters in cinema, and to be entrusted with 007 is the honour of a lifetime," Pattinson said in a statement. "I couldn't be more excited about this partnership and the work ahead with this fantastic team."

Pattinson has appeared in several big-ticket films this year, including Christopher Nolan's "The Odyssey" and psychological thriller "Primetime," and he'll soon appear in "Dune: Part Three," also directed by Villeneuve. Pattinson, who rose to fame with the Twilight movie series, is also in production for Matt Reeves' "The Batman Part II," a follow-up to the 2022 film based on the DC comic.

The upcoming Bond film will have a script by Steven Knight and will be produced by Amy Pascal, known for her work on the Spider-Man franchise, and David Heyman, who produced all eight Harry Potter films.

"We were surrounded by extraordinary talent, but there can only be one 007," Villeneuve said. "I am overjoyed to welcome Robert Pattinson as our new James Bond. He has the timeless soul of 007 and brings a spirit that is completely new. I cannot wait to begin."

In 2022, Amazon bought MGM Studios, which had long produced the Bond movies, for roughly $8.45 billion. Amazon MGM then acquired creative control of the James Bond intellectual property rights in 2025, when it closed a deal with longtime producers Barbara Broccoli and Michael G. Wilson.

The film is still untitled, and Amazon MGM hasn't announced a projected release date.

#614. JPMorgan Chase says temporary software bug is affecting consumer banking

JPMorgan Chase reported a "technical issue" that was affecting some consumer banking services Monday.

The bank said a "temporary issue related to a software bug in on-premise hardware systems" was causing some customer transactions to take longer than usual, though it said customers can still access their accounts and funds.

"There is no evidence it is cyber-related," a JPMorgan spokesperson said in a statement to CNBC.

"This is not a systemic issue — other banks may have been impacted by slowness in transactions only to the extent that they were a party to a transaction with us," the spokesperson said. "We're working diligently to resolve this by the end of the day today."

CNBC's Eamon Javers reported there was no indication of bad actors involved.

Still, other major banks were listed in outage reports as transactions between Chase and other institutions appeared to get held up. Users reported errors as early as 11:30 a.m. ET.

A representative for U.S. Bank said that company was "not directly impacted. However, clients attempting to send or receive Zelle payments with another financial institution may experience transaction delays."

Zelle is the peer-to-peer payments system backed by many of the largest U.S. banks, including JPMorgan.

The outage reports come days after separate, widespread issues with Microsoft Outlook and communication systems used by air traffic control centers.

— CNBC's Leslie Picker contributed to this report.

#594. United Airlines resolves system outage that affected departing flights

United Airlines on Monday said it had resolved a "widespread system outage" that briefly affected departing flights.

"We have resolved a technology issue that delayed flight departures," the airline said in a statement. "While some flights were delayed, there was no impact on flights in progress, or on United Express flights.

A United spokesperson said there was no indication that the outage was a cyberattack.

The Air Line Pilots Association, which represents pilots for United and other carriers, said in an earlier message to members seen by CNBC that the operational outage was "affecting multiple pilot facing applications" and that United could not "provide outbound flights with final weights."

United's outage came three days after a nationwide ground stop halted departures across the country for more than four hours, snarling travel and leading to widespread delays and cancellations over the weekend.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy blamed the issues that led to Friday's ground stop on a router in Arkansas that overwhelmed systems that feed radar and other data for air traffic control. Federal Aviation Administration Administrator Bryan Bedford said the upgrades were part of "routine" maintenance to switch over from copper wires to fiber optic.

There were significantly fewer flight delays and cancellations Monday than had been reported Sunday, according to FlightAware data.

There was no indication that United's outage Monday was related to the issues that caused Friday's ground stop.

— CNBC's Leslie Josephs and Phil LeBeau contributed to this report.

The 2026 midterm cycle was largely powered by megadonors, with SpaceX and Tesla CEO Elon Musk becoming the largest contributor by funneling more than $366 million into campaigns.

The biggest donors in this year's election disproportionately favored Republicans, according to a CNBC analysis of Federal Election Commission records. Fourteen out of the 20 largest donor groups supported GOP candidates, according to that analysis.

Republicans spent $5.51 billion on political advertisements, compared with $5.11 billion spent by Democrats, according to AdImpact.

While Republican spending increased by 27% from 2024, spending by Democrats fell by 4%.

Fundraisers told CNBC in interviews that Democratic donors' hesitancy largely stems from memories of Kamala Harris' defeat in 2024, unease about the rise of democratic socialism and distrust in party leadership.

Though they were outspent, Democrats made sweeping gains against Republicans in races nationwide, taking control of both the House and the Senate and flipping seats in some of the most competitive places, including Texas and Kansas.

It is not the first time spending did not align with the winners. In 2024, President Donald Trump defeated former Vice President Harris despite Democrats outspending Republicans.

While party majorities in Congress have switched hands, the most popular medium for political ads has not changed.

Broadcast television represented the largest category for spending in this year's election, accounting for 49%, or $5.85 billion, of political ad spending, according to AdImpact. Broadcast's share of political ad dollars is up slightly from 48% in 2024.

Broadcast TV's strength comes despite facing pressure from falling viewership and increasing competition from streaming.

Industry experts have told CNBC that political advertisers continue to spend on broadcast because it reaches local markets and active voting populations. The Associated Press also reported that airtime was so expensive during the midterm elections that some candidates, including Republican Ken Paxton in Texas, bought ads during national sporting events, figuring that reaching enough constituents through a national broadcast might actually be less expensive than buying across the state's local television markets.

The fastest-growing categories in this year's cycle were connected TV, or internet-connected devices like Apple TV or Roku, with $2.66 billion, and digital, with $1.91 billion. Both mediums grew double digits from the 2024 presidential election. Spending on cable and radio declined 7% and 13%, respectively, from 2024.
