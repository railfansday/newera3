#653. #515: Are Prediction Markets Going Mainstream Again?, & More

Is the boom in prediction markets structural or the result of momentary catalysts? Hosted across the US, Canada, and Mexico, the 2026 FIFA World Cup is the latest test. The notional volume of prediction markets hit an all-time high of $13 billion, or ~$675 billion at an annual rate, during the first full week of group play ended June 15. Year-to-date, the broader market is on pace for notional volume of ~$330 billion at an annual rate, more than quadrupling the $71 billion in 2025. In the early days of prediction markets, the 2024 US national election caused the initial spike. Now, the World Cup is the prime mover. Importantly, volume is ratcheting higher in the aftermath of each event.

#501. Big-ticket holiday items may be harder to find as shipping costs rise

This holiday season, big-ticket items are expected to be in short supply with diesel prices near records, trucking rates close to a post-pandemic level and carrying costs hitting multidecade highs. All of that is forcing many retailers to rethink their holiday inventory strategy.

"We are going to have some of the lowest inventory levels on some of these big-ticket items we have seen in history," said Deborah Weinswig, CEO of retail analytics firm Coresight Research.

Weinswig said retailers she is working with are holding less inventory of big-screen TVs, grills, large consumer appliances like washers and dryers, and other items that take either big amounts of space or cost a lot of money to hold.

"It's freight getting it from there to here; it's carrying costs; then it's just the trucking to get it into the stores," Weinswig said. "We're talking three different pieces right here and each is up significantly year on year."

The surge pricing for supply chain services is being caused by domestic and international policy.

According to Evercore ISI, the rates for general trucking have increased more than 47% year over year during the week of Nov. 2.

Industry experts say a large factor has been a crackdown by the federal government on non-citizen truck drivers and non-U.S.-based truck drivers operating in the U.S. That has created a capacity crunch that has raised rates and is expected to continue to create upward pressure.

At the same time, fuel surcharges are spiking.

Most transportation providers charge customers an extra fuel surcharge to offset changes in the cost of diesel fuel. That surcharge has spiked 64% year over year, according to Evercore ISI data, as diesel prices have hit new record highs due in part to the U.S.-Israel war with Iran.

And inventory holding costs — or the total expense of storing unsold goods, which could include the costs for capital, service, storage and other risk factors — are closely tied to the yield on the 10-year Treasury note, which exceeded 5% in October and remains at multiyear highs in November.

Collectively, the higher costs could mean bigger discounts for shoppers willing to wait, according to Bill Catania, CEO of logistics technology company OneRail, which powers same-day delivery for companies such as FedEx and Lowe's.

"You're going to see more discount and price promotions as we get closer to the holiday to liquidate inventory, so they're not having to transfer it or dispose of it or position it some other way," Catania said.

Retailers are also facing a conundrum in their holiday inventory strategy when it comes to shipping costs versus opportunity costs. In other words, is it worth it to stock up now, or wait it out in hopes that shipping rates will come down some but potentially miss out on having the right product mix?

It's leading to a wait-and-see approach for stocking shelves after early holiday sales in October, executives said.

UPS CEO Carol Tomé said on the company's earnings call on Oct. 27 that it's "just starting to get peak season forecasts from our customers."

Similarly, Derek Leathers, CEO of Werner Enterprises, a trucker for big-box stores and major retailers, said retailers are cautious.

"When rates are higher and the cost of capital is more expensive, you are going to have a much more intense focus on not having excess inventory," Leathers said.

Still, 2026 holiday sales are expected to top $1 trillion as inflation drives growth and customer spending is expected to remain healthy, according to Bain & Company's annual forecast. Leathers said even with the surge in trucking and shipping rates, he's seeing that retailers are willing to pay up to stock up.

"The bigger concern right now is wanting to make sure they have their capacity locked in. There is way more dialogue around the assurance of having a truck or whatever mode that needs to be secured," Leathers said. "Not saying they are price insensitive, because they are certainly always concerned about price. But you can't sell it if it's not on the shelf."

ShipMatrix, a data analytics firm for the logistics industry, forecasts 2.4 billion packages will be delivered during the 2026 holiday peak, a 4% increase year over year.

It's an increase that will likely lead retailers to increasingly use gig delivery, especially later in the holiday peak. Gig workers allow retailers and delivery companies to adjust to changes in order flow and accommodate surges of last-minute shopping without spending time and money onboarding new team members.

"Gig economy delivery is great. You know, it fulfills fast, but there's better unit economics out there," Catania said.

Industry experts said they also expect to see more retailers use drones for last-mile delivery for smaller items.

A 2024 PwC study forecast that the cost of drone delivery would "drop by more than 70% over the next 10 years, which will not only match traditional last-mile delivery costs, but surpass them and create an inflection point for the industry."

Amit Regev, CEO of drone company Flytrex, said the company is in active talks with retailers about using drones for holiday deliveries.

"The expectation now is becoming so much more instant," Regev said.

Retailers have been increasing their use of drones. Lowe's in September said it was launching a drone delivery service in partnership with DoorDash and Alphabet's Wing. Amazon earlier this year said it was aiming to offer drone deliveries in nearly 500 U.S. cities and towns by the end of the year.

Flytrex currently delivers for Walmart and a number of restaurants including Little Caesars and Papa John's.

"Customers are looking for that instant gratification," Regev said. "So if it takes too long, they might not want to order with you. And from what we are seeing that's a stronger incentive for them to try and test or deploy deliveries using drones."

#485. Figma employee sues, alleging company forced her to confront harassers

A Figma engineer is suing the design software company and its co-founder and CEO, Dylan Field, over allegations that she faced sexual harassment and retaliation while managers and HR repeatedly told her to confront the very people she accused of misconduct.

The lawsuit, filed Monday in U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York, alleges that Figma’s culture of “direct feedback” led managers and HR to make engineer Serry Park address harassment herself rather than investigate her complaints. Park, who joined Figma in 2022, alleges the company’s response damaged her career, compensation, and mental health. The lawsuit also alleges that Park, who is Asian, faced racial and gender discrimination, including performance criticism based on stereotypes that Asian women are meek, timid, and conflict-averse.

“Figma was my dream company for a long time,” Park told TechCrunch in an interview before the lawsuit was filed. “It was a place where I felt that I could level up in my career, working on a lot of really cool, interesting things at scale.”

Park is seeking damages, a jury trial, and a court order prohibiting Figma from requiring employees who report harassment to confront their alleged harassers. She also wants the company to adopt and enforce a process for investigating harassment complaints promptly and confidentially.

When reached for comment, a spokesperson for Figma said, “Figma does not tolerate discrimination or harassment. Figma also does not tolerate extortion. These claims have no merit, and we will present the facts in court.”

Park told TechCrunch she initially had faith in Figma’s complaint process, expecting that if she documented the alleged harassment and reported it to the right people, the company would act. But as an engineer in a male-dominated field, Park told TechCrunch, “I don’t feel that they took me seriously throughout this process.”

Park, who is still employed at Figma, joined its San Francisco office in October 2022. Within weeks, her onboarding mentor — a fellow engineer — asked her on a date and continued pursuing her after she declined, Park alleges. She initially feared reporting him, worrying that her manager would “dismiss her reports or view her as the problem,” according to the lawsuit.

Park says she tried to avoid her onboarding mentor. In early 2023, for example, she delayed choosing a work project until he had picked one. When her manager criticized her for not being a “team player,” Park explained that she was trying to avoid the colleague she accused of harassment, according to the suit.

Her manager allegedly dismissed her concerns, saying he had “met his wife at work too” and treating the behavior as “harmless flirtation.” When Park asked to be kept off projects with the onboarding mentor, HR allegedly instructed her to confront him directly about his behavior.

The lawsuit ties that response to Figma’s culture of direct feedback, which Park alleges Field established and enforced. It says the company’s People organization trains managers to direct employees, including those reporting harassment, to give feedback directly to the person involved.

In March 2023, her manager assigned him to a major project she was leading, despite her request to be separated from him. Park told her manager that she would “figure it out,” but alleges that having to work with him affected her performance. Her manager gave her a 2 out of 5 on her performance review, criticizing her for not speaking up enough or engaging with others and for lacking presence. Park was put on a coaching plan, which she completed in late 2023.

Changing managers didn’t help, according to the lawsuit. Her second manager also refused to separate them, allegedly telling Park that someone at her level should be able to resolve conflicts with teammates and that separating them would be “avoiding the problem.” Like her previous manager, he allegedly pointed out that he had met his partner at work.

Park alleges the harassment continued throughout 2023.

In April 2024, she transferred to another team, a move she claims set back her career by resetting her performance and promotion timeline.

On her new team, which had more women, Park said she felt better and no longer had to work with her alleged harasser. Her performance reviews improved, but in late 2025, she alleges another incident of sexual harassment, this time by her new manager.

The incident followed a night out for drinks with her wife and two teammates, including her manager. The next day, during a team lunch, the manager allegedly said Park’s sex life had been discussed the previous evening. Park, who said no such conversation had occurred in her or her wife’s presence, felt “horrified and humiliated” by comments she believed fetishized queer relationships.

A colleague reported the comments to HR as sexual harassment, according to the suit. Weeks later, Park’s manager apologized in a Slack message, saying she didn’t recall what she had said. When Park sought HR’s guidance, she was again told to address the matter directly with her manager.

During their conversation, the manager allegedly became defensive, and the encounter left Park feeling “crushed, anxious, and vulnerable.” The manager also allegedly said she would never make such a comment because she had “a lot of gay friends.”

The manager told HR that they had agreed it was a misunderstanding. HR closed its investigation without interviewing witnesses, according to the suit, even though Park disputed her manager’s account.

“Requiring an employee to confront her harasser as the price of complaining — and then closing her complaint — would dissuade a reasonable employee from complaining at all,” the suit said.

Park alleges her manager retaliated by publicly berating her, sometimes so aggressively that colleagues intervened or checked on her afterward, according to the lawsuit. The suit also alleges that other teammates left the team over concerns about the manager’s behavior.

Park again complained to HR about “escalating retaliation” and expressed concern the manager would “derail her career.” HR allegedly defended the manager’s conduct, saying the manager was “within her reasonable authority” and again instructed Park to address her concerns directly with the manager.

In early 2026, Park received another low performance review and a reduced bonus. The lawsuit alleges that similarly situated men continued to receive promotions, raises, and bonuses. When Park reported the negative performance review to HR in March, she was allegedly told her sexual harassment complaint against her manager could not be “substantiated.”

Park took medical leave in March to receive treatment for PTSD symptoms and generalized anxiety. Cleared to return to work in June, she was prescribed workplace accommodations, including a graduated work schedule and mandatory remote workdays.

Park told TechCrunch that Figma approved the accommodations but had not implemented them at the time she returned to work. In July, Park and her lawyers notified Figma of their intent to pursue legal action.

“It’s really scary for me,” she said about taking legal action. “I don’t know what’s going to happen next with this, I don’t know what the outcome is going to be, but at least it feels like this is my chance to do the right thing.”
