#653. Eli Lilly hikes sales outlook, as CEO says there's room for 'dramatic' obesity market expansion

Eli Lilly on Thursday reported third-quarter earnings and revenue that blew past estimates and hiked its full-year sales outlook, as demand for its blockbuster weight loss drug Zepbound and diabetes treatment Mounjaro soared again.

Despite that strength, sales of its new GLP-1 obesity pill Foundayo, which launched in the U.S. in April, were under Wall Street's expectations for the quarter.

The pharmaceutical giant now expects 2026 revenue to come in between $91 billion and $92 billion, up from a previous guidance of $85 billion to $87 billion.

Lilly projects its full-year adjusted earnings will be between $35 and $36 per share, which compares with a previous outlook of $35.50 to $36.50 per share. The company said it raised the underlying profit guidance by $4.07 per share at the midpoint, but noted that it is offset by $4.57 per share in charges tied to deals in the quarter.

The company's shares rose about 7% on Thursday.

Lilly's massive diabetes and obesity windfall – and market share lead over chief rival Novo – shows no signs of slowing down, even as the company grapples with lower net prices in the U.S. The pharma giant is converting its massive profits into a deal-making spree, most recently striking an agreement to buy Merida Biosciences to develop a new class of treatments for serious autoimmune and allergic diseases.

Here's what Eli Lilly reported for the third quarter compared with what Wall Street was expecting, based on a survey of analysts by LSEG:

Resilient demand for Zepbound and Mounjaro has helped fuel several strong quarters for Lilly despite lower prices for the medications in the U.S., which came after a drug pricing deal struck with the Trump administration last year. In a release, Lilly also pointed to expanded U.S. coverage for obesity care, referring to Medicare's decision to start covering GLP-1s for weight loss in July under a temporary pilot program.

"This is a program that's really working," Lilly CEO Dave Ricks said on CNBC's "Squawk on the Street." "We're pleased with the early uptake and access, and I think doctors and patients are as well."

Mounjaro's worldwide revenue rose 65% to $10.72 billion for the quarter, including U.S. sales of about $5 billion. That surpassed the $10.10 billion in worldwide sales and $4.79 billion in U.S. revenue that analysts were expecting for the quarter, according to StreetAccount.

Mounjaro notably saw strength internationally, with sales outside the U.S. jumping 93%.

Zepbound, which entered the market roughly three years ago, posted $5.35 billion in revenue for the third quarter. That's up 49% from the year-earlier period, as demand for the drug also rose while realized prices dropped. The vast majority of those sales came from the U.S.

Analysts were expecting $5.09 billion in global sales for Zepbound, including $4.99 billion from the U.S., according to StreetAccount.

Lilly's newly launched obesity pill, Foundayo, which won U.S. approval in April, took in $184 million in sales for the third quarter. That fell under analyst forecasts of around $229 million in sales, StreetAccount estimates showed.

It marks the second earnings report that includes revenue from the GLP-1 pill, which is competing head-to-head with a rival oral drug from Novo that launched in January.

Total U.S. prescriptions for Foundayo and the number of new Foundayo patients in the third quarter doubled from the previous quarter, Lilly CFO Lucas Montarces said during a Thursday earnings call. Around 4 in 10 patients starting an oral obesity medicine choose Foundayo, he added.

Lilly expects Foundayo to win U.S. approval for Type 2 diabetes in the fourth quarter.

Lilly is working to maintain its dominance in the booming market for GLP-1 drugs, with the company holding 63% share of the U.S. obesity and diabetes drug market in the third quarter, according to an earnings presentation on Thursday. Novo's market share in the quarter was 36.6%.

During the earnings call, Montarces said 7 out of 10 U.S. prescriptions for injectable obesity and diabetes drugs in the third quarter were for a Lilly medicine.

Ricks told CNBC that he estimates 3% to 10% of eligible patients access obesity care in the U.S., but said the "room for expansion here of this market is dramatic." Lilly is at the front of that push, he added.

Internationally, Lilly held 58.4% share of the market, while Novo had 41.6%, the presentation said.

Eli Lilly's revenue in the U.S. climbed 34% to $15.1 billion. Lilly said it saw a 41% increase in volume — or the number of prescriptions or units sold — for its products, primarily for Mounjaro and Zepbound. That was partially offset by a 7% decrease in realized prices, primarily driven by Zepbound.

Lilly's quarter was also boosted by a sales-based milestone payout of $410 million for its Type 2 diabetes medication Jardiance, related to the company's collaboration with Boehringer Ingelheim.

Sales outside the U.S. jumped 47% to $9.3 billion, propelled by a 76% surge in volume and partly offset by a 29% drop in realized prices.

The pharmaceutical giant booked net income of $6.45 billion, or $7.22 per share, for the third quarter, which includes the $4.57 per share deal charges. That compares with net income of $5.58 billion, or $6.21 per share, a year earlier.

Excluding one-time items associated with the value of intangible assets and other adjustments, Eli Lilly posted earnings of $7.48 per share for the third quarter.

#619. Cruise companies aim to keep fuel costs down. Their strategies for doing so differ

Carnival, Royal Caribbean and Norwegian Cruise Line Holdings posted strong earnings for their most recent quarters despite rising fuel prices. How the leading cruise companies navigate fuel costs boils down to starkly different strategies.

Oil prices have skyrocketed since the U.S.-Israel war with Iran began on Feb. 28. Brent crude oil reached a six-month high on Friday, settling at $116.72 per barrel. With no clear end in sight for the conflict in the Middle East, the pressure affecting cruise ships' lifeblood seems poised to last into the holiday travel season and beyond.

Both Royal Caribbean and Norwegian practice "hedging," or a strategy of using derivatives to reduce exposure to swings in the price of fuel, to account for sharp increases.

Carnival CEO Josh Weinstein, on the other hand, told CNBC in an interview last month that cutting fuel consumption is the best way to avert the effects of expensive fuel.

"At the end of the day, fuel is an input cost for us. The best thing that we can do to control that cost is to use less," Weinstein said at the time.

Jefferies analyst David Katz told CNBC in an interview that companies that hedge do so primarily to manage the cost of fuel, but also to manage Wall Street expectations and prevent hectic stock trading based on fluctuations in the price of fuel.

"When you're public, the Street will trade your stock around based on the price of fuel," Katz said. "The Street's argument is that volatility puts pressure on your multiple."

"While it is mathematically most likely true that over the long term it is more expensive," he explained. "The benefit of having less fuel volatility in the model and less trading volatility is [it's] good for your multiple."

Despite the potential positives of the strategy, Carnival has maintained the stance it has held since 2019: Cutting fuel consumption works better than hedging.

And Carnival's third-quarter earnings report last month suggests the strategy may be paying off.

Carnival beat analysts' expectations on both the top and bottom lines for its most recent quarter and reported an all-time high net income of $1.9 billion.

Weinstein told CNBC in an interview on "Squawk on the Street" following the release that the company has been "maniacal" about cutting its fuel consumption, saying it's slashed its unit fuel consumption rate by 26% since 2019.

"We estimate we've saved about $700 million in 2026 based on that consumption savings rate going down since 2019," he said.

Carnival spent $615 million, or about 7.3% of its revenue, on fuel in the third quarter, according to its most recent earnings release. That's an increase from the $425 million, or 6.7% of revenue, that the company spent on fuel in the fourth quarter of 2025, before fuel prices spiked.

Carnival projects its fuel costs will rise to $640 million in the fourth quarter, according to its earnings release.

Royal Caribbean similarly beat analyst expectations for earnings per share for its third quarter, reported Tuesday. However, it narrowly missed on revenue.

CEO Jason Liberty attributed the company's beat in part to its hedging strategy during an interview on CNBC's "Squawk on the Street" Tuesday morning.

"We're a little bit over 60% hedged here for 2026, and so that's helping insulate us from some of the impacts on the rising fuel costs," he said.

"It's obviously still impacting us," Liberty said. "[High fuel prices] evaporated a good chunk of our Q3 earnings beat into the full-year guide. But we're continuing to hedge, and we watch."

Royal Caribbean spent about $347 million on fuel in the third quarter, or about 6.2% of its revenue, according to its earnings release. That compares with $293 million, or 6.8% of revenue, spent on fuel in the fourth quarter of 2025, which ended on Dec. 31.

The company projects fuel costs will continue to rise to $379 million in the fourth quarter.

Liberty pointed out that high fuel prices have not dampened demand from consumers for vacations.

"It's not that we don't see points of challenges, but really what we've seen is that even in those points of challenges, the consumer comes roaring back," he said.

Royal Caribbean reported strong bookings for the third quarter and said reservations for 2027 are already up by a high-teens percentage.

Norwegian Cruise Line Holdings beat on both the top and bottom lines for its third-quarter earnings, reported on Thursday, with revenue of $2.9 billion and adjusted earnings per share of $1.12.

The company has hedged approximately 48% of its fuel consumption for 2026, according to the earnings release.

Norwegian spent about $196 million on fuel in the third quarter, or about 6.6% of its revenue, according to its most recent earnings release. That's a lower share of revenue than the 7.4% — totaling $167 million — spent on fuel in the fourth quarter of 2025, which ended on Dec. 31.

The company projects fuel consumption to rise slightly in the fourth quarter, up to 255,000 metric tons from 239,000 metric tons consumed in the third quarter.

The company is in the midst of a turnaround effort. And despite facing added challenges from the macro environment, Chief Financial Officer Mark Kempa said he is reassured by this quarter's results on an earnings call on Thursday.

"These results reinforce our confidence in the appeal of our products and the opportunity to translate stronger revenue performance and disciplined cost management into improved profitability," Kempa said.

#545. Cheaper cars are selling considerably faster than at the beginning of the year

Consumers are concerned about rising fuel prices and affordability — and that could be showing up in how fast the least expensive cars are moving off dealer lots.

From the first quarter to the third, the number of days it took a dealer to sell a vehicle priced between $20,000 and $29,999 dropped 20%, according to data from Edmunds. That pace of selling saw the biggest acceleration of any price segment.

Vehicles priced from $30,000 to $39,999 sold about 18% faster, and the number of days it took to sell the next tier — $40,000 to $49,999 — dropped nearly 12%, according to Edmunds.

All of those price segments also took fewer days to turn than their more expensive counterparts. The lower-priced tiers took under 60 days to move off lots, with the least expensive ones selling the fastest. Every segment of vehicles above $50,000, meanwhile, took more than 60 days to sell.

"If you're a dealer and you have inventory and that price point, you're definitely noticing," said Edmunds Director of Insights Ivan Drury.

Cheaper vehicles may be selling faster, but they're still hard to find. The average listing price for a new vehicle in September was $49,726, according to Cox Automotive.

And, the overall sales numbers for some key segments priced under $40,000 have fallen. This has especially been true for compact cars, which are down roughly 4% since the beginning of 2026, and compact SUVs, down nearly 8% in the same period, according to Cox Automotive.

These different data points seem to be moving in opposite directions. On the one hand, cheaper cars are moving faster off dealer lots. On the other, sales of affordable segments are down.

There are a few reasons for this, according to analysts who follow the industry. One is that there might not be enough lower-priced vehicles on dealer lots in the first place.

"I think there are some supply things going on at that end of the market," said Cox Automotive Deputy Chief Economist Mark Strand. "We have to kind of grade the market on a curve until we see what's going on with fourth-quarter data."

The compact SUV segment, in particular, is suffering from the lack of two popular models.

The Toyota RAV4, which is the most popular SUV in the U.S., has been in short supply at many dealerships this year, as the automaker shifts production to its Kentucky factory, Strand said. The company also has shifted the entire lineup to a hybrid powertrain at a time when hybrids have been in exceptionally high demand.

The other missing SUV is the Ford Escape, which that automaker discontinued to make way for its upcoming midsize EV pickup truck.

The other slow-selling, low-priced segment — compact cars — faces structural inventory challenges, said AutoForecast Solutions Vice President of Global Forecasting Sam Fiorani.

"We've seen the disappearance of affordable, compact new vehicles over the last decade," he said.

Detroit automakers swapped out their lineups of compact coupes and sedans in favor of small crossover SUVs, which tend to carry slightly higher sticker prices than comparably sized passenger cars.

Currently, there is not a single compact passenger sedan for sale by an American manufacturer. Most on the market are Japanese or Korean, with a few German models, such as the Volkswagen Jetta and the Golf hatchback.

"You have all these brands that have just abandoned the marketplace," Fiorani said.

The smallest American passenger cars are the Ford Mustang, General Motors' Chevrolet Corvette, and the midsize Tesla Model 3 sedan, according to AutoForecast Solutions. The firm classifies both the Mustang and the Corvette as sports cars, and as such they occupy a different niche than compact sedans like the Honda Civic or Toyota Corolla.

The Mustang has a starting price of $33,490, the Model 3 starts at $34,990 and the Chevrolet Corvette starts at $71,300. Cars like the Civic, Corolla and Jetta, meanwhile, all start below $25,000.

"Compact vehicle buyers are very price-sensitive," Fiorani said. "And so when the price of gas goes up, that eats into their car payment. That means that they can't afford a $500 or $600 a month car payment, and they have to move into the used market."

Indeed, the wholesale used prices of compact cars have risen and are up more than almost any other segment, according to Cox Automotive. Wholesale prices — the prices vehicles sell for at large auctions, like Cox Automotive's Manheim auction — are indicators for prices overall in the fragmented used-car market.

Along with the rise in prices, auto finance stats show that a historically high number of buyers are stretching to afford cars.

Roughly one in four buyers financing their purchases are getting 84-month loans — a record. Nearly 3 in 10 trade-ins toward new-vehicle purchases carried negative equity in the third quarter, meaning the cars being traded in were worth less than the outstanding balances of the loans on them. Shoppers are often rolling these negative balances into new loans, which pushes up the new payment cost.

Automakers have also been more tightly aligning production with demand to avoid discounting vehicles. Incentives on average are about 6.6% of the average transaction price as of October, according to Cox Automotive. Leasing offers a way to get a car with a lower payment, but leasing rates are also down.

Vehicle trade-in data suggests that many buyers who are snapping up those fast-selling cheaper vehicles are either first-time buyers or are purchasing supplemental vehicles, Edmunds' Drury said.

The subcompact SUV segment is one lower-priced group that has seen sales rise in 2026. That is a segment that attracts a lot of first-time buyers. But this is still a relatively small share of the total car market, Fiorani said.

"It's not the mainstream vehicle that Americans want," he said. "It is the vehicle that entry-level buyers need to get to work, to get to school. ... It's people who don't have families yet."

Repeat buyers are less likely to want to step down into a compact car or SUV, Drury said.

"The moment you get into that other vehicle and it's a little bit smaller, it's not as comfortable, it doesn't fit all your stuff, all your kid's equipment for soccer practice, you're done," he said. "You're not giving up 360 cameras, ventilated seats, heated steering wheels, these niceties that you've become accustomed to. Automakers know that too. Dealers know that."

The truly cash-strapped are either snapping up the relatively few low-priced cars on dealer lots, moving to the used market, or holding on to their cars longer.

"The people who are trying to buy the smaller vehicles are really hurt by the way the economy is at the moment," Fiorani said. "So those people are trying to move into used vehicles or trying to maintain their current vehicle as long as they can. Automakers should be prepared for a softening in the marketplace."

#517. Automakers like Rivian and Ford are ‘democratizing 3D printing’ to reduce costs and increase speed

DETROIT — Automakers including Ford Motor and Rivian Automotive are leaning into a new type of manufacturing line: 3D printing.

Manufacturers say it's helping them reduce costs and increase speed. The technology has been around for decades, but it's becoming more commoditized in the automotive industry, with 3D printers used to create everything from vehicle parts and accessories to tools for assembly plants and testing units for research and development labs.

"At this point, we don't have a choice but to scale up and begin using it through all factions of Rivian," Jonathan Dankenbring, Rivian senior manager of prototype manufacturing, told CNBC during a tour of the automaker's expansive 3D-printing operations in suburban Detroit. "We're going to be democratizing 3D printing for the entire company."

Also known as additive manufacturing, 3D printing can create three-dimensional objects from digital designs by dispensing material layer by layer, much like a traditional printer does with ink on paper. Instead of ink, the machines use a wide variety of materials such as plastics, composites, ceramics and even metals.

Printers can range from hundreds of dollars for hobby-like devices to hundreds of thousands of dollars for specialty equipment, depending on how big the product being produced is and the material it needs. They can produce individual pieces or hundreds of smaller parts in a batch using powders and resins.

Rivian said it has spent more than $5 million on 3D-printing machines, with the cost of materials for the operations growing to about $1.7 million annually.

Dankenbring said despite the costs, the machines typically pay for themselves within a year. He said the technology helps lower supplier tooling costs, streamline logistics expenses and cut production times for parts from weeks to days, if not hours.

"It's well in the millions [for savings], but the main thing is speed for Rivian," Dankenbring said.

The all-electric vehicle company's latest expansion of 3D printing includes adding new machines that can build flexible silicone parts as well as installing a bay of machines at its plant in Normal, Illinois, for employees to print parts on demand.

"3D prints should be readily available for every engineer so that they can make a part and get it in their hands as soon as possible," Dankenbring said.

Rivian has also used the technology for electric-motor models for fluid testing, creating screw guides for tooling and stamping press inserts and some production parts, including trim pieces to fill gaps in some early builds.

There's a similar push underway at larger, more-established automakers like Ford and its crosstown rival General Motors, which have used 3D printing for many years, including in their massive assembly plants.

Instead of having a bay of 3D-printing machines like at Rivian, Ford has departments for additive manufacturing at many of its plants to produce and distribute needed parts. That includes a new vending machine for 3D-printed parts for production of F-150 pickup trucks near its Dearborn, Michigan, headquarters.

"By putting the vending machine up, all the team leaders have access," said Gregory Foresi, a Ford mechanical engineer who ran Ford's 3D-printing lab at the plant from 2024 until last month. "We're able to keep that thing stocked rather than have a bunch of parts laying around in the lab. So that's been pretty huge."

Foresi used roughly two dozen machines to print off thousands of tools, some of which have been designed by employees, such as torque bit sockets, hood jackers and hand tools for smoothing adhesive.

Employees can use an app to request that specific auto parts or tools be printed. Ford's 3D-printing teams help develop them or determine what to produce next, as the machining time to build different parts can range from hours to days, if not weeks.

Foresi said creating the tools with 3D printing has allowed the automaker to uniquely design them when needed, get them faster and, in many cases, receive them at a lower cost.

Ford also has used 3D-printed parts to help keep production running. For example, Foresi said a large machining wheel was needed to maintain assembly line production and the company was able to 3D print the part until a permanent replacement from a supplier was available.

"They didn't perform as well, and it's not really a replacement for the ones that we were purchasing, but they kept the plant running," Foresi said.

Ford said it expects to have 3D labs deployed at more than 23 plants by the end of this year. Its current 3D-printing program has more than 200 machines, and there are additional printers outside the official program, the company said.

GM has used 3D-printed parts for some of its low-volume products in addition to research and development and inside plants, including recently expanding its use of industrial Stratasys printers that can cost hundreds of thousands of dollars based on use case, training and certification.

The Detroit automaker uses 135 components that are 3D-printed for its bespoke Cadillac Celestiq as well as other components for its performance Cadillac Blackwing sedans, including a unique medallion on the manual shifter knob.

Fadi Abro, senior global director of automotive and mobility at 3D-printing company Stratasys, said GM has been a great example of how companies can "standardize and then scale" 3D printing.

"The material properties improved so significantly that the use cases improved," he said, noting the company works with virtually every major automaker. "From a product perspective, we have to get the technology to become faster and more accessible, in an economic sense."

Physicl is entering a crowded category of startups vying to provide data and tooling to foundation model builders and robot operators. “The space is very young and very blurry sometimes,” de Vigan admits. There are a variety of companies collecting, labeling, or generating data in different modalities as their customers try to figure out the secret sauce that will make robots live up to their promise.

In particular, Physicl wants to focus on post-training models on difficult edge cases. The startup is releasing a product called Studio with 10,000 3D environments and 100,000 assets that represent various objects. It lets users create scenes to generate synthetic data for training robots on specific problems. For example, Studio could be used to generate training data to help a robot that has trouble discerning a cup on a cluttered counter-top, or while the cup is in shadow.

SpAItial, a world model builder, collaborated with Physicl as an early customer to improve the fidelity of its products, the latest of which, Echo-3, was released last week. SpAItial’s tool generates 3D simulations from 2D imagery, and to do that effectively, its CEO and founder Matthias Niessner said they needed to train on the kind of high-fidelity synthetic data Physicl offers.

However, Physicl wants to be more than a data provider; it sees itself as the tooling for ongoing synthetic data generation. “What we created 10 years ago, without knowing it, was basically an agentic workflow to transform unstructured information into high-fidelity 3D,” de Vigan said. #498. Ramp data shows AI spending stalling just as Anthropic nears its IPO

Buy One Founder Summit Ticket, Get a Second for Free. Register now.

Founder Summit begins Nov. 4, 9:00 a.m. ET. Get your pass savings & deals before it starts. REGISTER HERE.

Companies are using more AI than ever, but they’re not spending more on it.

A new report from Ramp shows AI spending flatlined over late summer even as token volumes rose, a sign that price competition and savvier customers may be capping revenue for OpenAI and Anthropic. According to Ramp’s data, token spend only grew 1.5% across August and September. To compare, in July, it grew 42% from the previous month.

As Anthropic gears up for its IPO, the data suggests investors might be wrong to assume AI companies will keep growing at breakneck speed.

“Almost the entire spend on AI is from venture-backed, high-growth tech companies,” Ramp’s lead economist Ara Kharazian told TechCrunch. “That’s fine as long as AI spend continues to rise, but if it starts to slow down because of something in the market or some other factor, they will all start to slow down simultaneously, and that could lead to other cascading effects.”

Kharazian said he’s already seeing evidence that the top 1% of firms — the most “AI-pilled” — have stuttered slightly in their spend in August and September, coming up just below the $10,000 per-employee they were hitting at their summer peaks. A slowdown among that cohort is “sufficient to slow down revenue entirely, despite the fact that we’re seeing rising volumes,” he said.

Ramp data shows growth in spending among median and top 10%, but it’s not enough to make up the difference. Median firms spend $12-$15 per employee per month, and the top 10% spend $600-$1,000.

“If you look at the use cases today, it’s not necessarily clear they will get up to that $10,000 per employee per month mark that the 1% is at,” Kharazian said. While there’s plenty of demand for AI coding agents in industries like software and finance, “it’s not necessarily true that a doctor’s office or a car dealership will need to spend that much on AI.”

“The era of hypergrowth may be over, and now that we know there may not be as much juice to squeeze out of the top 1%,” he continued.

October data did see a re-acceleration, with token spend up 8.9% from September 23 to October 23, but Kharazian said the headwinds on spend growth are strong.

Kharazian (who has published a series of blogs called “Cracks in the AI Thesis”) said the slowdown isn’t due to a demand problem. Again, AI volumes are up. Instead he points to the competitive nature of the marketplace.

“OpenAI and Anthropic price cuts have…just made it cheaper to use AI,” he said. In September, OpenAI cut API prices in half for GPT-6 Sol and GPT-6 Luna. Answering the price war, Anthropic has also made significant reductions to pricing for Fable 5.1 and Opus 5.5 — a decision that the company said in a recent filing slashed revenue growth by about $600 million. Both companies have also been offering enterprise discounts that are intended to lock-in spend, but in reality are hitting their revenue targets.

Kharazian said this dynamic means it has very much become a buyer’s market.

Subsidizing use of expensive technology in order to lock in customers has always been a part of the Big Tech playbook. Just recall how cheap it used to be to take an Uber or Lyft ride, and now that there are few other ride-hail players, the cost of going from A to B has risen significantly. Kharazian says those dynamics don’t work in the AI world because there’s just too much competition.

“Meta and Nvidia are also introducing enterprise offerings with pretty cheap models or open source models,” Kharazian said. He also pointed to the range of other open model companies from China and the United States, as well as a new company called Jev — a lightweight tool for choosing between a few predetermined outputs — that is increasingly among the top performers on Ramp.

“For a lot of AI use cases, you just need a simple Yes/No,” Kharazian said. “So you take your already cheap models, you shift them over to Jev, and now your already cheap stuff gets even cheaper.”

The ability to complete workloads at a much cheaper cost is another factor driving the spending slowdown. That’s partly due to the discounts from AI companies, but also because companies are just getting better at being thrifty.

“As you get really advanced in using AI, you get better at figuring out how to control it,” Kharazian said. “You’re more likely to use defaults that prevent usage of very expensive models for long-running tasks. You’re more likely to impose some budgets on employees. You’re more likely to offload some of the spend from standard and light models to even cheaper light models via routers.”

Notion co-founder Akshay Kothari recently posted internal data that matched Ramp’s insights. As he succinctly put it: “tokens go brrr. costs go ahhh.”

“A lot of the assumptions that we had about the trajectory of AI spend are starting to break down,” Kharazian said. “You can use more AI, but use it for less. That’s great for the customers…but it’s not necessarily great for the model companies. And it does introduce new questions around their revenue trajectories.”

Bloomberg reported Friday that Anthropic’s potential valuation sank from the $2 trillion estimated in early October to just $1.2 to $1.3 trillion. The company is also reportedly expected to receive a high “junk” rating from credit agencies. OpenAI also lowered its proposed valuation to $1.25 trillion from $1.4 trillion and slashed its latest fundraising goals by about $10 billion.

Kharazian says Ramp’s dataset likely informs many investor concerns. There is, however, one potential brightside for spending trajectories, albeit a speculative one.

Much of the thesis for hypergrowth spending is that AI will undoubtedly introduce new use cases that will accelerate things. AI coding emerged just a year and a half ago as the most commercially viable product that has led Anthropic and OpenAI to becoming trillion-dollar companies. The hope among investors is that there will be another revolution that will drive further spend.

Some think personal agents will be the next coding agents. OpenAI, SpaceXAI, and Meta have all released their own personal agents, alongside startups like Instinct. Kharazian is skeptical, though, that personal agents, which are “not as economically productive as coding,” will provide the boost the industry needs for nonstop growth.

“The projection is that there will be new products being introduced that will drive further growth,” Kharazian said. “But based on what we can observe today and the products available and what is commercially advanced, AI spend has started to slow down.”

Security researchers have discovered hundreds of copies of a Chinese-made AI hacking tool publicly exposed to the internet for anyone to access, potentially allowing outsiders to launch cyberattacks against unsuspecting targets.

The hacking software, called Artex, is an open source autonomous penetration testing tool, meaning software that automatically probes computer systems for weaknesses, and it’s been linked to several cyberattacks since its public release in July. As open source software, it’s free for anyone to download and run on their own servers, making it a popular tool for those who cannot access Anthropic or OpenAI’s most advanced cybersecurity AI models for discovering security vulnerabilities. Microsoft has previously described Artex as a system of AI agents that can conduct cyberattacks “from reconnaissance through data exfiltration with little needed human interaction.”

In a blog post, cybersecurity firm Censys, which scans the internet for exposed or vulnerable systems, said that it identified more than 800 distinct deployments of Artex that were accessible from the web without needing a password to log in.

Silas Cutler, principal security researcher at Censys who wrote the blog post, said that not every instance of Artex is operated maliciously and many are used for authorized testers. But it’s unclear why hundreds of these Artex systems are exposed to the web, as the software does not require them to be accessible from the internet.

The findings underscore how even powerful hacking tools can be misconfigured to be publicly accessible by anyone, increasing the chance that someone will find and misuse them. The publicly accessible systems also expose attack tools, such as implants (hidden malicious software) used to compromise and maintain persistent access to a victim’s network.

Security companies have linked the use of Artex to several broad-scale cyberattacks, including against the Korean finance industry in September. Cybersecurity giant CrowdStrike attributed the attacks to a Chinese-speaking hacker who used an Artex instance in the attacks, in part because the hacker had left an “open directory” hosting the system. Censys said that its data showed the Artex instance in question had been publicly exposed for months, and appeared to contain more attack data that extended “well beyond South Korean banks,” including payment and gaming companies.

Artex was also used in an attack on IT support giant ConnectWise earlier in October, which allowed Chinese-linked hackers to spy on thousands of organizations whose data and files are hosted in Microsoft’s cloud. Microsoft, which has visibility into the attack, said in a blog post that the hackers used Artex to break into customers’ cloud environments and steal their data at such speed that defenders had “little time to detect and respond.”

Many of the attacks linked to Artex involved exploiting simple vulnerabilities, such as guessing weak or known passwords, and a lack of multi-factor authentication.

The findings show that simple vulnerabilities, including misconfigurations, can expose both the attacker and their targets.

Artex has since announced it would go closed-source, effectively limiting further releases for paid customers. There are still dozens of copies of Artex’s code available on code-sharing sites that can still be used in active attacks.

Right before the midterm elections, OpenAI hired Jessica Schumer, daughter of Democratic Sen. Chuck Schumer. The company also said it would start calling its data centers “compute campuses.”

For the past two years, many of Silicon Valley’s biggest names have built closer ties with President Donald Trump and his administration. Administration policies also benefited the industry, including tax breaks, the pausing of some federal investigations, and looser AI regulation. Meanwhile, Meta faced lawsuits and billion-dollar fines, and OpenAI faced suits alleging its technology harmed users.

Now, with the midterms shifting the balance of power in Washington, those same companies are working to rebuild ties with Democrats, who have pledged to tighten oversight of AI. How far that gets them may depend less on image-polishing than on where the real fights are happening: in state capitols, in communities deciding whether to host data centers, and, as we’ve seen this past week, in the markets watching the AI boom.

J.B. Branch, director of federal AI governance and technology policy at Public Citizen, a nonprofit advocacy group that describes itself as nonpartisan, told me that the tech industry “realizes they have an optics problem.” He characterized moves like the Schumer hire and the rebranding as “PR washing.” He expects more of it, too, pointing to OpenAI’s partnership with the American Federation of Teachers, announced last year, as well as Anthropic’s newly launched Claude Corps and Meta’s America’s Workforce Academy. “I would imagine more of that sort of investment in these corporate, private-public partnerships,” he said.

These initiatives didn’t spring up overnight. Meta and other tech companies reportedly began looking for ways to strengthen their ties to Democrats as Democratic gains in the midterms looked more likely. Reached for comment about the midterm elections’ impact on Meta, a company spokesperson pointed TechCrunch to remarks that Meta President Dina Powell McCormick made this week at WSJ Tech. “You have to work with the government that the American people elect,” McCormick had said. “I have seen it across the board that you have to be bipartisan, and you want to be, because you want buy-in on the things that you’re trying to do.”

Not everyone expects them to work. Alice Marwick, director of research at the tech policy nonprofit Data & Society, told me she expects Big Tech to try to pivot back toward the middle, “if not the left. But it’s a little too late,” she said. “Tech companies should be asking themselves how their products can make people’s lives better, because right now, it doesn’t seem like they are.”

If Democrats follow through on pledges to tighten oversight of AI and Big Tech, the industry could face a tougher environment. Data center moratoriums are gaining traction, and a wave of AI regulation bills awaits drafting. At the same event, Powell McCormick said Meta wants to show that communities welcoming its investments will see “an enormous amount of benefit,” while those that don’t want them “will of course have the right to stop them.”

Still, the next two years could bring political gridlock in Washington. Aryan Ranjan, founder of Baraza, a political campaign management software company, told me most action will likely happen at the state level, since states have already sued tech companies and passed regulations. “A coalition built on newer, working-class voters is likely to push Democrats to frame AI around electricity bills, data-center siting and jobs rather than abstract AI safety,” he said of what to expect at the federal level.

Last December, the Trump administration signed an executive order creating the AI Litigation Task Force, a federal group meant to challenge state AI laws. The administration has argued that U.S. AI companies “must be free to innovate without cumbersome regulations.” Many states have nonetheless passed their own laws, including measures on chatbots and deepfakes.

It also remains to be seen how President Trump will respond, including whether he’ll sign more AI executive orders or veto bills that could be seen as anti-AI. Clearly, voters want stronger guardrails around the technology.

In a statement to TechCrunch, a White House official said: “American leadership in super intelligence is essential for our national security, economic prosperity, and the quality of life of the American people. President Trump is committed to ensuring America continues to lead the world in super intelligence while protecting American consumers. As the President has said, he’s willing to work with anyone to continue delivering victories for hardworking Americans.”

Branch argued that Trump’s political fortunes are now tied to the AI industry. On Wednesday, the day after the midterms, a trillion-dollar market sell-off spooked investors, some are whom are reportedly now reconsidering Anthropic’s reported trillion-dollar IPO. “There’s a very strong likelihood that there will be an AI bubble pop, and it’ll happen on Trump’s hands,” Branch said, predicting a possible market collapse. “Does he want to sink or swim along with the AI industry?”

For now, the rules companies face may vary by location. “For the next two years, a company’s AI rules may depend more on where it operates rather than on anything Washington passes,” Ranjan said.
