#595. Uber and Zipline’s drone delivery service takes off in Texas, with dozens of cities to follow

Uber Eats customers in a Houston suburb can now have their food delivered by a Zipline drone, the first commercial launch under a partnership announced earlier this year between Uber and the autonomous drone delivery startup.

The commercial partnership kicks off Tuesday in Spring, Texas, before expanding to Dallas. But the companies have far larger ambitions. Uber and Zipline plan to launch drone delivery services in dozens of additional U.S. cities in their collective bid to make one million deliveries per day using the startup’s drones by the end of 2029.

Zipline’s long-range autonomous delivery drones will be used to shuttle food orders weighing up to eight pounds to Uber Eats customers, flying about 300 feet above the ground at speeds of up to 70 miles per hour. Zipline drones don’t land. Instead, the drones lower the orders, which are encased in a small protective pod, to the customer’s location using a tether.

Uber Eats customers in service areas will be able to tap a new “drone” icon on the app’s home page to view all participating restaurants. Uber said that at launch in the Houston area, several nationwide chains, including Jersey Mike’s and Zaxby’s, along with dozens of local restaurants such as Urban Bird and Abu Omar Halal, will offer drone delivery.

“In partnership with Uber Eats we’re going to create a new expectation for delivery in America,” Zipline CEO and co-founder Keller Cliffton said in a statement that underscores those aspirations.

Companies trying to scale in this nascent industry have argued that delivery via drone is a net benefit for society, citing advantages such as traffic reduction. Customers won’t need to hop in their cars and drive to the store for that forgotten item or takeout order, instead relying on a drone, industry proponents have said.

And while Zipline and other companies have been developing their drones for years, the industry is still in its early days, as is adoption in the communities where services are available.

Zipline, which was founded in 2014, has developed a drone delivery ecosystem that includes aircraft, launch and landing systems, and logistics software. The California-based company’s initial focus was delivering blood in Rwanda, but it eventually expanded its scope to the United States. Its business in the U.S. scaled quickly after receiving its Part 135 air carrier certification from the Federal Aviation Administration in 2022, a critical regulatory hurdle that authorized the company to conduct long-range commercial drone deliveries.

Uber’s partnership extends beyond a commercial relationship with Zipline. The ride-hailing giant also invested in the startup. Neither company has disclosed the investment amount, but Zipline announced last week it had raised $1.5 billion in a round led by Paradigm, WCM Investment Management, and Layer Global, and included backing by Uber, among others.

#592. Daydream’s new AI agent acts as your personal stylist — and can shop for you, too

Can an AI agent serve as your personal stylist? That’s what e-commerce veteran Julie Bornstein’s fashion startup, Daydream, aims to find out.

On Tuesday, the company launched “Daydream Agent,” an AI agent that is described as a personal stylist that can help you plan a look, virtually try on clothes, surface relevant pieces based on your taste, and more.

The idea, the company explains, is to put a personal stylist in everyone’s pocket. This application of AI could be promising, given that early adopters of consumer AI agents like Meta’s Muse have found that online shopping is a key use case.

Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg recently noted that shopping was a top activity for Muse’s over 10 million users and that 25% of people who set up Muse now use it daily.

In Daydream’s case, the fashion startup has been focused on using AI to deliver personalized shopping results to its users. Its new AI agent builds on its earlier work with an AI chatbot that could help users find outfits and make suggestions, but not complete purchases.

Now the company says its Daydream Agent expands on that experience by remembering each shopper’s own personal style, fit preferences, and budget to refine its recommendations. Shoppers can also upload a photo to have the agent reuse that image to virtually try on any item they’ve saved. When they find something they like, the agent can complete a purchase through a one-click checkout process.

The agent can also handle other fashion-related tasks, like keeping an eye on when items go on sale or when brands have a new drop, or providing weekly updates or spotting new fashion trends.

Also, instead of leaving items in their cart, shoppers can save pieces they’re interested in to a persistent “clothing rack” so they can compare options and think about their purchases before deciding what to buy. Shoppers can also connect their own closet and their calendar to the agent, helping them to build outfits around what they already own and plan what to wear for upcoming events.

The tech works by pulling real-time pricing and availability information from its catalog of over 10,000 brands, then combines that with its fashion-focused artificial intelligence. It’s powered by a combination of OpenAI, Gemini, and Claude models, which it fine-tuned, along with its own trained models designed for particular use cases.

However, considering that fashion is more of an art than a science, the company says it’s keeping humans in the loop. To do so, this month it brought on its first stylist in residence, Francesca Salih, an international fashion stylist and the founder of StyleGrid. Salih provides the startup with styling content that helps shape how the agent pulls together outfits and offers advice. This advice is part of the company’s fashion knowledge database, which the agent experience relies on.

Bornstein, formerly Stitch Fix’s COO and the founder of The Yes (exited to Pinterest in 2022), started Daydream in 2023 as an AI search engine. The company, backed by $50 million in funding from Forerunner Ventures, Index Ventures, GV, and True Ventures, says more than 2 million shoppers have used Daydream to discover apparel and accessories since its June 2025 launch.

#584. AI models can be tricked into identifying civilians as military targets by telling them it’s a game

Some AI models from Anthropic, OpenAI, and other leading AI labs could be easily tricked into identifying targets for military strikes — even if they include civilians or locations like schools — simply by being told they’re playing a game, according to a new research report by Vals.

Vals, a San Francisco-based AI evaluation startup, tested whether leading AI models could be manipulated into participating in autonomous warfare. Researchers used simulated scenarios and a combination of generated and real imagery from Ukraine and Iran to see if the AI models would mark targets in drone and satellite imagery.

The models from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, DeepSeek, and other companies frequently identified potential targets in drone and satellite imagery, including civilians and surrendering soldiers. When the models refused to cooperate, researchers were able to jailbreak them by claiming the battlefield scenarios were part of a game.

The findings come amid growing fears about the immediate risks posed by AI, beyond the existential threats associated with artificial general intelligence.

Earlier this year, Anthropic became embroiled in a dispute with the Department of Defense after it told the agency it could not use Anthropic’s technology for mass surveillance of Americans or autonomous warfare. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei has insisted that today’s AI systems aren’t reliable enough to make life-or-death decisions without a human in the loop. Meanwhile, the Pentagon argued it should be able to deploy the technology for any lawful military purpose.

The new research raises further questions about whether AI companies are capable of preventing their models from being used in warfare.

Ironically, AI models from Anthropic performed the worst in these simulated scenarios. But none of the models performed particularly well. None of the researchers’ requests were blocked by a safety classifier, and refusals were uncommon. Even when models did refuse, they often complied after being told they were playing a game.

Anthropic spokesperson Eduardo Maia Silva told TechCrunch the company appreciates Vals’ report, recommendations, and collaboration, and acknowledged that the outcomes were not acceptable.

“The examples in the report are very clearly against our rules and we have banned and will ban users when we see this behavior,” according to an emailed company statement sent by Silva. “Even for authorized settings, Anthropic has long been firm that today’s frontier AI systems are not reliable enough to power fully autonomous weapons.”

Anthropic told TechCrunch that its teams were already developing additional safeguards before the report was published and that those measures would be in place soon. Anthropic is also expanding its own testing and enhancing safeguards and enforcement to address any gaps it finds.

OpenAI spokesperson Nate Evans told TechCrunch the company prohibits using its public models to facilitate violence or for unapproved national security purposes, including in the scenarios Vals simulated.

“We actively detect and disrupt misuse, including by suspending accounts that violate our policies,” OpenAI said in an emailed statement. “We’re also developing additional safeguards for our public models against misuse involving drones and drone software.”

Vals notes in the report that the models were never asked to directly make strike decisions, a task the researchers said the models would typically refuse. That doesn’t mean their outputs couldn’t have dire or fatal consequences in the real world. For instance, targeting information could be used by autonomous weapons systems.

In some scenarios, the models were asked to report whether people were visible. Positive reports were followed by simulated strikes and the models would see the consequences.

The report relies on commercial models from frontier AI labs, not the models those companies might provide to the military. It’s not clear whether the additional safeguards would apply to military use, though.

#569. As decision model use skyrockets, is customization the next frontier?

In October, more than one in four requests on Vercel’s AI model router went to decision models, not LLMs — an enormous share of work for a kind of computerized intelligence that was invented only a month earlier.

Decision models are LLMs that don’t produce output tokens; instead, they’re tuned to use their general intelligence to quickly and cheaply decide between preset options, while registering its level of confidence in the choice. They’re useful in production environments, where fast decisions can be more important than the reasoning provided by a slower and more expensive frontier model.

TypeSafe became a $7.5 billion company after releasing its Jev family of models, which continues to dominate usage (at least where we can see metrics from services like OpenRouter, which handled more than 1.5 billion queries to TypeSafe’s models last week).

However, it’s easy to make a decision model, and dozens were released in the wake of Jev. One Amazon engineer made one in a weekend, and soon every hyperscaler had its own option. On Tuesday, Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella said that his company is rolling out its decision model, Microsoft-Decision-1.1, for wide use on Azure immediately.

The action is happening on both ends of the ecosystem: Sqwish, a U.K.-based AI startup behind a decision model called kettei fat, announced a $3 million pre-seed round today. Co-founders Ushnish Sengupta and Federica Freddi, who met as Cambridge grad students, had been developing small LLMs when Jev arrived on the scene, and they realized their architectures could be repurposed as a powerful decision model.

Data appears to be the key to a successful model in this category — TypeSafe CEO Diogo Almeida told TechCrunch that the hardest part of building Jev was finely calibrating its judgement using statistically-adjusted synthetic data. Sqwish, too, has been working with synthetic data, but its unique offering is built-in post-training tools that use a customer’s own data to improve the model’s judgement.

“What makes us not a commodity, I think, is the fact that we are offering our customers the same power…they can keep uploading their production data, and just the same way that we trained our models, their models can also keep getting better at the tasks that they care about,” CEO Ushnish Sengupta told TechCrunch.

Attempting to rank decision models is difficult; Sqwish’s “kettei fat” model has performed well on some benchmarks, but most builders of the technology say that the real proof is in the pudding: It’s generally simple for developers to sub in one model for another and see how it does in a specific context.

“The benchmark is more of an invitation to try the model out, and then the continual tuning is the reason why they should stick to us,” Sengupta said.

The Microsoft-Decision-1.1 model is also the result of the company carefully balancing across cost, latency, intelligence and calibration. Achint Srivastava, the engineering VP who led the project, agreed that too much focus on benchmarks is a mistake.

“What we see is that ‘benchmaxed’ models fall flat under real enterprise usage very quickly,” Srivastava wrote in an email. “Our testing underscores just how challenging it is to deliver accuracy, reliability, speed and cost efficiency in a single model. Microsoft-Decision-1.1 and Jev are among the few that perform strongly across all four dimensions, even when evaluated on ‘held out’ or unseen benchmarks reflective of real use cases.”

For startups like Sqwish, competition with hyperscalers and frontier labs is an opportunity. Freddi predicts that future systems will see so-called System One intelligence, like decision models, working in concert with System Two intelligence, like frontier LLMs.

“Even in that future, System One is maybe a $10 billion, $50 billion [opportunity], and OpenAI is doing this IPO with you know $1 trillion, $2 trillion market value in mind,” she told TechCrunch. “This is all we care about; whereas for them, this is just a side project.”

Trillion-dollar-plus IPOs may be stealing the headlines, but for companies hoping to launch that aren’t Anthropic or SpaceX, the path to public markets looks less certain. Companies that have spent years getting IPO-ready may have to wait longer to launch as market conditions shift yet again.

To explore how companies can prepare for an IPO in an unpredictable climate, The Information reporter Dakin Campbell spoke to three investment experts about the long road to going public.

Successful IPOs, said Kate Claassen, should be market-ambivalent.

“It’s more about being a successful public company on days when markets go up and when markets go down,” she said, adding that success means having alignment with investors on strategy and the metrics that point to it working.

Kiran Choudary knows first hand how long the road can be. Rubrik began preparing for an IPO in late 2022 but didn’t go public until early 2024, after a prolonged slowdown in the IPO market. Choudary broke preparation into three stages.

The first is business readiness: having the scale, executive team and durable growth story to appeal to investors. The second is financial and risk infrastructure, including clean audits and an audit committee. The third is the IPO process itself: working with the Securities and Exchange Commission, bankers and lawyers, and testing the waters with investors.

“Since 2022, there’s never really been a solidly open window,” Choudary said. Instead, there have been short periods when a handful of companies go public before activity slows again. “If you want to be a public company, if your business is ready and you feel good about it, you have to find a reasonable window and go.”

More companies are staying private for longer, in part because they have the option of raising capital and growing outside the public markets. The NYSE is responding by getting involved earlier in companies’ IPO journeys, sometimes as early as Series A, giving them access to the exchange’s resources and its network of 2,400 publicly listed companies.

“A lot of times, we’re hosting events where private issuers can get exposure to their publicly listed counterparts. That’s exposure to potential end-market customers,” said Crystal Shen. “You leave a lot on the table if you wait until the very end to engage with an exchange.”

Investors need the same early attention, said Claassen, who advised companies to build relationships with major IPO investors years before a roadshow begins. Existing investors who know a company well are more likely to participate in its IPO and remain significant shareholders afterward.

“Data does show that the longer an investor’s been with you, the longer they’re going to stick with you,” she said.

Even decisions made during a company’s earliest funding rounds can have consequences much later. Shen pointed to index inclusion, which can hinge on how a company is positioned in registration statements and how much of its stock is available for public trading. Voting rights in the hands of unrestricted investors, for example, can affect eligibility for indexes such as the Russell US Equity.

“That should shape how you structure some of your private rounds to ensure you have [the] appropriate float for index inclusion down the line,” Shen said.

As companies spend longer in private markets, raising more capital and growing larger before going public, IPOs are getting bigger. The funds investing in them are growing, too.

“The large funds are getting larger, and it’s creating this cycle,” Shen said, noting that smaller and mid-cap funds are declining in both number and assets. That makes it harder for smaller companies to attract institutional investors. Choudary agreed, recalling that when he was a banker, software companies with $100 million to $150 million in revenue could go public.

“In tech, I don’t think that sized company going public even exists anymore,” he said.

The pressure doesn’t end at the IPO. Claassen said the biggest challenge is volatility between listing and the end of the lockup period, which can hit employees’ tax bills. Shen added that how companies structure tender offers, in which shares are bought from existing holders, can have repercussions later on. Those programs often let only current employees and certain early investors sell, leaving other investors and former employees to find liquidity once the company goes public. That, she said, creates pent-up supply ahead of the IPO.

“Companies and management teams need to think about that increased level of secondary activity pre-IPO,” Shen said, “to alleviate some of those issues you’re going to have post-IPO.”

“When it comes to policy, I think the language needs to be, ‘Hey, this is not us complaining. This is us saying here is an opportunity to make the magnitude of impact even greater,’” Wehmeier said. “When we frame it as problems, that becomes harder.”

#509. Goldman CEO Solomon says AI financing remains strong despite market jitters

Goldman Sachs CEO David Solomon told CNBC on Monday that financing demand for artificial intelligence infrastructure remains strong despite recent market jitters as investors reassess the risks involving the massive build-out.

Solomon said that his firm has been "very active" in AI capital markets deals over recent weeks, a setup that he expects to continue.

"There's still very strong demand for investments in the AI infrastructure build and the technology around the growth of AI," Solomon told CNBC's Leslie Picker.

Changes in the political environment are among factors that will "create recalibrations and ups and downs, and we're seeing some of that," Solomon said. "But I still deeply believe in the long-term trajectory of the power of AI in our economy to drive productivity."

AI-related stocks, including chipmakers and neocloud providers, have tumbled in recent weeks as investors reassess whether the hundreds of billions of dollars being poured into AI infrastructure will generate good returns.

The selloff, which began before the U.S. midterm elections, intensified after Democrats scored sweeping victories on Tuesday, raising concerns that political opposition could slow the industry's expansion.

The shifting sentiment comes at a key moment for AI model lab Anthropic, which is preparing for a potential IPO that could become one of the biggest tests yet of public-market appetite for AI companies. Goldman is among the banks leading that IPO process.

"We'll see, obviously, if this IPO moves forward, how it all plays out," Solomon said, declining to comment in detail about the IPO. "I would say that the market environment is still pretty constructive."

Solomon also pushed back against political resistance to data center construction, arguing that the infrastructure is essential to the U.S. economy and that it can be built responsibly.

"We certainly don't want this infrastructure going to places outside the U.S., which is what will naturally happen because the demand for compute is there," Solomon said.

#489. Nvidia promises that revenue will keep soaring — for now

Despite the turmoil at Nvidia following the departure of its disgraced founder and CEO Jensen Huang, the company — as expected — delivered a solid quarter on Tuesday. More importantly, reassured investors that its streak of record-breaking revenue won’t stop.

On Tuesday, CFO-turned-interim CEO Colette Kress, reported revenue of $115.8 billion, up 103% from the year-ago quarter and a beat above the company’s guidance. Nvidia had told Wall Street to expect $108 billion in revenue for its third quarter of fiscal 2027. Kress also projected an even bigger fourth quarter, with revenue expected to reach $130 billion, plus or minus 3%.

She also said full-year revenue is on track to exceed expectations.

“We are raising our fiscal year 2028 outlook. We expect revenue to grow approximately 75% year over year, up from 70% and off what it now a higher fiscal 2027 base,” Kress said on the quarterly conference call on Tuesday. “Our outlook considers data center readiness, the strength and durability of end customer demand, and our customers’ access to capital.”

The company also reminded investors that it was using some of its billions in cash to buy back shares. In September 2026, Nvidia increased its share buyback program by $150 billion. It spent $28.1 billion on its own shares during the quarter and still had another $227.3 billion remaining in the kitty for additional repurchases.

Ironically, Nvidia could also benefit from the plunge in its share price following Huang’s resignation last week, should shares remain depressed over the longer term. The stock is down about 10% since his departure. Investors, however, celebrated Tuesday’s quarterly results, sending shares up about 4% in after-hours trading.

Kress stressed that the company was in good shape now and for the foreseeable future, even without Huang at the helm.

“We have the best technologists on the planet with a world-class multi-year roadmap,” Kress stressed. “From business perspective, it’s business as usual. We’ve engaged with our customers, we’ve engaged with our suppliers, our partners. They’re all 100% up behind us.”

She added that the company has secured memory and advanced packaging capacity and is working with its chip manufacturer, TSMC, to “narrow the supply and demand gaps.”
