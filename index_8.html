#665. Automatic 401(k) rollovers reduce cash-outs, research finds — but few accounts reach new employer plans

Leaving a job can make a small 401(k) hard to hold on to: Some employers' plans cash out accounts under $1,000 or move slightly larger balances into an IRA. Taking the money in cash and moving on can feel like the simplest option, but it can have short- and long-term financial consequences.

A system backed by major 401(k) administrators aims to make it easier for those savings to follow workers to their next job. New research from one of those companies, Vanguard, shows it's helping fewer people cash out — although most of the money has yet to reach workers' new retirement plans.

Among workers with less than $1,000 in their 401(k), the share who cashed out within 10 months of leaving their job fell to about 9% from roughly 50% after their employers began participating in a new system called the Portability Services Network, according to the Vanguard research. Among those with balances of between $1,000 and $7,000, the share cashing out fell to 31% from 39%.

Portability Services Network is a coalition of large 401(k) administrators — including Vanguard, Fidelity Investments and Alight Solutions, among others — in conjunction with Retirement Clearinghouse and has been in operation since late 2023.

How it works: When a worker with a small 401(k) balance leaves an employer that participates in the network, the money is generally automatically rolled into a safe harbor IRA, where it remains until the network matches it with the worker's new employer plan. The balance then gets rolled into the new 401(k) from that interim IRA. The catch, however, is that the receiving employer must also participate in the network for the money to move to the next employer's plan.

In Vanguard's research, just 6% of accounts that have been transferred into IRAs as part of this automatic portability network successfully made it to a new employer's plan as of early 2026, largely because few employers have opted in. The rest generally remained in those interim IRAs, waiting to be matched with another employer plan that participates in the network. While workers can always roll over the money into their new 401(k) themselves, the point of the network is for it to happen automatically.

Of the roughly 220,000 outbound rollovers involving Vanguard participants analyzed, the median balance was just $200, and nearly three-quarters involved less than $1,000. The typical saver affected was 31 years old, earned about $20,000 annually and had worked for their employer for roughly six months, according to the research.

The cash-out analysis is based on workers at 83 employer-sponsored retirement plans administered by Vanguard that have adopted the automatic portability.

Typically, when you part ways with an employer, you can leave your money in its 401(k) plan if the account value is above $7,000. Balances under $1,000 usually result in the plan cashing you out and mailing you a check. For amounts between $1,000 and $7,000, most plans roll the balance over to an IRA if the former employee takes no action to move it themselves.

Cashing out a 401(k) when leaving a job, whether done by an employer or the employee, can have lasting financial consequences. Workers who withdraw their savings can owe income taxes on the money and may face a 10% early withdrawal penalty if they're younger than 59½. They also lose the opportunity for that money to grow over time.

Most workers — 84% — have changed employers at least once during their careers, while 53% have switched three or more times and 27% have made at least five moves, according to a new study from Empower, a 401(k) recordkeeper and a founding partner of the portability network. The report is based on an online survey of 2,005 adults.

Additionally, 50% of Generation Z respondents and 45% of millennials said they may have retirement savings from a previous employer that they've lost track of, the survey found. That compares with 30% of Gen X and 14% of baby boomers.

Since the Portability Services Network's launch in November 2023, there have been 53,118 completed transactions, totaling $71.3 million in assets transferred from a previous employer plan to a new employer plan via automatic portability, according to Steve Holman, senior vice president of Portability Services Network strategy and development at Retirement Clearinghouse.

There are 23,787 plans that have adopted automatic portability, representing 7.73 million participants, Holman said. The overall estimate of participants in 401(k)s and similar workplace plans is about 96 million, he said.

There are 1.22 million eligible participants waiting to be matched with a new employer, up more than 25% since the beginning of 2026, Holman said.

Keeping the money in a safe harbor IRA can be better for investors financially than cashing out — but management fees may take a toll on small balances, especially when the money is in an interest-bearing cash account or other conservative investment.

"For many of these small accounts, earnings on the conservative default investment may not be sufficient to offset recurring account-level fees while the account remains unmatched, leaving the balance vulnerable to gradual erosion," the Vanguard study said.

Workers with Roth savings face another complication. If retirement savings are in a traditional 401(k) — funded with pretax contributions and taxed when withdrawn in retirement — it goes into a traditional IRA. Likewise, Roth 401(k)s, funded with after-tax contributions and withdrawn tax-free down the road, go into Roth IRAs.

However, IRS rules don't allow for a Roth IRA to be rolled into a new employer's 401(k), even a Roth 401(k).

"We are advocating for future legislation to allow for Roth dollars to be eligible for auto-portability — our technology can support it today," Holman said.

#609. Why China is desperate to ship cars outside of the country

To understand why China is desperate to ship cars overseas, visit a dealership in Beijing.

Autumn has traditionally been the peak time to buy vehicles in the country, but this year many showrooms are empty or shut.

"In past years, customers would come to the showrooms nonstop," he told CNBC during an interview in his deserted showroom at a complex of dealerships on the outskirts of the Chinese capital. "We would have deals to sign every day. But nowadays we see only two to three deals a week. It's a dramatic drop."

Many people in China aren't buying cars because an unstable job market and a continued slide in property prices have undercut consumer confidence. The government has also scaled back incentives such as cash-for-clunkers subsidies and tax breaks to go electric, car salespeople said.

Passenger car retail sales for 2026 in China totaled 13.33 million units through September, falling 21.7% from a year ago, according to the China Passenger Car Association. For the full year 2026, China's domestic passenger car retail sales are set to drop 22% to 18.5 million vehicles, the association said — under the level recorded in the first year of the pandemic.

Even electric vehicles, which were once a bright spot, are down. Gasoline car sales have been declining in China because of the country's transition to electric vehicles, but sales in China of new-energy vehicles — which includes EVs — dropped by 12.8% from January through September, according to the association.

"Customers are watching every penny," Li said. "When looking at all the new functions on all these new models, they are weighing what is most practical, economic and worth their money over the long term. We don't see impulse buying. #515. Ford reports mixed third-quarter results, says 2027 earnings will be in line with this year

DETROIT — Ford Motor expects earnings next year will be roughly in line with the automaker's anticipated results this year, the company said Wednesday as it reported mixed results for the third quarter.

The Detroit automaker beat Wall Street's third-quarter earnings expectations but slightly missed revenue estimates. The company has been working to stabilize production of its F-Series pickup trucks after dealing with supplier issues for much of the past year.

"This quarter's results reflect deliberate actions to improve profitability and deliver consistent financial returns," Ford CFO Sherry House told reporters during a call Wednesday.

Ford reported third-quarter adjusted free cash flow of $4.7 billion, roughly $2 billion higher than what analysts were anticipating, according to StreetAccount.

The Detroit automaker also narrowed its 2026 guidance to adjusted earnings before interest and taxes of between $10.5 billion and $11 billion, compared with a previous outlook of $10 billion to $11 billion. Ford maintained its adjusted free cash flow guidance for this year of between $6 billion and $7 billion.

House said the company expects 2027 earnings to be roughly in line with this year's results, citing several offsetting factors in production issues this year as well as rising commodity costs.

House noted a $1.3 billion tariff refund during the first quarter of this year was largely offset by additional costs from alternative aluminum sourcing that the company does not expect to have next year, leveling out the year-over-year comparison.

She also said production of its F-Series Super Duty trucks in Canada in 2027 will make up for lost production it's expecting at its F-150 factories to retool for new models.

"Next year will be the third year on our journey of increasing and improving our warranty and material cost savings, and that's getting extended to structural costs throughout the company as well in terms of savings," House said.

Ford CEO Jim Farley called 2027 a "foundational year" for the company on its way to a previously announced target of an 8% adjusted EBIT margin by 2029. The company reported a 5.4% margin during the third quarter.

Ahead of the results, Wall Street analysts were mixed about whether Ford would be able to maintain profits in 2027.

The company has multiple efforts that will add to costs next year: It's rolling out several new or redesigned models, including its next-generation EV and Canada-produced pickups, and is enhancing its energy business that it announced earlier this year.

The missed revenue for the third quarter was a result of each of the automaker's main business units – which are broken into Blue traditional operations, Pro fleet and Model e electric vehicle segments – underperforming compared with Wall Street's expectations, according to StreetAccount.

As part of its modified guidance, Ford said it now expects pre-tax earnings of its Ford Blue unit to be $5.5 billion, which is at the high end of a previous guidance.

It's also anticipating EBIT of about $7 billion for the year for Ford Pro and Ford Model e losses of about $4 billion. Its Ford Credit unit is expected to record about $2.8 billion in EBIT for 2026.

Ford's overall revenue, which includes its credit segment, was off roughly 2% compared with the third quarter of last year, to $49.5 billion.

Its net income also was off $400 million from a year earlier to $2 billion, while adjusted earnings before interest and taxes were up slightly year-over-year to $2.7 billion.

Ford's 2025 third-quarter results included $47.19 billion in automotive revenue, net income of $2.4 billion and earnings per share of 45 cents adjusted.

#493. The market view of the oil, AI and bond trades went through a major change in October

If there was one safe bet over the course of 2026, it was that treasury yields would rise alongside a move up in oil futures contracts. Over the past month, that ended, and according to Jeffrey Rosenberg, senior portfolio manager of the Systematic Alternatives ETF (IALT) at BlackRock, the world's largest asset manager, the breakdown in that trade has big implications for the market.

"Oil and treasury yields had this amazing correlation and in October it just stopped," Rosenberg said on the most recent CNBC "ETF Edge."

The relationship between oil and bond yields was featured in Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh's FOMC press conference on Wednesday, with Warsh noting how oil futures have continued to rise, a factor the central bank must consider in its fight against inflation. But Rosenberg said what Warsh didn't talk about was "the most important takeaway."

"Treasuries and oil had an 80% correlation this year," Rosenberg said, which is "outsize" relative to normal correlations. "We've basically been trading off oil for good reason." "

What happened? Rosenberg says you can sum it up by looking to the deflated AI trade. As the market moved away from that oil-treasuries trading correlation in October, "It told us something important," he said. "In the market's mind, the geopolitical risk is secondary to AI risk," he said.

Over the past month — which became most painfully clear in Friday's Nasdaq market wipeout and, earlier the week, Google's biggest single-day decline in its history, wiping out half a trillion dollars in value — the AI trade experienced a retracement. Bond yields went lower, Rosenberg says, because the market is equating weakness in the AI trade as a broader weakness in the U.S. economy. And he said, last week's GDP report backed up that thinking. "AI is everything, everywhere, all the time," he said. Both the growth for the economy on the investment side and the wealth effect on the consumption side are all AI, he said.

"Q3 GDP was a blowout [number] and all AI-related," Rosenberg said on the "ETF Edge" podcast extended conversation. He pointed to equipment investment increases and consumption well above expectations and both fueled by the AI wealth effect, which with the latter is even more notable given that real incomes have been coming down in the U.S.

Business investment of 12.7% in Q3 was the biggest increase outside the pandemic since 2011, led by a 37% rise in information processing equipment. Industrial equipment investment rose nearly 25%, which while not a straight AI trade, does partially reflect spending on data centers.

Warsh did not agree in his assessment of the economy at the FOMC press conference. "It is convenient to say that the strength of the economy is all about this new technology wave. In fact, if we look at the data, really including data from the last meeting until this, the capex story is not just robust, not just strong, but it is broadening. And so if you wanted to tell the story somehow that this is strength we're seeing only in narrow pockets, that's a much harder story to tell now than it would have been several quarters ago," he said.

It is true that market breadth, a measure of how many stocks within the S&P 500 were showing positive trading momentum, improved significantly in October after having reached a 2026 low by late September, and as defensive sectors of the stock market like healthcare and staples rallied. But Rosenberg says the conclusion that the broader October market came to is that any "wobble" in the AI trade is going to be seen as a wobble in the growth outlook for the U.S., and as disinflationary. And in a disinflationary environment, there is "only one thing that hedges," he said, and that is "the thing everyone wants to hate. ... the long end of the treasury curve."

"That's the really important lesson from last month of market performance," he said.

Despite this view, Rosenberg is cautious about suggesting that this dynamic makes bonds a safe bet. "The simple answer is it's not a return to normal, where normal was bonds hedging stocks," he said.

That is because of the conflicting forces on bonds. Oil price increases and the persistent level of inflation above target are not going away as challenges to fixed-income assets and the "efficacy," Rosenberg said, of fixed income as a hedge.

"There's too much inflation and that limits the ability of the Fed to provide accommodation, and we haven't gotten away from that," he said. "But what we've learned in the last month is if it's big enough, if the AI trend turns against the macroeconomy and inflationary trends, it can bring about a wholesale change in that direction that's disinflationary and bonds may react like they did in October," he said.

Still, he says investors should not bet the trade will be one-directional or come without a potential cost. Investors need to weigh the potential diversification benefit from bonds based on the October action versus a scenario in which AI does not unravel the economy. And the longer the AI boom persists, coupled with the broader fiscal challenges in the U.S., "bonds will be challenged," he said.

The AI trade itself is a very big piece of the bond market, and that showed up over the past week in a major way between both the Google earnings decline and Microsoft coming back into the public bond market. "It was a surprise to the markets. It hadn't been back in close to a decade," Rosenberg said.

As the capex needs of the tech giants begin to overwhelm their free cash flow more regularly, the public bond markets are coming to terms with an expected flood of issuance. "When the bonds markets, the public bond markets, look at where it is all coming from ... it's a huge marginal issuer, and its increasingly all coming again and again," he said.

The hyperscalers are reaching the limits of the financing that bank loan and private credit markets can offer and while Rosenberg stressed that a $40 trillion to $60 trillion global bond market can absorb an expected $4 trillion in AI debt over the next four years, "the market is not unlimited in capacity."

Rosenberg said it's a good thing that more of the hyperscale debt is being pushed into the public bond market because it means there will be more transparency in the form of bond prices and CDS spreads. While spreads on Microsoft credit default swaps are currently trading near a five-year high, roughly 55, they are still a world away from Oracle CDS spreads, by comparison, which are closer to 300. That presents another trading opportunity for investors who may not want to buy bonds outright given the inflationary environment and fiscal challenges in the U.S., but may still be looking for ways to hedge bets on the AI boom.

"That old tool from the Great Financial Crisis," he said. "Short it. You can trade two ways in [public] credit markets and you can't do that in bank loans and private credit. But once they start going in public markets, you can do that."

Rosenberg said a 12% down day in Google is "not even on the chart" historically. "So it's moving into a different zip code, if you will, of market pricing and market volatility and market indigestion. It's not the end of the world, it's just a different world of the AI buildout," he said, with the greater transparency in the public bond market giving investors more ability to trade "the emotional swings between fear and greed," he said.

What the CDS market is telling investors with the large-cap hyperscaler names, according to Rosenberg, is not that the prospect of default is high, but that as all the companies try to access more debt financing, the capacity of the banks and financial intermediaries to find counter-parties for the risk is becoming more limited.

"Think about financial markets and these big investment booms. They don't move in one direction. They ratchet up, reset." But with the AI boom, it's only been "the positive part," Rosenberg said. "It's still an incredibly positive story, but you get a little of the balance with the AI pessimism."
