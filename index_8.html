#649. United Airlines resolves system outage that affected departing flights

United Airlines on Monday said it had resolved a "widespread system outage" that briefly affected departing flights.

"We have resolved a technology issue that delayed flight departures," the airline said in a statement. "While some flights were delayed, there was no impact on flights in progress, or on United Express flights.

A United spokesperson said there was no indication that the outage was a cyberattack.

The Air Line Pilots Association, which represents pilots for United and other carriers, said in an earlier message to members seen by CNBC that the operational outage was "affecting multiple pilot facing applications" and that United could not "provide outbound flights with final weights."

United's outage came three days after a nationwide ground stop halted departures across the country for more than four hours, snarling travel and leading to widespread delays and cancellations over the weekend.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy blamed the issues that led to Friday's ground stop on a router in Arkansas that overwhelmed systems that feed radar and other data for air traffic control. Federal Aviation Administration Administrator Bryan Bedford said the upgrades were part of "routine" maintenance to switch over from copper wires to fiber optic.

There were significantly fewer flight delays and cancellations Monday than had been reported Sunday, according to FlightAware data.

There was no indication that United's outage Monday was related to the issues that caused Friday's ground stop.

— CNBC's Leslie Josephs and Phil LeBeau contributed to this report.

#591. A transcription firm used by police, prosecutors, and tech giants exposed customer data online

A transcription company that caters to law enforcement, government departments, and Silicon Valley tech giants has resolved a security lapse after leaving a large amount of internal data, including customer transcriptions and their source materials, exposed to the web.

The data security lapse at San Francisco-based Tigerfish exposed transcriptions of police interviews, body-cam footage, and customers’ raw audio and video footage. The exposed data also included invoices of the company’s customers, which include police departments and prosecutors’ offices.

According to Anurag Sen, a good faith security researcher who found the data, the files were located on an Amazon-hosted (S3) cloud server that was openly listed and accessible to anyone via an easy-to-guess web address — no passwords needed.

The security lapse underscores how simple flaws or misconfigurations can result in sensitive information getting exposed online, especially as AI makes it easier to uncover weaknesses that can put companies and their data at risk.

Sen discovered the exposed data online in October and alerted the company to the issue, but did not hear back. Sen later provided details to TechCrunch in an effort to flag the incident with the company for resolution.

Sen, 30, is no stranger to TechCrunch. For the past decade, he has searched for sensitive datasets exposed online with the goal of notifying the companies and organizations that their information is open to the internet. Like many security researchers, much of his efforts are driven by a duty to secure the internet, and he describes his work as an “unpaid air-traffic controller.”

From his island home in Indonesia, Sen has uncovered sensitive U.S. military emails spilling from a compartmentalized Department of Defense cloud server; databases exposing reams of customer records; the inner workings of a country’s national license plate surveillance system; and public systems that inadvertently sent out streams of SMS codes used by people around the world to log into their online accounts.

Given Sen’s propensity for finding often highly confidential information online, we verified the source of the leak and the data, and contacted Tigerfish.

Soon after making contact, Tigerfish secured its exposed S3 cloud bucket.

“We’ve blocked all public access to the bucket you identified, including directory listings and individual files,” Tigerfish president, Adam Goldberg, said in an email. “We’ve also verified that all of our other S3 buckets are blocked to public access.”

“We’re continuing to review and strengthen our storage security more broadly,” added Goldberg.

The company said that Sen’s email disclosing the data leak was caught in its spam filter.

It’s unclear if the company has notified its customers. Tigerfish did not respond to a follow-up email.

Discoveries of exposed datasets have consumed a large part of Sen’s early cybersecurity career so far. He scours the web daily using automated scanners and specialist search engines to identify databases, cloud storage, or other sensitive information that likely shouldn’t be left unprotected online.

The modern web runs on absolute trust, he said, and operates under the notion that the world’s data is kept secure. But he described how his findings reveal the reality of how “fragile” the internet is behind the scenes.

Despite the recent focus on how AI is accelerating people’s ability to discover vulnerabilities, Sen said that many of the data leaks and exposures that he finds come down to basic human errors.

“An engineer spinning up a test cloud database on a Friday, disabling authentication to test an API, and forgetting to shut the door before heading into the weekend,” was just one example, he recounted.

Sen’s findings over the years highlight the ongoing problems caused by such security mistakes, just as the new wave of vibe-coding and AI-generated development is creating new security problems due to insecure and unchecked code deployed to the web.

Similar to Amazon’s recent efforts to make it difficult to make S3 buckets public without going through clear multistep warnings, newer vibe-coding platforms like Lovable and Supabase have also made changes to their platforms to prevent user mistakes.

“Everyone often fears shadowy hackers,” Sen said. But he argues that the real crisis is leaving sensitive systems and databases “wide open to the public internet without a password.”

#586. Bending Spoons buys Mailchimp for $3.68B

Bending Spoons has bought up another distressed internet company. This time, it’s the email marketing firm Mailchimp, bought for $3.68 billion from the tax-preparation giant Intuit.

Intuit acquired the company in 2021 for roughly $12 billion, so it’s taking quite a loss — and, perhaps, Bending Spoons is getting quite a bargain. At the time of the sale to Intuit, that acquisition caused headlines because Mailchimp employees reportedly received no equity, so no share of the sale proceeds, because the founders had allegedly promised them they didn’t need it as they would never sell.

Flash forward to now, with Intuit hit hard by the Death Star to SaaS trade — aka AI — with the company’s shares worth less than half what they were a year ago. The company told Reuters that the sale will let it sharpen its focus on AI tools for small businesses, and the new cash will be used to repurchase shares.

Bending Spoons describes the transaction as an all-cash deal, although that’s not quite true: As part of the same package, Intuit is buying $368 million in newly minted shares of Bending Spoons, so the net effect is a split of 90% cash to 10% stock.

Still, the transaction is a major pickup for Bending Spoons, which has become a kind of clearinghouse for late-stage web properties, with its game plan to hold them long term. In just the last year, the company has snapped up AOL, Eventbrite, Airtable, and Miro. Previous acquisitions include older stalwarts like Evernote, Meetup, and Vimeo. In each case, Bending Spoons integrated the products into centralized engineering and revenue systems, resulting in significant cost savings while often improving the available support and product resources.

“We want to place ourselves as an operator that takes beloved brands and makes them much better,” Bending Spoons co-founder and chief product officer, Matteo Danieli, told TechCrunch in July.

#584. Disney beats fourth-quarter estimates, targets $3 billion in savings through cost-cutting efforts

Disney on Tuesday reported quarterly earnings and revenue that exceeded analysts' expectations and announced it's targeting $3 billion in cost savings by fiscal 2029.

Those cost-cutting efforts include several rounds of layoffs announced in recent months, Disney's chief financial officer, Hugh Johnston, told CNBC. The most recent round, which seeks to reduce costs by up to 20%, was reported earlier this month.

Future cost-saving measures will include reductions in administrative and other general expenses, Johnston said, while adding, "We do expect more layoffs."

Disney CEO Josh D'Amaro told investors on the company's earnings call that the job cuts are "not easy."

"They affect people who care deeply about Disney and have contributed so much to this company," he said. "At the same time, these steps are important if we want to be a company that moves faster, adapts to a rapidly changing environment, and keeps investing in the content and technology that will drive our growth."

The cuts will help Disney fund its $26 billion in projected content spend for fiscal 2027, a 9% increase from fiscal 2026.

The entertainment giant is also forecasting double-digit growth in earnings per share in the next fiscal year, as well as segment operating income growth of around 8%.

Disney noted that the majority of that growth will be concentrated in the first half of the year due to the theatrical debut of "Avengers: Doomsday" and a boost from the Super Bowl, which will air on Disney's ESPN and ABC networks.

"The growth is really going to come in the first half of this year," Johnston said. "That's the opposite of what we saw in '26, where the growth was backloaded."

The company will also increase its annual dividend to $2 a share, a 33% increase from the previously declared dividend. It will be payable in two installments of $1 per share, one in January and one in July.

Here's how Disney performed in its fiscal fourth quarter ended Oct. 3, compared with what Wall Street expected, according to LSEG:

Disney reported net income for the fiscal fourth quarter of $2.61 billion, or $1.51 per share, compared with $1.31 billion, or 73 cents per share, during the same period the year prior.

Adjusting for one-time items, including severance and other restructuring charges as well as the value of intangible assets, Disney reported earnings per share of $1.77.

The company's revenue for the quarter was 16% higher year over year, and results were once again lifted by its parks and streaming divisions.

Disney's experiences segment, which includes its theme parks and cruises, reported nearly $10.11 billion in revenue, up 15% year over year. Operating income for the unit increased 20% to $2.25 billion.

The division is already a top performer for the company, but it benefited in the fiscal fourth quarter because of an extra calendar week compared with the previous fiscal year.

While international attendance continues to lag at Disney's domestic parks, it is being offset by domestic attendance, especially at the Walt Disney World resort in Orlando, Florida, Johnston said.

Growth at Disneyland Paris was driven by recent investments including an expansion area called World of Frozen. Attendance at the Hong Kong and Shanghai parks remained soft because of broad economic challenges in those regions, according to Disney.

Still, D'Amaro said he believes the growth in parks will be sustainable through next year, especially with new additions, including the "Monsters, Inc."-themed land "Monstropolis" opening at Walt Disney World in 2027.

"Q1 forward resort bookings at Walt Disney World are pacing up compared to the prior year," he said. "And new capacity and new experiences will start coming online in 2027. We keep investing to make the experience fresh, and what we see is that drives repeat visits."

The company expects operating income for the experiences segment to grow in the high-single digits in fiscal 2027. Growth is expected to be front-loaded as the company laps the launch of the Disney Adventure cruise ship, which set sail in March.

Over at its entertainment division, Disney saw revenue increase 17% to $11.89 billion. Operating income nearly doubled to $1.33 billion.

Revenue from streaming, specifically, rose 19% to $6.1 billion, Disney said.

The segment benefited from the extra week on the fiscal calendar as well as from growth in subscription and affiliate fees. Additionally, the company saw higher theatrical distribution revenue from titles like "Toy Story 5."

Disney said Tuesday it expects operating income for the entertainment division to grow in the high-single digits in the coming fiscal year.

The company's sports division saw revenue increase 16% in the fiscal fourth quarter, reaching $4.61 billion. Operating income for the segment surged more than 50% to $1.39 billion.

Subscription and affiliate fees fueled this growth alongside an increase in advertising revenue.

The company expects its sports division's operating income to grow by double digits in fiscal 2027. It noted that the fiscal first quarter of the year will see some declines because of the timing of College Football Playoff games and the New Year's window. However, the fiscal second quarter will benefit from the college championship as well as the broadcast of Super Bowl 61.

Disney's outlook for overall and segment results for fiscal 2027 excludes the impact of a 53rd week in fiscal 2026.

D'Amaro has made advancing technology a pillar of the company's growth strategy. In September, Disney hired Karandeep Anand in the newly created position of chief technology officer.

"Technology is central to how we connect fans to more of Disney and drive returns on our investment," D'Amaro said on the earnings call. "[Anand has] been energized by how much opportunity he sees to move faster and go further in product and technology. Done right, that means serving our fans even better, working more efficiently and being more innovative across every one of our businesses."

Disney announced a partnership with Adobe earlier this month to develop generative artificial intelligence models.

D'Amaro told investors that the team is exploring ways to use AI to make booking trips to Disney parks easier.

"There's actually a vacation planning tool that's in beta right now at Walt Disney World. We built that with our partners at Google, and I've had a chance to experiment with it myself," he said. "It'll be incredibly helpful for our guests who are booking trips. With these AI features, it can feel like you've got someone sitting right by your side, like a personal concierge, that plans and responds to the way that you want to actually book your trip to Walt Disney World."

The entertainment company has also expanded its short-form content, adding a vertical video channel within the Disney+ mobile app. The company had previously announced a partnership with TikTok to bring creator content to the streaming app.

The data center ecosystem currently depends on debt financing, with cloud providers borrowing to build out data centers and then paying back the loans with a share of the revenue.

Those deals typically use the chips as collateral — if the data centers default on their loans, the lenders can take possession of the chips and resell them. That backstop makes it cheaper for companies to finance their chips. Even older chips retain value — according to Compute Credit Index Research, some H100s are selling for about half of their original cost basis from 2023, while some H200 configurations are selling for the same price they did when introduced in 2024.

But you’re not going to repossess chips installed in a satellite orbiting 300 miles above Earth at 17,500 miles per hour. The Financial Times is reporting that lenders behind SpaceX’s $40 billion chip financing deal asked that none of the chips purchased be used for the company’s orbital data center plans. It’s not clear if they succeeded in getting SpaceX to agree with the provision.

That may not matter to the company in the near term — SpaceX’s ability to deploy compute at terrestrial ground stations has proven lucrative, and on the company’s most recent earnings call, CEO Elon Musk detailed how he was dispatching engineers from the company’s rocket team to accelerate that build-out by taking on challenges like casting turbine blades for generators at faster than existing suppliers.

However, open questions remain about how easy it will be to find land and power to support the data center build-out. Many potential projects face delays and local opposition, while markets wait to see the state of compute demand and the ability of its buyers to turn it into revenue.

Musk, meanwhile, began saying at the beginning of 2026 that he believes space will be the “cheapest” place to operate data centers in two to three years. That’s unlikely until SpaceX can fly its Starship rocket in a fully reusable configuration. In its most recent flight test, the company failed to recover the booster stage but did succeed in landing the orbital stage of the rocket back at its launch facility in Texas. It’s not clear, though, when the second stage will fly again or how expensive it will be to prepare it for a second trip to space.

Even with Starship, however, SpaceX — as well as startups like StarCloud, Cowboy Space Corporation, Sophia Space, Orbital, and others — will need to buy chips. If the already daunting economics of space data centers weren’t challenging enough, it looks like they’ll be spending more to buy them than their terrestrial counterparts.

That might be an advantage for Google, which is focused on deploying its own TPU chips in space through Project Suncatcher. SpaceX and Tesla are working together to build their own chip foundry, Terafab, in the U.S., but that project has yet to break ground and will require years and billions of dollars before it starts producing semiconductors at scale.

#578. Super-hot rock geothermal could end data center energy woes

Geothermal startups are in a race to see who can tap the deepest, hottest rocks. The winners could power the next phase of the grid, data centers and all.

The broader markets got their first taste of enhanced geothermal earlier this year when Fervo Energy went public. But this week was all about super-hot rock geothermal.

Geothermal has become a darling among investors, tech companies, and even the Trump administration. The basic technology has been around for decades, but advances in drilling techniques borrowed from the oil and gas industry has supercharged it. The U.S. has plenty of potential, too, with up to 57 terawatts of generating capacity, more than enough to power the next wave of AI data centers.

An emerging class of geothermal energy, known as super-hot rock, could help the industry unlock much of that potential.

The secret to super-hot rock geothermal is what happens to water at extreme depths. As it gets closer to the superheated core of the Earth, both temperature and pressure rise significantly. When they get high enough, ordinary water turns into a supercritical fluid — a sort of gaseous-liquid state that can carry tremendous amounts of energy. A single super-hot rock well could generate 15 megawatts of electricity or more.

Super-hot rock geothermal is inching closer to commercial reality. Quaise Energy said on Tuesday that it had landed Google as a customer. The hyperscaler said it would pay more than $500 million to secure 50 megawatts of power from a Quaise project in central Oregon, which will tap into searing heat three miles deep.

Google is paying a premium for the amount of power Quaise will deliver, but that’s in line with the company’s strategy of getting in early with potential suppliers. For instance, it was an investor in and an early customer of Fervo.

Meanwhile, on Monday, Khosla-backed Mazama Energy announced it had figured out how to keep its drill heads cool as they tap into super-hot rocks. And last week, XGS Energy was given a grant by the Department of Energy to develop and refine materials, well designs, and models to improve reliability at depths where super-hot rock exists.

XGS is dipping its toes in the super-hot waters, while Mazama and Quaise have made it their core focus, though they’re taking very different approaches.

Quaise’s technology sounds like science fiction: Starting at about 2.5 miles deep, the company will swap out its conventional drill bit for one powered by a gyrotron, bombarding rock with millimeter-wave radiation and turning it into ash. The hope is that the technology can make drilling under super-hot conditions cost-effective, something that’s challenging with traditional metal drill bits, which wear out faster at those temperatures.

Mazama, on the other hand, thinks that traditional drill bits will work just fine if they can be kept cool.

The startup’s newly announced technology, Carbon Chill, uses carbon dioxide in place of water-based drilling “mud” to keep the bit cool. At those depths and temperatures, the carbon dioxide turns supercritical, allowing it to hold significant amounts of energy. Just above the drill bit, the CO2 flows through a valve that looks similar to a rocket nozzle. As the gas flows through the valve and expands on the other end, the expansion rapidly cools it, allowing it to absorb significant amounts of heat. The CO2 also removes drill cuttings on its trip to the surface, where it’s filtered and reused.

In both cases, Quaise and Mazama are betting that their technology could leapfrog today’s state-of-the-art commercial geothermal power plants. By going deeper and hotter, they’re hoping to get substantially more power out of each well. Given that drilling accounts for about a quarter to a third of a geothermal power plant’s total costs, drilling fewer wells could make a project more competitive.

Investors have been betting that the technologies will soon prove themselves. Quaise has raised more than $270 million from investors, including Prelude Ventures, Collaborative Fund, and Engine Ventures, with Vinod Khosla stepping in as an angel investor, according to PitchBook. Mazama has raised $171 million from investors, including Khosla Ventures, Doerr Capital, and Bill Gates.

As with any new technology, there’s some risk that the new techniques won’t pay off. But early results suggest they will — Mazama drilled its second well 80% faster than its first, and its CO2-cooling tech cut drilling time another 20%, co-founder and CEO Sriram Vasantharajan told TechCrunch.

That, plus Google’s vote of confidence in Quaise, suggests the race to super-hot rock geothermal is, well, heating up.

#575. AI claimed a solution to the 167-year-old Riemann hypothesis — and a human did, too

It’s a big week for Levent Alpöge: The mathematician saw his employer, Anthropic, become the first publicly traded frontier AI lab. He also appears to have solved one of the most important problems in mathematics.

“The precise timing is horrible,” he wrote in an essay announcing his findings and describing how he messaged Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei last Sunday night to ask how to announce that he had found a Lean-compilable solution to the Riemann hypothesis, which dates back to 1859 and is among the Millennium Prize math problems.

The hypothesis conjectures that there is a hidden structure underlying the appearance of prime numbers. If the claimed result holds true, we’re entering sublime-mysteries-of-the-universe territory.

Maybe the best way to understand how exciting this is to the mathematics community is to watch the experts puzzling through the result in a chat on Zulip. Mathematicians like Chris Birkbeck, a number theorist at the University of East Anglia, are posting thoughts like this …

The cohomology bound is proved by what looks like an automorphy-lifting argument. It genuinely uses the existence of Galois representations attached to regular algebraic, polarized cuspidal automorphic representations of GL_n over CM fields, along with base change, Jacquet–Shalika, Clozel’s purity, Bernstein–Zelevinsky, the main theorem of CM, Deligne–Ribet and more.

For my part, I tapped out of mathematics at college calculus, so I can’t help decode exactly what that means.

But that comment was followed by 20 readers reacting 🤯.

It will be a while before the mathematics community feels like it has a handle on the proof, but most appear confident that the formalized Lean result will hold. Lean is an an open-source coding language used to express mathematical ideas as code, allowing proofs to be formally verified. Several participants in the Zulip chat, including MIT mathematician Andrew Sutherland, say they have successfully confirmed that Alpöge’s solution can be compiled successfully.

Alpöge appeared in his essay to feel a bit guilty about releasing the proof without a truly human-intelligible explanation, but his obsession with the problem since childhood made him feel he “could not live” if he waited to release the finding.

Alpöge described a “stream-of-consciousness jaunt through the experience of finding all the pieces of the argument through incredible confusion with Claude” with more than a hint of AI euphoria, describing how the model “flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line.”

However, for all that action to mean anything, we’ll have to wait for humans to be able to understand the result and explain it to each other, something Alpöge has committed to pursue.

Alpöge and a collaborator, NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster, also released another important proof related to the Navier-Stokes equations, problems that helped kick off the race between frontier labs and mathematicians to solve outstanding problems this summer.

Meanwhile, Ravi Vakil, a Stanford professor and the president of the American Mathematics Association, posted a proof of the General Riemann hypothesis written by Stanford postdoc Alessandro Masullo, noting that “it is all human, with no AI. The hope was to bring Alessandro to Stanford for a year to polish the writing and then announce it after running it by enough people. Now it seems best to put out the current draft.”

Without being expressed in Lean, it is too early to say whether the proof checks out, much less if anyone understands it.

In a brief introduction to his proof, which he said is the result of 12 years of work, Masullo wrote that “if any message at all this work is meant to send to the mathematician who feels discouraged by the pace and tone of the current climate, it is this: we are still here. We are very much alive. We are not going anywhere.”

#570. Daniel Ek backs AI researcher’s fusion power startup. Here’s what they’re building.

Spotify founder Daniel Ek is backing Europa Fusion, a stealthy fusion power startup founded by an AI researcher who is taking a different approach to developing a fusion power plant.

The U.K.-based startup was founded in February, though sources told TechCrunch that the founder, Nantas Nardelli, has been working on the idea for longer. The people asked not to be identified because the information isn’t public. Bloomberg first reported Ek’s backing of Europa Fusion.

Nardelli has extensive experience in the AI world, having previously worked at Google DeepMind, Facebook AI Research, and Microsoft Research Cambridge. Recently, he was head of machine learning at Pacific Fusion, the fusion power startup based in Fremont, California.

Pacific Fusion recently broke ground on a demonstration facility in New Mexico. Like most fusion startups of its size, Pacific Fusion leans heavily on physical devices to test its theories and improve its simulations.

Nardelli apparently didn’t agree with the focus on hardware, instead thinking that fusion startups should invest in a more simulation- and data-heavy approach, a source familiar with his thinking told TechCrunch.

Like Pacific Fusion, Europa Fusion will pursue a pulsed-power approach to fusion. Pulsed-power reactors use massive, perfectly timed surges of electrical current to compress fuel targets until the atoms inside fuse and release energy.

Fusion power has the potential to be a significant energy source in the coming decades, but to date, no one has built a power plant capable of supplying electricity to the grid. If fusion power becomes commercially viable, though, it could possibly be a significant source of carbon-free power that draws on nearly limitless fuel supplies. The technology also has the potential to alleviate concerns about AI’s energy consumption.

Fusion’s ability to generate electricity without exacerbating climate change apparently drove Nardelli’s pivot from AI.

“My original background is in electronics and control engineering,” he wrote on his website. “I used to build robots for a living. One day I’ll be back to that, but it’s time to fix climate change for good.”

#567. Experts warn: Nearly everyone in AI is susceptible to a honey-trap scheme right now

Even as the fall-out for Nvidia over Jensen Huang’s alleged honey-trap scandal is still unfolding, Silicon Valley has yet to reckon with a new reality: many more people among them are at risk.

“Nobody briefs founders on counterintelligence,” Aaron Brown, a former senior CIA officer and current founder of the software company Lumbra, told TechCrunch.

That’s because historically honey traps have been associated with politicians, the military, and national intelligence — the people “behind clearances and a workforce trained to expect an approach,” from spies and other attackers.

But now many of the most valuable secrets “sit inside companies building chips, models, and infrastructure,” Brown says, adding that a spy “wants what the CEO can touch. The roadmap, the export decisions, the customer list, who gets the technology. The CEO is the shortest path to all of it.”

Huang has been accused of falling for just such a honey trap by having an affair with Alice Huang. She’s a former executive of a Singapore neocloud who’s been accused of being a Chinese spy and of smuggling billions of dollars’ worth of restricted Nvidia chips into China.

After the Huang allegations broke, jokes flooded the internet about which tech CEO will be next, but it’s not just CEOs and founders who are at risk. The WSJ reported that OpenAI started warning its employees about outsiders trying to cozy up to them in search of company secrets. Anyone with access to sensitive information — from board members to executive assistants — can be the target of a honey trap.

And typically, the attackers aren’t lovers. Friendship is actually the oldest honey-trap scheme around, Brown said.

Honey traps gain access to their targets as investors, customers, recruiters, and consultancies. Their requests look legitimate on paper and don’t need their targets to fall in love. “They need you to feel comfortable talking,” Brown said. “They last longer because nobody’s looking.”

What’s interesting, too, is the irony. So many tech founders are moving to automate and digitize so much of human life and yet remain vulnerable to the oldest bug around, a person who preys on the human need to be understood by others.

“Why fight through a firewall when you can get close to the person who can open doors, influence decisions, or make requests nobody wants to question?” Perry Carpenter, a criminologist and chief deception strategist at KnowBe4, told TechCrunch.

Once targets realize they have been compromised, the threat can escalate: attackers now have a means of blackmail.

Another irony: AI has made honey-trapping much easier and cheaper. Planning a scheme used to take months or years of research, crafting a persona, knowing the target, and executing a plan. These days, it’s easy to use AI to research a target, generate fake social profiles, fake backgrounds, even fake voice calls.

“AI can help sustain an entire fictional backstory,” Carpenter said. “The technology helps set the stage, but the deception ultimately plays out through human relationships.”

The AI industry “became a national security industry faster than its culture caught up,” Brown said.

“Part of being honey-trapped is not knowing,” he added. “It doesn’t feel like manipulation. It feels genuine, even lucky.”

Carpenter said a good first step is looking for unusual patterns. Note, for instance, “coincidental encounters that happen a little too often,” he said. There are also obvious signs, like persistent curiosity about work and colleagues, or requesting secrecy about meeting up, and pressing for introductions or information.

Bigger red flags include if someone’s requests are getting bigger, pushing the boundaries of what you usually would be uncomfortable doing. “A simple gut check,” Brown said. “Would this person still be around if you lost your job tomorrow?”

It also helps to try and understand when emotions might be influencing a decision. Carpenter suggests putting up a clear boundary between one’s personal and professional life. Within companies, it also helps to “eliminate executive exceptionalism,” he said, the idea that the rules don’t apply to people in power at the company.

Brown added that company culture plays another role, too: Are employees comfortable with raising concerns, challenging unusual requests, and escalating possible threats? “One of the most dangerous security cultures is one where nobody feels comfortable questioning the CEO,” Carpenter said.

“Founders need to take risks, and they need to explore business opportunities,” Brown said. “Paranoia doesn’t help. Recognizing you’re a target does.”

#562. Startup sues Apple in India over tap-to-share contacts feature

Apple is being sued in India by a U.S.-based startup that alleges the iPhone maker’s NameDrop feature, which lets users share contact information by bringing devices close together, infringes its patented technology.

GG Technologies, the startup behind StayTouch, an app that lets users exchange contact details by bringing their phones close together, is seeking ₹15 billion (about $161.86 million) in damages and an injunction against Apple, per court documents reviewed by TechCrunch.

Santa Monica-headquartered GG Technologies filed the lawsuit in India in October after mediation proceedings initiated in April failed in September, the startup told TechCrunch. Pre-institution mediation is generally required before filing commercial lawsuits in India. The company also sued Apple in the U.S. in April over related patent infringement claims.

In its 781-page court filing in the Bombay High Court, GG Technologies alleged that Apple’s NameDrop infringes its Indian patent on technology that helps phones exchange contact details with nearby devices without requiring manual pairing. The company launched StayTouch in 2019, the same year it first sought patent protection for the technology in the U.S. It filed its Indian patent application in 2021 and received the patent in July 2024.

Apple introduced NameDrop as part of iOS 17 in June 2023. The feature debuted on iPhones later that year, with support for compatible Apple Watches arriving in October through watchOS 10.1.

“We are seeking an injunction to restrict the usage of the NameDrop feature which is native to iOS,” GG Technologies told TechCrunch. GG Technologies urged the court in its filing to restrict Apple from selling or importing iPhones and Apple Watches preloaded with NameDrop in India. The case has yet to be listed for a hearing.

In the U.S., the International Trade Commission opened an investigation into Apple’s alleged patent infringement in August, following a complaint from GG Technologies. The startup is seeking an order that could block imports of infringing Apple devices into the country, along with restrictions on their sale.

Founded in 2018 by Gaurav Goel, a graduate of the Indian Institute of Technology (IIT) Delhi and a former Goldman Sachs investment banker, GG Technologies developed StayTouch as an alternative to exchanging business cards. The app is available on iOS and Android.

Apple did not respond to a request for comment.

India has become increasingly important to Apple, both as a fast-growing consumer market and a major manufacturing hub outside China. The Cupertino company has expanded its retail presence in the South Asian nation, where iPhones still account for less than 10% of smartphone shipments, giving considerable room for growth.

#558. Alaska, your next governor is a Wikipedia nerd

We have arrived at a moment in American politics when people who are young enough to have grown up on the internet are being elected to office. That means there’s a lot more snooping into politicians’ online pasts to come. This month’s elections were no exception.

Earlier this month, 37-year-old Democrat Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins won the election for governor of Alaska, making him the first person to hold the office who was born in Alaska after it became a state in 1959. But when people went looking for more information about the Alaskan governor-elect, they didn’t find ill-advised tweets or compromising photos — they found out that he used to be an extremely active Wikipedia editor.

On a Reddit page dedicated to discussing Wikipedia, someone posted that they had found what seemed very likely to be Kreiss-Tomkins’ account — a page for a user named Jonathan.s.kt from Sitka, Alaska who went to Yale, liked mountaineering, and made 13,358 Wikipedia edits between 2005 and 2011. The governor-elect is also from Alaska, went to Yale, and climbs mountains.

Wikipedia editors are an impressive community: They keep one of the most important sources of information online (mostly) clean and accurate. This only makes Kreiss-Tomkins look better as a legislator. Check this guy out! A dedicated public servant to the global constituency of Wikipedia! But a moderator on the Wikipedia subreddit pointed out that we cannot be 100% certain that this account is his.

“The odds that somebody would spend six years creating articles about Sitka, Alaska, Yale, and uploading PD photos of mountains, all to deceive people into thinking they were a disenchanted Howard Dean volunteer who was from Sitka, attended Yale, and was a mountaineer, is effectively zero. Plus they uploaded a photo of themself that looks like every other photo of JKT, minus the bald part,” one commenter replied.

Fair enough. If someone created a Wikipedia account pretending to be some guy in Alaska, made over 13,000 Wikipedia edits, and then waited two decades until that guy became the governor of America’s 49th state in order to sabotage him by making him look like a conscientious denizen of the web, kudos to them. But I’m a journalist. I have literal nightmares about getting stuff wrong. If you’re not careful, it’s easy to get duped.

#554. Is selling an AI version of your face to this microdrama startup the future of Hollywood?

For the most part, the content offered by the microdrama startup Shortical is indistinguishable from the rest of its industry. The company produces soapy, melodramatic storylines — almost always risqué romances — that, in bite-sized two-minute episodes, compel viewers to stay glued to their phones so as not to miss the latest twist in a tawdry, cliff-hanger-happy saga.

The primary way that Shortical — which was founded last January — makes money is through subscriptions. Typically, the first 7-10 episodes of a micro-drama are free. After that, if the user wants to finish the series, they have to pay a small monthly fee which unlocks access to all of the site’s content.

Over the past year, the company has churned out a gush of popular live-action romances like “Crossing the Line with My Neighbor,” “Accidentally Married My Boss,” and “Spoiled by My Billionaire Sugar Daddy.”

Lately, however, the company’s homepage is covered in AI-generated content. The reason isn’t exactly hard to figure out: the production overhead for AI content is substantially less than live-action. The profit potential, meanwhile, is substantially higher, if you can draw an audience.

Its latest microdrama premiered in Los Angeles last week. The series, called “Inevitable,” stars Aki Avni, an Israeli film and television star who has been visible in American TV shows for years (he had a stint on Fox’s 9/11-era thriller “24” years ago) but is now trying something largely unheard of in modern Hollywood: licensing his face to Shortical for the sake of its AI content.

The project, which is wholly AI, used Avni’s face to create a digital avatar of him. In “Inevitable,” he plays himself – in a very meta plot in which he — the actor Aki Avni — licenses his face to a film studio, after which thriller-like antics ensue.

This is Shortical’s first foray into such a licensing deal, but it likely won’t be their last.

In a phone interview, Avni told TechCrunch that he found the project both nerveracking and exciting. “Look, it’s a new era for artists,” the actor said. “AI is a new kind of art. It’s a new format. It’s always great to be the pioneer in something.”

Guy Shimoni, the CEO of Shortical, emphasized the creative potential of the new tool. “One of the things that AI does is it enables smaller teams to be able to produce these movie-scale productions,” Shimoni said. “When we were doing mobile-first live action, we were limited by budget, but now we can serve them with epic fantasies. We have kingdoms and dragons. We have superheroes. We have werewolves.”

But AI-generated romances involving werewolves — who, exactly, is watching this kind of thing?

Apparently, a lot of people. While China boasts the biggest microdrama audience globally, Shimoni says that most of his platform’s viewers are actually in the U.S. The industry’s viewer demographic skews heavily female. Many viewers may watch them to wind down at night, getting minor dopamine hits as they drift off to sleep. Many of the AI-generated programs that already exist on the company’s website appear to have millions of views.

Now, Shortical is betting on the creation of a new generation of creators — those who, like Avni, are willing to surrender their likenesses for compensation. “LICENSE YOUR FACE” Shortical’s website advertises, explaining that interested parties can submit their social media handles to the company, which will then decide whether to offer them “a contract setting out the terms” of using their persona in the company’s AI-generated content.

A company rep told TechCrunch that this “offering is still in its early days and hasn’t yet resulted in any signed contracts.” They added: “The series with Aki is the first time Shortical has used a real person’s likeness, and it was done through a direct agreement with him rather than through the platform. Shortical hopes the series will draw more attention to face licensing and plans to promote the offering more actively following this launch.”

Much of this trend has already taken hold in China where the microdrama industry originated, where AI-generated content is big, and where face-licensing is already a trend. Although the trend may not be a particularly profitable one for creators. A recent report in Rest of World found that Microdrama firms were paying people “between US$15 to US$700 to license their facial likenesses.”

How much money is Shortical offering for its face-licensing deals? The company isn’t saying. As for if Shortical will offer royalty-based compensation for creators — like the residual system in television, “It’s a case by case [basis],” Shimoni answers vaguely.

A Shortical representative further explained to TechCrunch that the company didn’t want to “share a specific figure” for Avni’s particular contract. However, the person said, he came “on as a partner in the project, and his compensation was in line with industry standards for a comparable non-AI acting role.”

For other actors, however, the deals may be quite different. Plus, many creatives are understandably unsettled by signs of automation creeping into their industry. Until recently, microdramas seemed like a safe, entry-level gateway into the entertainment industry, where young actors could gain experience on sets and hone their craft. Now, companies are downsizing and producing content that doesn’t even require real actors.

Jennifer Cooper, a longtime fan of the industry and founder of the consultancy firm Vertical Drama Love, notes that there’s a lot of distrust of AI — both from audiences and within the microdrama industry itself.

“Everyone’s been impacted by AI,” she said. “A lot of us have either lost work to AI or can see that it’s devastated this industry — [taking microdramas] from something that was a rare thing in the world, that was in growth, and providing work, particularly for young people…[and now] you know, it’s been decimated.”

Questions of consent with AI likenesses have also dogged the industry (some creators claim they have had their likenesses stolen), which is something that Shortical is obviously trying to address with its new face-licensing endeavor.

Yet these licensing deals could also help to gig-ify the industry, creating a scenario in which creators team up with a distributor for the chance to platform their content (or their face), then receive payouts based on the audience engagement they’re allowed to generate. Shortical recently partnered with a company called Payouts.com, which describes itself as a “payout infrastructure platform powering creator and gig economy disbursements at scale.” The plan is to create a system by which creatives can seamlessly interact with Shortical’s platform and get paid.

Cooper believes the push towards AI in the industry is driven by broader financial trends. Most of the microdrama business is led by startups, and VCs love to hear a growth game plan involving automation. “For anyone to get investment at the moment in microdramas, they’ve got to mention the word AI,” she says. “It’s like AI equals gold in everyone’s eyes.”

Despite all the investment in this space, Cooper seems skeptical that there is actually a huge audience for this kind of content. “Some of it does get views. A lot of it doesn’t,” she said, of the AI content. “I think it’s being pushed on an audience more than there is an audience demand or wish for it.”

#550. Here are the top AI agents that can live in your text messages

Rather than downloading another app, a growing number of agents can simply be texted like an ordinary person.

You text it what you need, and it can remember context, connect to the apps and services you already use, and complete tasks on your behalf. That can mean scheduling an appointment, organizing a calendar, researching a trip, sending an email, making a reservation, shopping online, or reminding you about something days later.

While Instinct is one of the buzziest AI agents at present, thanks to its $10 billion valuation after its latest funding round of $1 billion, there are many others also making a play for this space.

Below are the most notable options so far, from general-purpose personal assistants to agents designed for families, travel, and work.

Ask Proxy’s biggest differentiator is that it “thinks like a woman,” meaning it aims to anticipate your needs before you even ask, the company explains on its website.

It works like a chatbot you can text like a friend, whether you need gift ideas, help planning an event, or recommendations for things to do that weekend. You can ask it to add important dates to your calendar, set reminders, or help with everyday decisions. You can also send it photos, like an empty table, to figure out how to decorate the table for a dinner party.

Unlike a typical chatbot, Ask Proxy also takes a proactive approach. It can reach out when it notices something you might forget or when it comes across a new activity, restaurant, or experience you might enjoy.

There’s also a work-focused version, which the company explains “gets employees back to work faster, while keeping personal data off company systems.”

The personal version costs $1 per day. For businesses, pricing starts at $15 per employee per month for large companies and $23 per employee per month for small teams with fewer than 49 employees.

Ask Proxy works over SMS. You can text “start” to 820-732-2625.

Caddy is an AI assistant that turns the information scattered across your phone into things you can actually act on. It lives in iMessage for iPhone users and RCS for Android users, so there’s no separate app or inbox to constantly check.

For instance, an email contains an appointment that needs to be added to your calendar or a friend sends a list of things to pick up. Instead of worrying about all the details getting buried, Caddy connects to your calendars and conversations, then identifies things that may require action. It can add events to your calendar, set reminders, keep track of follow-ups, and even do research for you.

Caddy has been available in public beta since April 2026.

Comma is designed to manage tasks across both work and everyday life. Comma carries tasks through to completion, checking its own work and determining when a task is finished.

Completed work is surfaced for review when a decision or approval is needed. It also proactively notifies users and recommends the next steps.

It can work across a user’s browser, computer, and files, while remaining accessible remotely through messaging apps such as Signal, Telegram, and WeChat. Comma maintains context and memory across devices and conversations, and can deliver daily briefings, flag urgent matters, and summarize recorded calls.

Fambot is an AI-powered “chief of staff” for families, designed to bring together the many moving parts of family life, including school communications, sports activities, meal planning, calendars, and other day-to-day responsibilities, and turn them into an organized plan.

The service is designed to work across both an app and text messages. Fambot is available on iOS devices, Android devices, and the web, and families can also interact with it directly over SMS. Every night, Fambot automatically sends a summary of the following day, including upcoming events, to-dos, and details such as school uniform or packing requirements. Parents can reply to those messages to ask questions, check information, or make changes to their calendar.

Fambot currently connects to Gmail, Google Calendar, Outlook, and WhatsApp, with support for Apple Calendar planned for the future.

The company launched in beta in early September 2026 and has raised $3.5 million in pre-seed funding. The service is currently free during the beta period, but Fambot expects to eventually charge roughly the equivalent of a Netflix subscription.

Folk is designed to be an AI assistant you can reach through iMessage, WhatsApp, and Telegram. It can remember personal context, manage reminders, research topics, track flights, handle email, and perform real-world tasks such as restaurant reservations.

One distinction is that Folk runs on its own private cloud computer. It can also run code and execute multi-step tasks rather than merely responding conversationally.

Folk launched its beta in May 2026, and it’s available for free but also offers a Pro subscription for $8.33/month if you want unlimited tasks running in the background.

Haven distinguishes itself by presenting the concept of a “digital twin” rather than merely serving as a text-based AI assistant.

According to the company, Haven understands and adapts to your preferences, routines, and decision-making styles, creating a personalized memory graph based on your interactions. As the company describes, “A personal assistant knows what you asked it to do. Haven knows what you would do and how you would do it.”

Notably, the company envisions future features that customize your experiences across devices, like TV and car.

Currently, Haven is available for free in beta on iPhone and Android devices, and the web. You can also reach Haven by calling or texting (435) 260-6500.

Martin is a general-purpose personal assistant reachable through SMS as well as phone, WhatsApp, email, Slack, and its own iOS app.

It’s a bit on the pricier side, with subscriptions starting at $21 per month.

It includes a free basic option, but also offers different subscriptions based on family size, starting at $9.99 per month.

Orbits also has an AI assistant that is focused on the household.

It works through both the Orbits app and text messaging, and can bring together calendars, lists, and conversations while also carrying out tasks for you. That can include searching the web, making purchases, booking restaurants, changing appointments, and requesting quotes for household services.

There’s a free plan available. It works over iMessage, RCS, and Telegram.

Poke launched in March 2026 as a general-purpose assistant that you can reach through text messaging. It can handle tasks involving calendars, planning, health and fitness, smart-home controls, photos, and other everyday activities.

#533. Can a TV be too large? An investigation

For the past couple of years, I’ve wanted to upgrade my home entertainment situation from the humble trappings of my long sufficient 55-inch TCL smart TV to the model much coveted by cinephiles the world over: the OLED.

The organic light-emitting diode is a specific monitor technology that has really changed the TV industry. Unlike an LED TV, which creates images by shining a backlight through a panel of pixels, OLEDs leverage millions of self-lighting pixels, which provides significantly better pixel control, and can generate a much more nuanced image with deeply vibrant colors and a more intense light and shadows.

They are also significantly more expensive, which is one reason that I’ve held off on investing in one for years. However, this year I couldn’t wait any longer. I finally sprang for an upgrade and, since I was already upgrading on image quality, I decided to upgrade the size, too: I bought a 65-inch LG evo C4, which is the company’s mid-range unit released in 2024.

At first, the experience was amazing. The television rendered colors and shadows wonderfully and, seeing as one of the goals with this purchase was to indulge my physical media habit, I began running through my collection of 4K Blu-ray discs with the madcap energy of a true fanatic.

However, things took a bit of an unexpected turn when, midway through “McCabe and Mrs. Miller,” I was suddenly overcome by a wave of nausea. I’m not particularly sensitive to motion sickness, but this sensation — which made it feel vaguely like I was going to hurl — persisted for several hours after it initially began. It was then replaced by a small tension headache.

I discovered that this is actually a somewhat common experience for new OLED adopters. Indeed, Reddit is rife with posts from new television owners who claim they feel like their big new toy is breaking their brain.

“I got the tv yesterday and still feel sick today,” one Redditor, who said they had recently purchased an LG C2, wrote. “Feel light headed and nauseous a day later. Thinking about taking it back. Is this normal? I’m scared to turn the thing on lol.”

Others blamed the sheer size of the screen. One guy, who bought an 85-inch TLC, claimed it had led his wife to the conclusion that “it’s too big and say that she feels sick watching. Is that a thing that a screen can be too big?”

There doesn’t seem to be a consensus on why this happens. Many Redditors blame certain motion-smoothing settings. However, my TV did not come with those settings enabled. Others blamed viewers for sitting too close to the screen — yet I was sitting a solid nine feet away, which is well within the recommended range for a 65-inch screen.

Finally, others have argued that the screen brightness could be agitating users’ eyes. I dialed down the brightness on mine to around 50 percent, although I had doubts as to whether this actually had anything to do with the feelings I was having.

In considering my nausea, one theory that sprang to mind was that it might just be my eyes’ inexperience with viewing such a big screen. The TV was so large that it often felt like my eye had to trace different parts of the screen to take in the whole picture and, if the scene involved significant motion (most movie scenes do, after all), it conjured the sensation of trying to stare too intently at something out the window of a moving car.

After a few days, however, my eyes finally adjusted. The nausea was gone. All that remained was an immense and beautiful home viewing experience that made every previous viewing experience pale by comparison.

Initial physiological reactions aside, the size of the 65-inch screen (and whether your living space actually has room for it) is still something that needs to be considered carefully by potential buyers. I recently moved into a place with a slightly bigger living room, which has given me the flexibility to install a bigger device. At my old apartment, however, this size of TV would have definitely felt like it was devouring the space.

Another slight frustration is that the C4 does not come with any detailed installation instructions. The screen (which is so wide it’s difficult to move without another person there to help you) comes detached from its base, and the user is just sort of expected to figure out how to effectively move and put together the two pieces. A quick YouTube video is all that is necessary to understand this process, but you have to be careful when moving and assembling the pieces so as not to scratch or damage the screen.

Once the whole thing is set up and assembled, the OLED is a truly beautiful thing to behold. The customization that’s possible, including advanced brightness and color control — and things like “Filmmaker Mode” (which claims to adjust each movie’s color and frame rate settings to those intended by the director) — make the user experience really powerful.

To sum up: The evo C4 is a really spectacular piece of equipment that has a lot to offer the avid home entertainment viewer — although there may be a bit of a learning curve before it makes your cinema dreams come true.

#525. Why data centers are at the center of the Big Tech backlash

In his new book “Hyperscale,” journalist Paris Marx investigates the impact of data centers and generative AI, taking readers inside the data centers themselves and explaining how computing infrastructure that goes back decades recently became a political landmine.

The book is well-timed, to put it mildly, as data center backlash seems to have been one of the motivators for the massive blue wave seen in the recent midterm elections. And those debates are likely to continue as Democrats wield more governmental power.

In an interview with TechCrunch, Marx described the data center buildout as “fundamentally unsustainable” and “completely out of line with a livable planet,” while also arguing that these facilities make for an obvious target amidst broader anger at the tech industry. After all, if a data center is being built in your community, “it’s very physical, it’s very material.”

“It doesn’t matter what side of the political spectrum that you’re on,“ Marx said. “You are going to feel the effects of that in your quality of life, in your power bill, potentially in your access to water, potentially in what you hear around your community, if these air conditioning units on the roofs of data centers are making a lot of noise.”

He also suggested that data center opposition is only the most visible piece of a wider movement targeting “the physical manifestations of the tech industry, […] whether it’s the opposition to Flock cameras rolling out across the United States, or the smart glasses that Meta is trying to put on people’s faces, or even the self-driving cars that Waymo is rolling out.”

During our conversation (which I’ve edited for length and clarity), Marx and I discussed data centers’ environmental impact, how tech companies are responding to community resistance, and what it’s actually like inside a data center.

I liked your description of trying to get access to the inside of a “hyperscale” data center and then eventually taking a tour [of one]. Could you talk a little bit more about what that experience was like? How did you get in? What surprised you when you actually got in?

Getting inside of a hyperscale data center was not the easiest task in the world. I spoke to some researchers who have been doing research on data centers for a number of years longer than I had, and they told me that before the generative AI moment, and before all the attention that has been put on data centers recently, that it was quite a bit easier to do so.

I sent emails to a number of companies asking if I could get inside, often didn’t get a response, other times got a no. I ended up, after visiting the outsides of some data centers in places like northern Virginia and Ireland and spots like that, I actually went down to New Zealand to go inside of a data center itself.  And the thing that struck me most was the level of security.

Obviously, you would expect that this is an infrastructure that is really securitized, because the data inside needs to be kept safe, obviously [they] have these big fences and all the security cameras around the data centers. But to go inside, I had to send my passport in advance. Then when I got there, they had to check the passport, and then as we were going through the data center itself, every single door in the data center was locked unless you had a key card to unlock it.

Then, when you get inside of the server room itself, where all this computer hardware is stored in tall racks that look like cabinets — you can imagine, in your mind, that this would be kind of a sinister location, with some eerie lighting or something like that. But in truth, it’s just quite utilitarian. It’s a warm room with some air conditioning blasting into the servers from vents that are nearby.

It was really loud as well, because you have all these fans running from all these servers, it was really kind of piercing. To be inside of this room, if you were going to be in there for a while, you’d probably want earplugs or something like that.

Before I read your book, I thought of data centers as something that’s important, but a little bit boring. How did you decide they were interesting and rich enough to sustain an entire book?

I felt the same. I was aware that data centers had been out there, but it was not something that I was paying particularly close attention to. But then there were a number of stories that of tipped me off to the fact that there might be more here, and that it was worth looking deeper into what was going on — the first being hearing about the water pressures created by data centers in Chile around the capital of Santiago, where companies like Google and Microsoft were building data centers, and communities were concerned about their access to water and started organizing around that.

And then hearing about how much electricity data centers were consuming in Ireland, to the point that now they consume more than 20% of the country’s electricity, and that was obviously pushing up power prices. It was putting pressures on the grid, such that the grid operator actually put a moratorium in place for grid connections for data centers around the Dublin area, back in 2022. This was even before the generative AI moment that caused a surge in construction of data centers.

And then the other one was in The Dalles, Oregon, where Google had actually built its first company-owned data centers about 20 years back. In that case, its water deal was coming up for renewal. It wanted to build more data centers there, so it needed more water. And when the residents started asking, “How much water is Google actually using?” a journalist at The Oregonian went to the city and essentially asked, “Hey, how much water is Google using? Our readers would like to know this,” he was hit with a lawsuit instead of being given that information. Because Google did not want it to be public. And then, of course, when it did come out about a year later, Google revealed that it was using 29% of the city’s water, which seemed quite significant.

That was around when ChatGPT was being released and generative AI was starting to take off. It felt like, okay, there are these pockets of places where a lot of data centers have already been constructed, where they are putting pressures on the communities that are causing people to respond, and it felt like that was going to start coming to a lot more communities. It was a good time to really start to dig in and pay attention.

You don’t just talk about the data centers’ environmental impact, but also, they [become] your way in to talk about this broader tech moment. They represent to you — particularly the hyperscale data centers — represent to you how these tech companies are doing business now.

Absolutely. The hyperscale data centers and just the scale of the construction — whether it’s the the size of the actual facilities themselves or the number that these companies are trying to build out, just feels fundamentally unsustainable.

They’re just demanding too much from the communities where they’re being located, whether that’s because of electricity or because of water use or what have you, or just the fact that they’re building out so many, that when you think about the cumulative impact and you look at the demand for water, but particularly for energy from fossil fuels, that means that it’s just completely out of line with a livable planet and an amount of computation that feels like it makes any degree of sense for us to have constructed.

I think it really goes back to the hubris of the tech industry and of these major tech companies and the tech billionaires that run them, and the fact that they have these particular visions of the future that are based on fantasies, but also on expanding their own power and influence over the world, and they don’t seem to mind that everyone else then has to pay the cost that comes with their ambitions and what they are seeking to do.

And I feel like it’s become particularly egregious with generative AI and the data centers to power them, where many of the benefits that were supposed to come of generative AI do not appear to have materialized — whether those are the economic benefits, the productivity benefits, but also the the benefits of delivery of services like healthcare and education to the public, as we heard Sam Altman saying a lot in the in the early days of ChatGPT.

Paired with that, there has been this massive environmental cost […] but there’s also this massive and growing social cost, when you think about the mental health impacts of chatbot dependence, of all the false information that pervades social media platforms now, of the deepfake crisis, and I could go on and on and on.

It’s not surprising that there has been backlash. But I have been surprised at how widespread it’s been, and also how bipartisan — you talk about Ron DeSantis and Bernie Sanders both being opposed to data centers. Why do you think it’s become such a unifying issue?

Because it’s not an abstract issue, right? I think even when we think about generative AI or social media or something like that, [it] can feel abstract. It’s a service that you access from your phone or from your computer. It’s not something that is tangible in the world.

But when a data center is coming to set up shop in your community, it’s very physical, it’s very material. It doesn’t matter what side of the political spectrum that you’re on. You are going to feel the effects of that in your quality of life, in your power bill, potentially in your access to water, potentially in what you hear around your community, if these air conditioning units on the roofs of data centers are making a lot of noise.

I think that there was already a pre-existing frustration with the tech industry that came from the quality of the products, the effects that they’re having on our society and our lives. For some people, [they have] frustration with the way that the tech industry has been collaborating with the Trump administration and with right-wing political groups in different parts of the world. But then you add on top of that the actual effects that come of having one of these infrastructures be planned or located in your community, and that anger gets kind of directed into that physical manifestation of this thing that you have an issue with.

Obviously, the data centers are at the forefront of this, and it’s what we pay the most attention to. But we also see this increasingly with other aspects of the tech industry, whether it’s the opposition to Flock cameras rolling out across the United States, or the smart glasses that Meta is trying to put on people’s faces, or even the self-driving cars that Waymo is rolling out.

There’s a lot of frustration with the physical manifestations of the tech industry, because that is something that can really be felt more directly, but can also be challenged more directly. You can delete an app on your phone, but what difference does that really make to the company? It doesn’t often seem like very much.

The tech companies have reacted to this backlash in a few different ways. One is that Microsoft, Amazon, and  OpenAI have all talked about [their commitment] that they’re not going to use NDAs anymore in their negotiations with local governments. How big a difference do you think that will make?

I do think it’s going to be good that it will be easier for people to access information on these projects that are being located in their communities. But part of the reason why we’re seeing these shifts is exactly in response to that opposition.

We’re seeing the tech companies propose to pay for more of the electricity infrastructure that will be required for their data centers to reduce the pressure on ratepayers, because people are angry about that. They’re promising to use different water-based cooling systems in their data centers, to say that they’re able to use less electricity.

When we look at the NDAs and the decision or the announcement that they’re not going to use those anymore, I think it’s also because it’s working against them. People know what a data center is now, and if people find out that a data center is coming to town and they have questions and they’re not getting answers, that’s only going to further fuel the opposition to those data centers.

So, it’s not really a surprise to see [the tech companies] do this, and it’s a way that they can get a positive PR thing by saying, “We’re not going to use these NDAs that are working against us anyway. “

One of the other ways they’ve responded is by saying that there’s a lot of misinformation about the environmental harms, or that they’re being overstated, or that they can mitigate them with renewable technology. How valid do you think those arguments are?

I think that the industry recognizes that there is a lot of concern and they’re trying to basically move into the discussion with narratives of their own to have people question whether data centers are really a problem or not.

I feel like we don’t see the industry doing as much on the energy side anymore, particularly on the fossil fuel side, because they realize that they have kind of lost that argument. If you look back a year and a half, two years ago, they were talking a lot about renewables, about nuclear energy, you don’t hear so much of that discourse anymore.

They have pivoted, instead, to focus on the water question, where I think that they feel that they can use more abstract national figures in order to confuse the public about what’s going on. They can say, “If you look nationally, agriculture and golf courses also use a lot of water. So you don’t need to be worried about a data center coming to tow,n because they don’t use as much, in aggregate.”

But the issue with data center water use is not a national issue. It’s a local issue. There are unquestionably going to be communities where they seek to locate data centers that are going to have plenty of water and that are not going to be concerned about a data center coming to town. But they’re also locating [data centers] in a lot of communities that are experiencing high degrees of water stress, which means that they don’t have the water to really provide to these infrastructures.

We have also seen them pivoting to move away from the evaporative cooling systems, the ones that require a lot of water — because you need to take in a certain amount of water as it gets too hot, it evaporates away, and so you can see why you need a consistent flow of water into the data center for that type of cooling system —  to a closed loop system, where you basically have a certain amount of water, it stays within the data center, you keep cooling it down by using electricity to do that, and so you clearly don’t need as much water.

That is their new way to say, “Hey, don’t worry about the water use. We’re going to switch to this other system.” But the issue there, of course, is that it does require more electricity in order to keep that water cooled down, and that is often not part of what they talk about or discuss when they are proposing that as an alternative.

Then the other thing is that it’s not just water that is in those pipes or tubes going through the data center. There’s also a lot of coolant in there, a lot of chemicals, and communities where these projects are being located with closed loop cooling systems are becoming increasingly concerned about those coolants leaking out into their water supply and what that is going to mean.

In the book, you say repeatedly that you’re not against technology itself or even data centers in general, but [against] this hyperscale approach. The book is called “Hyperscale.” The last chapter is called “Human Scale.” Why do you see scale as such an important part of the problem?

Because scale is what creates the pressures, whether those are the pressures on the communities or you know the pressures on our wider electricity system and whatnot.

I don’t think that the inherent problem is data centers, because we have had data centers for a long time, and sure, there have been occasional concerns about specific projects, but data centers as a whole have not been an issue that the public has felt merited this degree of attention. So that is why I feel the hyperscale data center, and particularly the acceleration that we’ve seen in the expansion of the size of these hyperscale data centers, but also the number of data centers that these companies are building out, is notable in this moment.

Linked to that is not just the scale of the demand created by these hyperscale data centers, but also, what they are supposed to be powering? That is where the other disconnect lies. They’re demanding a lot, but if we were actually getting something good for it, this might be justified. I think that for a lot of people, and I feel like we see this reflected in polling, they’re not actually seeing the benefits from generative AI.

So then the question becomes: “Okay, we’re expending all of these resources, we’re pushing up emissions such that climate change is going to be accelerated just to build out all these data centers to meet the ambitions of generative AI that the tech companies have, but then we’re not actually getting much benefit out of that technology. So what are we actually doing here?”

One other thing that really struck a chord with me was your description about how tech executives often talk about the future as if there’s [just one] inevitable future. The only thing that’s in question is how quickly we arrive there [and] who’s going to make the most money off of it.

This is so important for me, because I do feel like how we think about the future then shapes decisions that we make. The tech billionaires, they see the future in a certain way […] and I think that they have been very successful in getting us to buy into their vision of the future for the past several decades. But that does not mean that that is the only way that the future has to be.

We can imagine a very different kind of future, and that would mean that we place our resources in different places and actually develop technology in a different way, toward different outcomes, with different incentives driving that work.

There are interesting conversations happening in different parts of the world right now around things like digital sovereignty, getting off of these technologies from the U.S. tech companies. But in some cases, those conversations are quickly captured by industry in those different parts of the world to say, “Okay, we want to get off U.S. technology, but let’s have our local companies do effectively the same thing, and just let them do the same exploitative practices and make the money that the U.S. tech companies would be making.” And I don’t think that that is the right way forward.

I also think that there is a role for public sector development of technology in a way that we’ve eschewed or even looked at skeptically for the past number of decades, because the idea was that technology had to be left to the private sector. What we’re starting to see in Europe is a bit more funding of public technology, in order to develop the services that government itself depends on, and I think that that is a good first step.

But especially when we’re looking at the Microsoft outage that happened just recently, and we’re looking at Google threatening schools with their access to Google search if they don’t accept Google AI tool, I think that there’s a real moment right now to put resources into developing something different, that doesn’t come from these major tech companies, and that hopefully can have much more public benefit than what these companies now provide us.

#522. The best apps to make new friends right now

In recent years, people have been increasingly looking for new ways to form platonic connections, as loneliness and social isolation have become more prevalent.

In 2023, the U.S. Surgeon General went so far as to label this issue a public health crisis. Remote workers, who miss the everyday interactions found in an office, and younger individuals eager to create their adult social circles based on shared interests and hobbies, are among those seeking meaningful friendships.

Thanks to online dating apps, the stigma associated with finding connections online has largely faded away. This has welcomed a new wave of apps focused on fostering friendships and building local communities.

According to estimates from Appfigures, over a dozen local-focused friendship apps have collectively generated approximately $16 million in consumer spending in the U.S. so far this year. Some notable examples include Timeleft, Meet5, and Bumble’s BFF. Additionally, these apps have garnered approximately 4.3 million downloads thus far in 2025.

The apps aim to provide a less awkward solution than, for instance, approaching a stranger at the gym or local café and trying to strike up a conversation. These platforms clearly indicate that everyone using the app is looking for the same thing — friendship, not romance — making it much less intimidating to initiate conversations.

From major players like Meetup to newer ones like 222, there are plenty of apps that cater to the growing demand for meaningful friendships. We’ve compiled a list of them for you to try out.

The app 222 is an iOS-only social events platform that facilitates in-person meetups by pairing a group of strangers based on their personality test results. The app sends invitations to nearby public social events, such as wine bars and comedy clubs. There is then a vetting process, and selected participants are notified on the day of the event. A bonus for those who feel socially anxious: You’re allowed to bring a plus-one.

The service charges a $22.22 curation fee or a monthly subscription for the same price.

Dating giant Bumble is among the more established players to enter the friendship space, launching its friend-finding feature in 2016, which then spun out into a stand-alone app in 2023. The friend-making app has recently undergone a significant redesign, with an increased emphasis on facilitating group meetups, catering to users’ desires to grow their social circles.

BFF is available for free download on iOS and Android devices.

Clyx is another emerging app in the group-based social networking landscape, with a strong focus on discovering local events. The social platform helps users find community events by integrating data from platforms like Ticketmaster and TikTok. Additionally, Clyx allows users to upload their contact lists, helping them see which events their friends plan to attend. The app also includes a feature that recommends other users to connect with at these events.

As of now, Clyx operates exclusively in two cities: Miami and London. However, it has plans to expand to additional areas in the near future, with New York City and São Paulo at the top of the list. The app is available on the App Store and Google Play Store.

Crowdier is a new group social app that lets friend groups connect with others. Users can create a group of 2-5 friends and browse other groups to arrange hangouts in person.

To initiate a match, users can “Wave” at groups they like, and if those groups wave back, they can plan in a shared chat. A match requires the majority of both groups to agree. For example, 2 people in a 4-person group needs to say yes.

The app is available for free on iOS and Android devices.

Les Amís is a friendship app tailored for women, transgender, and LGBTQ+ individuals that leverages AI to match users based on similar interests and encourages participation in local events, such as pottery classes, book clubs, and wine tastings. Matches are made every Monday, allowing users to chat and plan meetups later in the week.

Available on iOS and Android, Les Amís serves cities across Europe, including Amsterdam, Barcelona, Berlin, Madrid, Paris, and Stockholm. In the U.S., the app is available in Austin and New York, with plans to expand to Boston, Miami, and Los Angeles.

The app uses a paid membership model that varies by city, with fees of $70 in New York and €55 in Amsterdam.

Users can RSVP to events; join groups that cater to a variety of hobbies, professions, or social causes; and create their own groups and events. This European community-building app recently launched in the U.S., targeting users over 40 who want to meet new people in their area and participate in group activities such as picnics, concerts, and hiking. Notably, in a short time, there have been around 777,000 U.S. downloads so far across the App Store and Google Play Store, according to Appfigures estimates.

Pie is another one of the newer social apps on the market. It features an AI-driven quiz designed to predict which users are most likely to be compatible with each other. Currently, the app is only available in Austin, Chicago, and San Francisco. Users can download Pie from the App Store and Google Play Store.

Instead of going to events alone, users can create or join “plans,” which are small group meetups tied to specific events. It’s available to download on the App Store.

Mmotion is currently only available to users in New York City on iOS and Android devices. Users must first submit an application to be considered.

Users must pay a monthly subscription of $44.99 after a 30-day free trial. It’s available on iOS and Android devices.

#517. OpenAI’s chief scientist has thoughts on how to limit AI development

A new blog post from OpenAI’s chief scientist Jakub Pachocki discusses potential limits on AI development, with a focus on a measurement called perplexity.

Slowing AI development became a particularly hot topic after a former OpenAI and Anthropic researcher said the companies were “gambling with our lives,” and even Anthropic’s IPO filing suggested that the technology could pose “catastrophic or existential risks to society.”

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei then published a plan to “pace the frontier,” seemingly endorsed by OpenAI CEO Sam Altman and SpaceX CEO Elon Musk. Among other things, Amodei called for companies to agree to “common safety standards as well as limits on the rate of unchecked AI progress.”

But to establish a speed limit on AI development, you need to agree on how you measure speed. Pachocki’s post — described by Altman as “the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while” — suggests perplexity (not to be confused with the company of the same name) could be that measurement.

In Pachochki’s argument, benchmarks that focus on AI’s “downstream capabilities” have quickly become outdated as the technology advances. But perplexity — which “measures how well a model predicts the next token on average; lower perplexity means better predictions” — has remained “informative.”

Pachochki’s post includes a chart of how the perplexity of OpenAI’s models has fallen since GPT 3.5.

“GPT‑4, at ~0.91, was proficient at utilizing chain-of-thought reasoning productively, which motivated the subsequent targeted elicitation of this capability and the creation of modern reasoning models,” he wrote. “Now, as we have reached losses below 0.8, we have seen the emergence of transformative capabilities in fields like cybersecurity and mathematics.”

So in his view, perplexity could provide the basis for “a shared benchmark for general intelligence,” which AI companies could then use to establish “common limits.” With perplexity serving as a standard measurement for AI progress, the companies would commit not to go past a certain threshold “until our ability to understand and monitor AI behavior achieves a certain concrete milestone.”

#507. Europe's cyber weak spot is the everyday businesses nobody thought to protect

Cyberattacks on refrigerators in French supermarkets in October spurred Swedish chain Coop to act. But it wasn't fast enough.

Attackers hit refrigeration systems in seven of its stores in early November. The affected stores had been scheduled for security upgrades just two days later, a company spokesperson told CNBC.

Mundane but essential equipment, used by everyday businesses, has become a favorite target of cyber saboteurs in recent weeks.

Experts are warning that a combination of known vulnerabilities, basic security failures and ambiguity over whether suppliers or their clients are responsible are making things worse, as AI makes cyber threats faster and more sophisticated.

Guy Segal, CEO of cybersecurity firm Sygnia, told CNBC that his company handled 285% more incidents in October than a year earlier, with demand forcing it to turn away some businesses seeking emergency assistance.

"It's going to get worse before it's going to get better," Segal said.

The recent cyberattacks targeted refrigeration systems across hundreds of European supermarkets, remotely altering temperature settings and forcing retailers to discard spoiled food.

France has since accused a cyber unit with links to Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps of the attacks, an allegation Tehran denies.

Fredrik Blix, a cybersecurity researcher at Stockholm University, told CNBC that thousands of industrial control systems remain accessible over the public internet, sometimes despite manufacturers' warnings.

Segal said that basic cyber protection measures such as multi-factor authentication and basic network segmentation are more important than ever.

He said risks to major civic infrastructure like the electrical grid were front of mind and so "usually pretty well protected."

"There are always those civil supply chain components that are way, way less protected. Could be supermarkets, could be gas stations, could be… the screens on the Tube," Segal said, referring to London's underground rail system.

The supermarket attacks showed "this is the new normal, and definitely it's a wake-up call for every company out there," Segal added.

Segal said that inexpensive AI tools mean technical expertise and resources are no longer needed to launch attacks. He described one recent incident in which clients suffered breaches within six hours of a vulnerability becoming known, as perpetrators used AI to act faster — a process that might have taken weeks a year earlier.

Segal, whose firm helps companies respond to breaches and strengthen their defenses, warned that businesses without prearranged incident-response support could struggle to secure emergency assistance, as overwhelmed cybersecurity firms prioritize existing clients.

In one case, he said, Sygnia had to turn down a company because no response teams were available. Segal added that the company in question had already been turned down by ten other providers.

Jacob Silverman, CEO of risk advisory firm Kroll, told CNBC earlier this month that the company was operating at full capacity in its incident-response business amid what he described as "the most rapid uptick in threat activity that we've ever seen."

Segal argued that retailers should treat cybersecurity risks with the same seriousness as fire safety and food hygiene, including when outsourcing maintenance.

When Ahold Delhaize stores in Belgium and Luxembourg were attacked on Nov. 2, it exposed how different independent store operators had responded to the warnings following the earlier attacks in France.

Retailers had been warned about the vulnerabilities following earlier attacks in France, and those that implemented the recommended protections were spared, CEO Frans Muller told investors in an earnings call earlier this month, but smaller operators with fewer technical resources had struggled to make the necessary changes.

Coop, whose stores are owned by regional consumer cooperatives or operated by franchises, told CNBC that public attention on the recent attacks was the main driver of security improvements. It said the vulnerability at the affected stores had since been addressed.

The financial consequences of cyberattacks can be substantial. Ahold Delhaize told investors in November that a separate October cyberattack on its online operations and supply chain would reduce fourth-quarter sales by 200 million euros ($219 million).

Danish shipping giant Maersk, which suffered an estimated $250 million to $300 million in losses from the 2017 NotPetya cyberattack, has spent nearly a decade strengthening its defenses.

CEO Vincent Clerc told CNBC earlier this month that the experience had prompted significant investment in security capabilities, but warned that AI was enabling attackers to become more effective.

"Everybody is in a race" to boost defenses, he said.

Research by cybersecurity firm Censys found that the number of internet-exposed refrigeration controllers made by Danfoss and Copeland exposed to cyber hacking fell 53% globally between Oct. 9 and Nov. 5.

However, more than 3,100 remained exposed, and Censys said it could not determine whether the decline reflected permanent security improvements or equipment simply being taken offline.

A Copeland spokesperson confirmed that some of its refrigeration systems were affected by the vulnerability and said it had contacted customers to reinforce cybersecurity guidance, including keeping devices off the public internet and updating software.

Danfoss didn't respond to CNBC requests for comment.

In the U.K., a covert cybersecurity operation has been underway to protect tens of thousands of refrigeration systems at supermarkets, warehouses, restaurants and other businesses, the BBC reported on Friday. After researchers discovered a major vulnerability in late October, cybersecurity specialists, IT teams and the U.K.'s National Cyber Security Centre worked to alert dozens of companies and secure their systems before hackers could exploit the flaw.

The EU's Cyber Resilience Act will also impose stricter security requirements on manufacturers of connected products from December 2027.

But the legislation does not require all existing equipment to be made compliant with the new standards.

Blix told CNBC on Nov. 6 that European authorities should take a more proactive approach to reducing vulnerabilities by identifying exposed industrial control systems and contacting their operators before attacks occur.

"What worries me is that we don't really do anything," he said, adding that "we are shuffling a lot of papers" and "doing a lot of risk assessments" but failing to take meaningful action.
