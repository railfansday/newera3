#676. Consumers are spending on physical gifts even as travel tops wish lists, Visa survey finds

Consumers are hoping for more travel and experiences this holiday season, but physical gifts continue to win the lion's share of their wallets, according to a new report from Visa on Thursday.

The report found that more than half of survey respondents are putting travel and live events at the top of their wish lists. Still, only 15% of those respondents are prioritizing spending on experiences for others, while 27% of them plan to favor physical gifts.

That gap between what shoppers want and what they plan to buy is largely due to higher travel costs, lower consumer sentiment and an emphasis on value, according to Michael Brown, Visa's principal U.S. economist.

"There is some interesting trade-offs there that we're seeing on the part of consumers," Brown told CNBC. "If I'm traveling more, I'm probably going to be gifting those physical goods less, so this is really the broader context."

The survey was conducted among roughly 4,660 U.S. adults ages 18 and older, designed to be representative of the broader population of consumers.

Brown said budget pressure also factors into traveling for the holidays, as Visa expects more people to celebrate the holidays "a little bit closer to home" than in the past given rising airfares and general inflation. In September, consumer prices rose 3.7% annually, driven higher by surging energy costs.

Airlines have hiked the price of plane tickets this year as their fuel costs have risen by billions of dollars due to the war in the Middle East. And even if fuel prices drop, executives have said fares are likely to stay high, with fares up more than 22% in September compared with a year earlier, according to the consumer price index.

Those trends mean consumers will likely be more intentional about where they're spending, especially as inflation drives up prices across the board.

"Consumers are aspirational. They want those experiences, they want to travel, they want to be out in the world," Brown said. "However, the economic reality is with prices rising, with economic uncertainty, they need to balance the reality of today's economic environment with those aspirations."

The report also found that only households with an income above $200,000 spent more on trips departing between Nov. 1 and early next year compared with 2025 into 2026. On the other hand, many other income groups pulled back on their travel spending.

"If you think about that segment, a lot of them own equities, and the equity market runup over the last several months has created a lot of positivity among that specific affluent demographic, and I think that's one of the factors that continues to motivate even aggregate macro spend today," Brown said.

Meanwhile, less affluent shoppers are placing an emphasis on value. Visa's consumer survey from October showed that roughly one-third of consumers with an income below $100,000 are going to switch to lower-cost brands or stores when shopping for holiday gifts this season.

Though Visa is projecting a 4.5% year-over-year increase in holiday sales growth, much of that figure is due to inflation. Without those price increases, sales are expected to rise just 1.1% this year, down from the 1.3% increase during the 2025 holiday season.

Ultimately, Brown said the survey findings may be encouraging for the broader retail scene this holiday season. With less emphasis on travel and more projected spending on physical gifts, it could boost the sector at a time when spending is under pressure.

At the same time, he said that consumers may just be expecting to spend more this holiday season because they've become accustomed to the higher prices.

"But, of course, consumers are internalizing higher prices of things, and I think that's why they're reporting higher anticipated spend this year," Brown said. "I think these numbers just need to be looked at through the lens of the inflation and particularly consumers' perception of where inflation is going to go. I think that weighed heavily on consumer psychology this season."

#652. Expedia CEO says AI agents aren't changing booking habits — yet

Expedia CEO Ariane Gorin said Thursday the company hasn't yet seen artificial intelligence agents affecting the way customers book, but the early signs show potential for an industry shift.

"It's a great way for people to do inspiration, to do trip planning," Gorin said in an interview with CNBC. "We haven't seen it change the way they're actually doing booking."

The comments come as personal AI agents like Meta's Muse and OpenAI's Dots enter the mainstream, promising to tackle everything from scheduling to shopping for everyday Americans. Opportunities in travel planning and booking could pose a competitive threat to traditional sites like Expedia.

Gorin said Expedia is "working with all of the agents" to make sure it surfaces in AI results, to understand how the agents get information and to make sure that the company has the necessary integrations.

She acknowledged it's possible that AI agents could develop their own travel programs and eventually claim market share, but she said complexities within the industry — customer service, for example — would be difficult to fully replace with artificial intelligence.

"People were already shopping across a number of places, so the agent can do that in a much easier way," Gorin said. "But when it comes to actually booking — being confident that the content that you're getting is right, the amenities are right, the price is right, if something goes wrong, someone's going to actually either solve it for you, answer the phone, be there for you — I don't think those agents are going to do it for you, and that's why people trust Expedia and come to us."

Shares of Expedia were down about 12% Thursday after the company delivered weaker-than-expected guidance for the fourth quarter on Wednesday. The company forecast revenue of between $3.69 billion and $3.75 billion for the final three months of the year, making the midpoint lower than analyst estimates of $3.74 billion, according to FactSet.

Gorin attributed the "moderation" in Expedia's fourth-quarter guidance to "geopolitical events in the Middle East and Europe," the midterm elections and the company moving its fall sale from the fourth quarter to the third quarter. But she noted the company reported a strong third quarter, as total gross bookings rose 11% and revenue climbed 12%.

#625. Wendy's rejects $1.4 billion takeover proposal from Nelson Peltz’s Trian

Wendy's on Thursday said it is rejecting a $1.4 billion takeover offer from a group of investors led by Nelson Peltz's Trian Fund Management.

The group offered $7.35 per share in cash on Oct. 28 to take the struggling burger chain private. In addition to Trian, it included investment firms BlueFive Capital and Sixth Street Partners, plus Flynn Group, one of Wendy's largest franchisees.

In February, Wendy's board formed a committee of independent directors to assess any potential bid from Trian in response to a regulatory filing from the firm. The committee found that the proposal "substantially undervalues" Wendy's and does not reflect potential gains from the chain's ongoing turnaround strategy, the company said in a press release Thursday.

"Our confidence rests firmly in [CEO] Bob Wright's proven track record of seasoned industry leadership, including restaurant business turnarounds," Art Winkleblack, Wendy's board chair and a member of the independent committee, said in a statement.

Trian declined to comment on Wendy's decision.

To stave off a hostile takeover, Wendy's has adopted a shareholder rights plan — or poison pill — with a one-year duration, as of Wednesday, the company said. Wendy's added that the investor group led by Trian rejected an offer from Wendy's for a standstill agreement that would have paused additional share purchases, adding to Wendy's decision to use a poison pill.

The rejection is the latest in the long-running saga between Peltz and the burger chain, which dates back to an activist campaign he led more than two decades ago. A Peltz firm has even owned Wendy's before. In 2008, his company Triarc, which also owned Arby's, bought Wendy's in a $2.3 billion deal. It later sold Arby's, turning Wendy's into a stand-alone company again.

In 2024, Wendy's named Peltz as chairman emeritus after he spent 17 years on the company's board. Trian executive Peter May and Peltz's son, Bradley, still sit on Wendy's board.

Wendy's also reported its third-quarter results on Thursday. Its same-store sales declined for the seventh straight quarter. However, its quarterly earnings and revenue topped Wall Street's expectations.

Shares of Wendy's fell less than 1% in morning trading. The takeover offer had lifted the stock, which has lost about 60% of its value over the last three years.

Wendy's is not the only restaurant chain that has found itself in the crosshairs of activist investors. Applebee's owner Dine Brands recently adopted a poison pill to block a takeover bid from investment firms Arkhouse Management and Cobblers Cove Capital.

#600. Marc Lore's Wonder confidentially files for IPO

Food startup Wonder has confidentially filed for an initial public offering, the company said Thursday.

Created by serial entrepreneur Marc Lore in 2018, Wonder advertises itself as "the best restaurants from across the country, all in one place," available for delivery and takeout on its vertically integrated app.

Originally envisioned as a new take on food delivery through sprinter vans outfitted with mobile kitchens, Wonder has grown to roughly 170 brick-and-mortar locations throughout the U.S. Its locations serve menu items from more than two dozen "restaurants" across a variety of cuisines, some with branding from celebrity chefs.

Starting Nov. 17, the company plans to introduce more dining options through Wonder Create. Influencers, entrepreneurs and brands can pay $10 a month to start their own restaurant business based out of a Wonder location and earn a portion of the revenue from orders.

Wonder has spent at least $1 billion on acquisitions, such as delivery service Grubhub, meal kit brand Blue Apron and delivery and logistics provider Relay, along with some buzzy restaurant concepts. Wonder has been able to afford such expensive dealmaking thanks to more than $3 billion in venture funding.

Wonder's Series D fundraising round earlier this year raised its pre-money valuation to $9 billion.

With the proceeds from an IPO, Wonder will be able to make more acquisitions and work toward its goal of making food more accessible, Lore told CNBC in September.

But as a public company, Wonder will also likely face investor scrutiny. Weak consumer sentiment has weighed on restaurant stocks recently, plus market volatility has resulted in a shaky IPO market for companies across a range of industries.

Lore previously founded Diapers.com and Jet.com, which he sold to Amazon and Walmart, respectively.

Create a list of the 10 most readily actionable recommendations for improving the process. Categorize these action items by the level of effort required to implement them, as well as by their potential impact.

While Huang deals with the fallout of his actions, the loudest voice of this counter-narrative is, for the foreseeable future, silenced.

But now that Huang has become a meme for cheating on his wife with an alleged spy, he’s perhaps not the best choice to help market these devices as a cool alternative to the Mac mini.

Huang demolished his reputation right as bond underwriters (lenders) and bond buyers are pretty much over AI build-out-related debt. Of roughly $80 billion in data center bonds issued this year, nearly half were rated high-risk “junk,” Forbes reports.

Huang sold a vision of Nvidia as “investable infrastructure” with a long resale cycle, like airplanes. Then he made the rounds with the financial press, as he often did, to pitch his vision. Now, however, Wall Street is concerned that Nvidia’s stock will crash and take their portfolios, and possibly the economy, with it.

Nvidia has lost its top salesperson and the AI industry has lost its top hype man. Even if Huang somehow comes back into the public eye to champion AI again, every interview and meeting will be about his sex life, choices, and national security — not his vision for the industry.

We’ll have to wait and see how Nvidia navigates the scandal Huang heaped onto it, but now that the company is finally in its post “founder mode” era, and hunting for a new CEO, it’s doubtful it has the cache to push the industry the way Huang was.

#528. Where's the growth? Legacy automakers face a tougher 2027 as costs rise, market tightens

DETROIT — There's a growing concern that the global automotive industry may be stuck in neutral, if not reverse, heading into 2027.

The auto industry — from the companies to their customers — has been surprisingly resilient this decade, but there are fears that rising costs, weaker vehicle pricing and a more challenging market could threaten profits and growth going forward.

Automakers also face pressure outside the U.S. from an onslaught of Chinese carmakers, a thinner new-product pipeline after retreating from some all-electric plans, and company- and region-specific challenges.

"With investor focus increasingly shifting to 2027, the setup appears challenging," Barclays analyst Dan Levy said in an investor note last month. "Rising inflation, continued China pressure, affordability concerns in the U.S., and a flat global production outlook suggest another year of limited earnings growth."

Several Wall Street analysts have said automakers will need continued pricing strength and additional cost savings to offset inflation, higher investment spending and a potentially weaker economy just to maintain their current levels.

"It's a little bit more optimistic in the U.S. and then very pessimistic in Europe," RBC Capital Markets analyst Tom Narayan told CNBC. "As a whole, there's some negativity on '27."

Automakers from the U.S. to Europe to Asia have been facing a growing list of headwinds.

For U.S.-based legacy automakers, Ford Motor said it expects next year's results to be in line with 2026, while General Motors grew more cautious about its ability to continue growing earnings as much as it had previously expected.

"We continue to believe that 2027 will be another strong year and better than 2026, assuming customer demand remains strong, and commodity, logistics and other recent cost pressures moderate," GM CEO Mary Barra told investors Oct. 20.

GM CFO Paul Jacobson said that while it's too early to officially forecast, the company is facing additional inflationary costs that could lead to another $2 billion headwind in 2027, like it expects this year. He specifically cited costs of commodity inflation, logistics and DRAM, or dynamic random-access memory chips as challenges.

Those chips are critical components in modern vehicles and are used for everything from temporary data storage and real-time processing from infotainment systems to safety-critical systems.

GM and others have been able to offset outside pressures through cost cuts, operational efficiencies and resilient consumer demand for higher-margin vehicles. However, there's a limit to what companies can do.

"There's only so much pricing you can push to consumers," Narayan said. "They're already at pretty high pricing."

European automakers including Volkswagen, Mercedes-Benz and BMW in recent weeks reduced their 2026 earnings forecasts in response to U.S. tariffs, the growth of Chinese automakers and rising costs.

Most recently, BMW this week said accelerated restructuring measures would reduce its automotive pretax earnings margin this year by 2.5 percentage points. The company said the restructuring should support "positive effects" into 2027 and a 3% to 5% pretax profit margin for 2028. That compares with a 5.3% automotive margin last year and 3.4% during the third quarter.

Chrysler parent Stellantis, which has large operations in Europe and the U.S., is facing its own challenges, as it's in the midst of a restructuring that aims for a low-single-digit adjusted operating margin this year. It has maintained a 2026 guidance that includes a mid-single-digit percentage increase in net revenue and a low-single-digit adjusted operating margin.

In Asia, South Korean automaker Hyundai Motor, which has experienced notable growth in recent years, also cut key metrics when it reported earnings late last month. The company lowered its 2026 wholesales target to 4 million vehicles, down from 4.16 million, and cut its operating-margin target to between 5.5% and 6.5%, down from a previous goal of 6.3% to 7.3%.

Toyota Motor is an outlier for its most recent quarter and outlook, as the world's largest automaker increased its full-year operating income forecast by 500 billion Japanese yen (about $3.2 billion) on Thursday.

Toyota's results were boosted by weakness in the Japanese yen as well as strong hybrid vehicle demand in the U.S. However, the increase in operating income was its first in six quarters.

Broadly, the changes to earnings and outlooks come as production for the global industry slows.

Consulting and insights firm GlobalData expects global light-vehicle production of about 90.96 million units in 2026, down 2.1% compared with last year. It forecast a production increase of less than 1% next year. That comes on the heels of strong annual growth earlier this decade, led by China.

Cox Automotive reported the average transaction price of a new U.S. vehicle in September was more than $50,500. That was just shy of the record of $50,614 in December 2025.

Lofty vehicle prices have led to record-high and record-long financing deals, as well as concerns that customers are getting priced out of the new-vehicle market.

The typical U.S. new-vehicle payment reached $776 a month in September, the highest since December 2023, according to Cox Automotive.

Cox Automotive chief economist Jeremy Robb, discussing the third quarter for the automotive industry, rated the overall economy, buyer ability and borrowing costs as cautionary "yellow lights."

But he did have some "green lights," such as good credit availability and employment conditions, which could give some relief for automakers and consumers, many of whom need vehicles for their everyday lives.

Robb said there's a "tug-of-war" between headwinds such as rising costs, interest rates and fuel prices and tailwinds such as growth in liquid assets and savings that has helped offset some of the additional costs.

"Consumers remain under financial pressure, but the vehicle demand continues to hold, showing more resiliency in the auto industry than we've seen in other parts of the economy," Robb said during a Sept. 24 call, noting consumer sentiment remains low.

#521. Phota Labs uses AI to turn everyday photos into professional-looking shots

A new iOS app called Phota Labs gives users access to a personal AI photo assistant that turns everyday snapshots into images that look like they were taken by a professional photographer, all while preserving the authenticity of the image.

Rather than giving consumers yet another photo-editing tool, Phota Labs aims to use advances in AI to automate the editing process so it’s easier for anyone to get access to better photos.

The a16z-backed startup was founded by Cecilia Zhang and Zach Xia, who met at Adobe while working on computational photography products, including Project Indigo, Lightroom, and Photoshop.

The duo saw a gap in the editing tools on the market and believed that although editing tools can be powerful for enhancing photos, they aren’t very effective at fixing a photo that wasn’t captured well in the first place. Phota Labs changes that by focusing on fixing the shot itself while preserving the natural appearances of those in the image.

“Phota is your personal photo assistant that can make great photos for any moment you want to keep before you even ask, which means that it’s a very proactive experience,” Zhang told TechCrunch in an interview. “Think of it almost like a photographer or virtual photographer who looks over your camera roll and tries to understand what’s happening and how they could have taken that photo for you on the spot for that scene.”

The idea is to use AI to transform photos, whether that’s turning a wide-angle group photo into a tighter portrait or transforming a selfie into a regular shot.

Users can choose from different photography styles that determine how AI transforms their images, ranging from subtle edits that preserve the original lens and composition to more dramatic transformations that introduce effects like fish-eye lenses, alternative angles, and entirely different perspectives.

While some people turn to ChatGPT and other generative AI tools to enhance or edit images, these tools can often alter a person’s appearance, especially when working with blurry photos because they don’t know what that person actually looks like.

“For Phota, we built our own tech,” Zhang said. ”We built this personalization technology that actually learns about people’s identity and is able to preserve them in any transformation we do to a photo.”

Images edited with Phota Labs feature invisible watermarks that identify them as altered by AI when shared on social platforms.

The startup is initially targeting people who take tons of photos and like to share them on social media, but don’t want to deal with the tedious process of editing photos. Over time, the founders envision a broader audience, from retirees looking to create beautiful photos for their grandchildren to parents who want to share polished family albums with friends and relatives.

“We have this obsession with authenticity,” Xia said. “I think there’s so much value in connecting AI with real moments and making sure those photos are not perfect, in a sense. That’s why we spend a lot of research into understanding personal context, into building this identity model because we want to make sure the photos are authentic. They’re just better photos of the same moment, rather than saying, ‘Oh, this is an AI image’.”

Xia said beta users were excited to see better versions of moments they had actually lived and saw the results as photos of real memories rather than AI-generated images. The founders discovered that users were taking more photos because they knew Phota Labs could enhance them later.

“We’re turning something that’s unusable into something that people want to print,” Zhang said. “People who just love photos but never really got into editing can now experience professional quality that could never have been possible. So, I think that meant so much to us. There’s so many cherished moments for photos, and we really want them to be actually meaningful and something that you can remember.”

In terms of funding, the company raised a $5.6 million seed round led by a16z last year.

Phota Labs is launching as a free app to build an initial user base before exploring a freemium model. The startup also plans to launch an Android app in the future.

#518. SoftBank’s billion-dollar loss may have put a cap on AI’s natural gas ambitions

SoftBank might not be the first to invest in a hot trend, but when it does, it goes big.

Witness its $30 billion bet on OpenAI in early 2025 or its announcement in February that its SB Energy subsidiary would spend $33 billion on a 9.2-gigawatt natural gas power plant. If you can’t be first, be biggest, I guess.

That philosophy apparently spilled over into procurement, where orders were also supersized. Starting about two years ago, SoftBank reportedly put down deposits on more than 13 gigawatts of turbines, almost 50% more than it needed.

The decision to over-order undoubtedly helped secure turbines and speed up SoftBank’s projects.

“SB Energy did not have a choice except to overbook turbines in the future if they wanted some turbines right away,” Peter Gardett, CEO of Noreva, told TechCrunch via email.

The energy world was caught off guard by the swiftness and magnitude of AI’s rising power demands. Queues for turbine orders now extend into the early 2030s. As natural gas was shaping up to be a go-to power source for the AI boom, CEO Masayoshi Son bet that by deploying his investment firm’s significant war chest, he could corner a portion of the market. If the AI world wanted power for its data centers, it would have to come to him.

Now that bet appears to have fizzled. SoftBank announced a $7.1 billion quarterly loss on Tuesday, with over half of that stemming from losses related to power-generating equipment. The investor had paid turbine manufacturers nonrefundable deposits to secure orders, and now it considered much of those worthless.

The move was also a costly bit of price discovery, Gardett said. Rather than overpay and potentially still be short of turbines, SoftBank overpaid and over-ordered.

“This charge is just that price discovery finally happening,” Gardett said.

The magnitude of that misplaced bet — over $3 billion — reflects some of the electricity mania that has overtaken the AI world. Faced with years-long waits to connect to the grid, many hyperscalers and developers have turned to natural gas as a sort of energy security blanket, something that is proven and familiar.

As AI data centers spend more time in operation, their operators are getting a better feel for what’s required to keep them operating. Natural gas might seem like a safe bet, but there are still risks, including winter curtailments when supplies dip and demand spikes, Brent Nelson, senior managing director of markets and strategy at Ascend Analytics, told the Equity podcast Wednesday.

In light of that, Nelson said the grid might still be their best bet. Most data centers would be best served by a grid connection, albeit a smaller one than they might normally request. To make up for the shortfall, large batteries are a cost-effective choice, Nelson said. They’d help shave peak loads or ride out requests from the utility to curtail electricity use.

Data center operators might be coming to that same conclusion, leading the natural gas market to develop more slowly than SoftBank expected.

SoftBank’s natural gas-related losses are unlikely to have a domino effect, leading to mass cancellations of gas power plants. AI’s power demand appears to be too large for that. For now, natural gas is riding high, though SoftBank may have found its ceiling.

The airline’s move follows a warning from banks, including Bank of America, which last week urged customers not to share their passwords with their AI assistants.

#507. Jensen Huang’s exit puts Nvidia’s support for open source in question

The open source community just lost one of its strongest advocates.

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang resigned Thursday after he admitted to an affair with an alleged Chinese spy, raising concerns about U.S. national security and the motives behind the executive’s support for softer chip export controls on China.

Huang has been a staunch supporter of open source AI, framing the ecosystem as good for safety, innovation, diffusion, and sovereignty. And as frontier AI labs increasingly move to reduce their reliance on Nvidia and build their own chips, supporting open source is also a good way for the company to diversify its customer base by selling chips to nation-states, enterprises, and any organization that wants to own its own models.

Still, Huang’s resignation raises questions about whether Nvidia will carry on his legacy by continuing to support the open model community.

Jensen no longer being CEO would be very very bad for the open model cause in the US.

Huang’s departure also comes as Nvidia has yet to finalize a deal to acquire open source platform Hugging Face for $13 billion. Hugging Face CEO Clem Delangue said in a post on X that the company would continue to fight for open source, whatever happens next.

Nvidia also agreed to pay $6 billion for a non-exclusive license for open AI coding startup Poolside’s technology.

Within Nvidia, Huang pushed for the development of the Nemotron family of open-weight AI models built for long-running agentic AI systems and reasoning workloads — potential competitors to closed frontier models. One company insider told TechCrunch that they don’t expect a change in leadership to have an impact on Nemotron development.

Huang also backed companies that would make American open models more competitive, investing in startups like Reflection AI and Thinking Machines Lab.

Huang’s influence extended beyond Nvidia’s investments and product roadmap. He also emerged as one of the most prominent voices pushing back against Washington’s efforts to restrict the open AI ecosystem.

Earlier this year, Huang wrote an open letter signed by 25 companies, including Microsoft, Meta, IBM, and Palantir, urging the U.S. government not to over-restrict open-weight AI models, many of which come from China.

The impetus for that letter was Washington’s debate over how the U.S. should respond to allegations that Chinese AI labs are stealing intellectual property from American counterparts like Anthropic and OpenAI, both of which have reportedly appealed directly to Washington on the matter, urging stronger protections.

That combination of Huang’s personal visibility and Nvidia’s market power made him the strongest counterweight to arguments from closed-model providers like Anthropic and OpenAI, which have advocated for greater restrictions on advanced AI models due to safety reasons.

Without Huang, the concern among open source community members is that whoever takes over will be more cautious. Nvidia has named CFO Colette Kress as interim CEO. It’s not clear how Kress will approach the Hugging Face deal or support for the open model ecosystem, but founder-driven priorities tend to get a downgrade when leadership changes.

Huang’s exit could also strengthen the arguments of those in Washington who are arguing for tougher restrictions on Chinese AI companies, particularly as the scandal surrounding his relationship with Alice Huang, the Megaspeed executive and alleged spy, puts renewed scrutiny on his efforts to loosen chip export controls.

Restrictions on open-weight models from China could kneecap competition for American companies, the most powerful of which have kept their models closed.

Still, supporting open source isn’t simply a passion project Huang pursued out of idealism. It’s increasingly central to Nvidia’s business strategy.

As some of Nvidia’s biggest customers, including Google and OpenAI, develop their own chips, Nvidia has an incentive to cultivate a much broader ecosystem of AI developers and customers.

More players training, fine-tuning, and running models mean more GPUs sold, and less dependence on a handful of frontier labs.

Huang may be gone, but Nvidia still needs an open AI ecosystem. The question now is whether that commitment can survive without the man who made it a priority.

#503. Cosm rides premium moviegoing wave with more immersive screenings and a wider library

The next big thing in premium large format theatrical viewing takes inspiration from the stars.

Cosm, an immersive entertainment technology company, got its start programming planetariums — its name a combination of "cosmos" and "colosseum." The company's massive, domed screens first offered guests a view of the universe, now they mimic courtside seats at sports venues around the world and drop film fans into some of Hollywood's most iconic sets.

At a time when the theatrical industry is seeing its biggest growth from the sale of premium large format, or PLF, tickets, Cosm seeks to offer a fresh take on an elevated viewing experience. And it's quickly expanding both its footprint and its content library.

Since 2024, Cosm has opened four theaters — near Los Angeles, Dallas, Atlanta and Detroit — with a Cleveland hub set to open in 2027. These experimental venues have garnered the attention of sports fans, screening live matchups in an arena-like environment. Showings often sell out, according to Cosm.

More recently, Cosm has partnered with Hollywood studios like Warner Bros., now a part of Skydance, to bring the likes of "The Matrix," "Willy Wonka & the Chocolate Factory," "Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone" and "It" to its locations. Cosm recently signed a deal with Universal, which will bring "Shrek" to its domes in December.

The agreements mark a lucrative new chapter for the privately held Cosm, expanding its audience reach and viewing opportunities beyond timed, live events.

As a privately held company, Cosm doesn't disclose its financials. Tickets cost anywhere from $18 for a matinee to around $70 for prime seats or in-demand showings. That doesn't include added fees for concessions or items from special, themed menus tied to screenings.

Sony invested $100 million in the company in June, taking a minority stake and placing Sony Pictures Entertainment Chairman and CEO Ravi Ahuja onto its board of directors.

Cosm also counts among its investors Cosm chairman Steve Winn's firm, Mirasol Capital; former Milwaukee Bucks co-owner Marc Lasry's Avenue Sports Fund; Rocket Mortgage co-founder Dan Gilbert's Rock; and David Blitzer, managing partner of the New Jersey Devils and Philadelphia 76ers, who invested through his firm Bolt Ventures.

"Cosm is really early in its growth trajectory at the moment, and we saw an opportunity to partner with the company at, what I would say is at, a very important stage in its development," Jay Levine, chief strategy and business operations officer at Sony, told CNBC. "They have about five locations with the goal of a pretty significant rollout. And the real reason why we did it was it gave us a lot of exposure to a growing distribution and what we deem an experiential platform that we think has long-term potential."

Sony has made several plays in the theatrical space in recent years. It bought Alamo Drafthouse in 2024 and entered a long-term lease to reopen and restore the iconic Cinerama Dome in Los Angeles in July of this year.

"Our experiences strategy is around tech, it's around fandom, and it's around new and innovative approaches to content distribution," Levine said. "For us, we're all about these differentiated experiences."

Cosm's 87-foot-diameter, concave screens drop viewers into the middle of the action, whether it's an NFL game or inside the world of "The Matrix." The company's forthcoming Cleveland location will have an even bigger dome, at 100 feet in diameter.

The experience is akin to that of the Sphere in Las Vegas, which has garnered headlines since opening in 2023 for hosting high-profile concerts and events.

"People absolutely love to live out what they love in fandom, just like wearing a sports jersey," Alexis Scalice, head of Cosm Studios, told CNBC. "And I love that I'm seeing that same behavior happen with cinema."

Stadium-style seating is nestled within each Cosm dome, with booths and tables set on each level. The dome's width is such that it fills much of the viewer's periphery, giving the sensation that they are part of the on-screen environment.

The Los Angeles and Dallas locations have capacity for around 350 seats each, while the newer domes in Atlanta and Detroit have up to 500 available seats. Cosm has not shared how many seats the Cleveland location will have, but with its larger screen it is expected to have a larger number of seats.

For live sports events, Cosm has specialty cameras mounted in arenas that broadcast back to the dome, creating the illusion of being on the field of play.

For theatrical features, the film is played in a large rectangle in the center of the dome and the background is transformed to extend the scene.

In Cosm's presentation of "Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone," for example, during the iconic scene where thousands of Hogwarts acceptance letters flood through the fireplace and into a living room, the background is swarmed with animated envelopes.

Cosm has partnered with experiential production company Little Cinema and creative studio MakeMake to create the visuals and environments for each scene of a film released in the dome. From storyboarding to post-production, this process can take up to a year as Cosm, the participating studio and the visual effects artists craft and render each moment.

"We really work hard not to take away from the story, so the film remains the hero in the narrative," Scalice said. "Working very closely with Warner Bros. and our creative partners to make sure that we're not detracting too much from the story, but we're enhancing those moments for more elevated emotional response."

With the exception of "It," which was released in 2017, Cosm's theatrical offerings thus far have been films that were released decades ago and that typically have long-established fan bases.

That helps Cosm leverage what Scalice called "that nostalgia factor" — "parents coming in that ... grew up on this, and they want to share it with their children."

"We can reinvigorate these legendary films," she added.

Cosm has dabbled across genres — horror, fantasy, science fiction — and also demographics — family-friendly and R-rated. "Shrek," set to debut Dec. 16, will be its first foray into animation.

"The thing that's so incredible about it is everybody thought initially it's just going to be working for sports, and when you look at what they've done, a lot of the titles with 'Harry Potter,' with 'Shrek,' and some of the other ones, I mean, the fill rates are actually incredible," Sony's Levine said.

Studios and theatrical exhibitors alike are looking for innovative ways to showcase Hollywood's product to moviegoers, especially as major shifts in viewing habits have disrupted the business.

In the post-pandemic era, the cinema space has been defined by fewer screens and fewer moviegoers, but those declines have been masked by higher movie ticket prices, particularly for PLF experiences.

These elevated offerings — think Imax, Dolby Cinema, ScreenX and 4DX — have bigger screens and better sound or introduce a unique experience, such as moving seats and fog machines. They're particularly popular with Gen Z and millennials, who've yet to be deterred by rising average ticket prices.

While an average standard movie ticket costs around $13, premium tickets average more than $18 each, according to data from EntTelligence.

Prices for Cosm's events fluctuate based on showtime and venue. Sony's Levine said the company's ticket prices and attendance rates are "higher than I think anybody really would have thought."

Unlike Cosm's sports offerings, which can also be viewed from outside the dome from its hall and deck viewing areas at a lower price, theatrical presentations are only available in the dome.

"They have no problem selling these tickets because people want to experience these things, like an immersive format," said Mike Polydoros, CEO at cinema marketing firm PaperAirplane Media. "That is something they can't get at home."
