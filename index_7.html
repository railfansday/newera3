#874. Why popular airplanes are getting scrapped years early

GOODYEAR, Ariz. — An Airbus A320neo, one of the world's most popular planes, rolled off the Mobile, Alabama, factory line in July 2022, painted bright yellow for its next destination: Spirit Airlines.

It was built to fly for decades. But this May, less than four years later, it was stripped for parts worth millions and crushed, the steel teeth of an excavator tearing through its fuselage at a facility on the outskirts of Phoenix.

More aircraft are getting dismantled years, if not decades, earlier than expected, industry executives told CNBC. It is the result of a shortage of parts, none more precious and expensive than aircraft engines, that has exasperated airlines and driven up costs for years.

Even after the engines are removed, firms are looking for parts as varied as landing gear and antennae to sell.

"It's the first time in my career that involved purchasing an aircraft that's less than five years since date of manufacture," said Bill Thompson, vice president who leads aircraft and engine acquisition for the Americas at aviation asset management and trading firm EirTrade Aviation, which bought the less than 4-year-old Airbus A320neo earlier this year.

He said the company paid "single-digit" millions each for that airplane and a similarly aged one, and it expects to make a profit from selling the more than 1,000 parts it took off them before they were crushed.

"I don't know if you're going to sell the coffee makers but you never know. Sometimes you do," he said.

Twenty-one newest-generation aircraft were torn down from June 2025 through this past August, according to aviation data and advisory firm IBA Group. The scrapped planes were 6.4 years old on average, and included mostly Airbus but also some Boeing planes. That compares with an average retirement age for narrow-body airplanes of 21.7 years, IBA said.

"Anytime that you end up with any period of market softness in aircraft availability, engine availability, it's going to cause investors to look at other options," said Simon Goodson, CEO of AerFin, a company that buys, sells, repairs and leases aircraft and parts. "We always say to our aircraft and engine traders, 'Please don't fall in love with the metal.'"

Hundreds of aircraft are retired each year as manufacturers ramp up production for airlines, so older planes are swapped out for newer, more fuel-efficient models.

But aside from retiring aging planes, some owners don't want to wait around for replacement parts or repairs on younger jets.

"A year might not sound that long, but does three or four years?" said Dave Querio, CEO of Ascent Aviation Services, a company that repairs, modifies, stores and disassembles aircraft, based in Marana, Arizona, outside of Tucson. "That's the issue for the aircraft that came in in '23, '24 and '25."

In some cases, owners can reap more for the parts than they can selling the plane or paying storage costs and waiting for it to get back in the air again.

Querio said he hasn't seen "the volume of younger aircraft being torn down as I'm seeing today."

A major reason for the young retirements is the long wait and rising prices for jet engines.

One problem is that cutting-edge engines that burn hotter and use less fuel are coming up for maintenance far earlier than airlines expected.

The maintenance requests have kept engine repair and overhaul facilities backed up, from those owned by manufacturing giants GE Aerospace and RTX unit Pratt & Whitney to smaller, independent ones. Airlines and leasing firms have been hunting for spare parts and engines anywhere they can find them, like here in Goodyear, Arizona.

The trend has added more fuel to the already hot secondhand parts market. An engine fan blade could cost $50,000 or even more than $100,000 for bigger jets, Goodson said. Engines that power some Airbus A320neo aircraft have 20 of those blades. A disc for the engine, meanwhile, could cost between $1 million and $2 million.

An engine recall because of a manufacturing defect has backed up repair shops and grounded hundreds of Airbus planes around the world. Spirit Airlines bore the brunt of that problem in the United States, years before the airline collapsed.

The engine is made by Pratt & Whitney, which issued the recall in 2023 after discovering contaminated powdered metal used in making some parts. Those engines are worth close to $17 million this year at the high end, up 60% from the upper end of the range in 2019, according to IBA.

Airlines are removing some engines off retired planes to keep current aircraft flying while they wait for engines to come back from the shop.

"So you're just playing Legos and moving engines on and off aircraft," said Mike Stengel, a partner at AeroDynamic Advisory, an aerospace consulting firm.

The problems are driving up the cost to buy or rent one of the popular engines to record levels.

Renting one is ranging from about $160,000 to $180,000 a month, according to IBA data. A competing engine for the A320neo family made by CFM International, a joint venture of GE Aerospace and France's Safran, is far from a bargain, renting in a similar range of $130,000 to $180,000 a month.

The problems with young retirements predate Spirit's collapse.

The 4-year-old Airbus plane that was torn down outside Phoenix in May is a key example. The aircraft was put in storage because of the engine recall, and it stayed until it was torn apart.

But the company's demise has brought more of its Airbus fleet to the market, forcing owners to figure out if it's worth the wait for engines or other parts.

CNBC visited AerSale's facility in Goodyear on a scorching day in late June, less than two months after Spirit collapsed, where dozens of the idle yellow planes were stored. It was so soon after the airline's demise that a former Detroit-based Spirit captain, Glenn Zecchin, popped in and asked about opportunities to ferry Spirit's aircraft to the desert, and to peek in on his former airline's planes.

"I knew the airplanes were there, and I wanted to see what happened," said Zecchin, who is now flying corporate jets.

A jet parked at the storage facility and maintenance hangar still had Spirit in-flight menus and safety cards in the bungee cord seatbacks. Pilot manuals were still in the cockpit.

Not all of these aircraft will be destroyed. Some companies that owned Spirit's planes before bankruptcy will just sell their parts, but some may keep the planes in storage. Other aircraft may go on to fly for other airlines, with potential carriers ripping out Spirit's tight 28-inch seat pitch for something roomier.

But as long as they're not flying, it means paying storage fees, which vary by aircraft type. Some planes in desert facilities — selected because the arid climate can preserve the aircraft better and avoid corrosion — can sit for decades.

Others won't make it back into the air if there's enough demand for the parts and too long of a wait to get the plane flying again.

"After you get rid of all the valuable equipment and sell it back into the aftermarket, what's really left is just a metal carcass that you will literally take a crane and crush," said AeroDynamic's Stengel. Then the owner can "sell it to recyclers that will make of it what they will, maybe into beer cans or something outside of aerospace."

Ascent's Querio said some owners opt to take that route.

"It's a little melancholy to watch it at times," he said. "But sometimes, it's kind of a thrill to ... see how fast an aircraft can be crunched up and sent off to the smelter."

— CNBC's Erin Black contributed to this report.

#857. Marc Lore’s Wonder bets on Texas, automation and user-built restaurants as it mulls IPO

Less than a decade after its founding, food startup Wonder has seen its footprint and valuation soar.

Still, the company has even more ambitious plans ahead, like a subscription program to let diners run their own "restaurants," an aggressive expansion in Texas and a potential initial public offering.

Created by serial entrepreneur Marc Lore in 2018, Wonder advertises itself as "the best restaurants from across the country, all in one place." Diners can place takeout or delivery orders from its food hall-style locations on the Wonder-branded app or third-party platforms, including Grubhub, which Wonder now owns.

As Lore described it to CNBC, "Wonder is a vertically integrated food technology platform to make great food more accessible."

To achieve its goal, Wonder plans to open locations in urban, suburban and rural areas nationwide and aims to keep prices lower than the cost of delivered groceries. Thanks to its ownership of Grubhub, Wonder customers who order through the startup's app don't have to pay delivery fees, and its menu prices aren't inflated to cover the cost of commission fees to the delivery platform. Plus, Wonder is automating some of its kitchen functions, like assembling salads and bowls and soon sauces and beverages.

The company aims for food preparation and delivery to be fully autonomous by 2040, Lore said.

Now, Wonder has to convince diners its platform built on cost savings and choice makes it worth their while in a competitive industry.

Lore's lofty vision for Wonder has helped the company raise $3.3 billion to date, from investors like DoorDash, Alphabet's venture arm and Gwyneth Paltrow. Its Series D fundraising round earlier this year raised its pre-money valuation to $9 billion — just under sandwich chain Subway's sale price two years ago.

Wonder's next pitch to investors could be on an IPO road show, assuming that the window for companies across industries to go public reopens in the coming months.

"[The mission] is really why I want to go public and raise the capital that it'll take to build that kind of generational business, also to have a public currency to be able to do more M&A," Lore said. "But I can't talk about timing."

Lore's expertise lies in e-commerce, not restaurants.

He helped found the parent company of Diapers.com and sold it in 2010 to Amazon for $545 million. Just six years later, Walmart paid about $3 billion for his follow-up venture, Jet.com. Lore stayed at the retailer until 2021 to accelerate Walmart's e-commerce growth.

Basketball fans might also recognize Lore as an owner of the Minnesota Timberwolves. In 2021, he and baseball legend Alex Rodriguez agreed to buy the team for $1.5 billion, although the deal was not finalized until 2025. Lore recently struck a deal to shrink his investment to a minority stake "to focus on his business," Rodriguez said in a statement in August, referring to Wonder.

Lore's latest startup began with the idea of bringing food delivery to consumers' homes through sprinter vans outfitted with mobile kitchens. The trucks lasted roughly two years before Wonder phased them out in favor of brick-and-mortar locations, which could serve more food options and lower costs.

Lore's successful track record has helped him win over investors for Wonder.

"I joke around at GV that I do enterprise software investing, AI investing and anything that Marc Lore is running as an active CEO," said Dave Munichiello, managing partner of Alphabet's venture capital arm GV, who has known Lore since his Diapers.com days and invested in Jet.com through GV.

Lore himself thinks that his vision is the reason for Wonder's billions of dollars of funding.

"Everybody eats," he said. "It's defensible from AI."

Wonder has spent more than $1 billion on acquisitions, not including the deals where financial details were not disclosed. Its purchases of Grubhub, delivery and logistics provider Relay, meal-kit provider Blue Apron and buzzy New York restaurant concepts have been expensive.

But Lore said he sees a "tremendous total addressable market" for Wonder if it's able to sell high-quality food at accessible prices, particularly in exurban and rural areas with few dining options beyond fast food and gas stations.

From Virginia to Massachusetts, 170 Wonder storefronts have appeared over the last three years. Next year, the company plans to enter Texas for the first time — with a goal of at least 100 locations in the state by the end of 2027.

Wonder prepares its food at commissary kitchens, then transports it to each individual location, where employees unseal, cook and finish the menu items. Its storefronts are all electric, without gas or venting, so Wonder can quickly move into a new space and cut down on renovations and red tape.

Its locations serve menu items from more than two dozen "restaurants," including Dabba Indian Kitchen, Tejas Barbecue and Pop Salad. Celebrity chefs including Bobby Flay, Michael Symon and Marcus Samuelsson receive up-front payments and equity in Wonder to let the company use their names for some of those concepts. More recent additions include acquisitions such as Blue Ribbon Fried Chicken and Salt Hank's, brands with established followings.

With its wide variety of options housed under one roof, Wonder has drawn comparisons to ghost kitchens, the once-buzzy startups without dine-in seating that flamed out as food delivery orders fell after the Covid pandemic. Once considered a potential disruptor to the restaurant industry, ghost kitchen companies such as Kitchen United have sought bankruptcy protection and wound down their operations.

"You can see there's a front of house [in the] brick and mortar," he said. "You'll see people at lunchtime, sitting there enjoying lunch, so it's not ghost-y in that way. But I think it's also just in the fact that we're vertically integrated."

Wonder has never marketed itself as a ghost kitchen, instead calling itself "a new kind of food hall" or a "modern food court" before landing on a "vertically integrated food technology platform."

Starting Nov. 17, the company plans to take the next step with Wonder Create. Influencers, entrepreneurs and brands can pay $10 a month to start their own "restaurant" based out of a Wonder location and earn a portion of the revenue from orders. Artificial intelligence will help them make their brand, including name, logo, pricing and recipes.

"It could be a restaurant for you and your friends, or you could be an influencer that has a following," Lore said.

He wants Wonder Create to democratize restaurant ownership — minus the capital, overhead costs or oversight of kitchen operations. The platform will eventually be available to the public, with users deciding menu items based on Wonder's pantry.

Besides Wonder Create, the company is also using AI for another initiative. MEL, a proprietary AI platform, can use a customer's health information, like data from an Oura ring or Whoop tracker or even blood biomarkers, to prepare breakfast, lunch and dinner for its user.

MEL is not currently available to Wonder customers, although Lore uses AI to advise his own meals, like the chicken muffin he ate for breakfast on a Tuesday in October.

While Wonder has won over venture capitalists, the battle for diners' stomachs and wallets is tougher.

Budget-conscious consumers have been dining out less often and becoming pickier when they do so. And while food delivery is still more popular than it was before the pandemic, consumers have pulled back on delivery orders compared with their Covid-era peak. Fees and high menu prices, which offset eateries' commission fees with apps, make delivery even more expensive than dining at a restaurant.

Plus, some consumers are skeptical about Wonder more specifically. Posts on social media decry the company's food quality and its funding from venture capital.

"It's OK, but it's just average," Amir Ings, a 27-year-old living in Philadelphia, said in a TikTok reviewing an order of three tacos from Wonder in late 2025. "My expectations were already in the gutter — it's not as bad as I thought it was going to be, but it's still not great. And I paid like $16 for this, but it should have been no more than $8."

Lore told CNBC that Wonder receives thousands of reviews every day from customers, rating the taste, temperature and portion size of their orders on a scale from 1 to 10. Wonder starts its board meetings by talking about taste scores, Munichiello said.

"We're maniacally focused on food quality .... We're constantly iterating on the meals that score lower, and see over time, the last 12 months, our taste scores have improved, our Google reviews have improved," Lore said. "I do expect food quality to improve as we scale, which is not always the case."

Despite lackluster reviews on social media, Wonder has fans. Based on transaction data from Indagari, Wonder had a higher weighted average retention rate — how likely a diner is to return — and higher lifetime value — how much a customer has spent over time — than Cava and Sweetgreen.

Plus, Wonder has succeeded with locations in the most competitive markets for restaurants in the country.

"They made this company work well in the hardest place in the country to stand up this model: New York," Munichiello said. "You have so much choice in New York."

Ultimately, Wonder wants to win over the rest of the U.S. — and public market investors.

In March, the company hired Gabrielle Scheibe Rabinovitch, previously at payment processor Worldpay, as its chief financial officer as it prepares to go public.

Lore declined to comment on the timing of a potential IPO to CNBC, although he has previously told Fortune that the company will be "ready and prepared to go public early next year."

Since buying Blue Apron and Grubhub, Wonder has been operating at a financial loss, The Information previously reported. It plans to bring down the price of its meals by leaning into automation and drone delivery. In the long term, that plan could cut costs, although laying the groundwork won't be cheap.

In recent years, investors have grown less tolerant of startups pursuing growth at all costs, according to Avery Marquez, director of investment strategies at Renaissance Capital. To sway investors, unprofitable companies have to show they are taking steps toward profitability — or that the growth will continue long after the IPO.

#850. FTC and 20 states sue Dollar General, alleging retailer overcharged shoppers

The Federal Trade Commission said Monday that it filed a lawsuit along with 20 states accusing Dollar General of charging customers more than the prices it displays on shelves, price tags and in advertisements.

Shares of Dollar General dropped about 4% after the FTC announced the lawsuit.

The complaint alleges that thousands of Dollar General stores have "taken advantage of vulnerable shoppers by extracting millions of dollars in unlawful overcharges with insufficient safeguards to prevent the practice."

It also argues that Dollar General has received "tens of thousands" of complaints from consumers about charging higher than advertised prices, but that it has not implemented policies to address the problem.

The states joining the FTC in the lawsuit are Alabama, Arizona, Arkansas, California, Connecticut, Florida, Illinois, Iowa, Kansas, Louisiana, Michigan, Mississippi, Nebraska, New Jersey, North Carolina, Ohio, Oklahoma, Tennessee, Utah and Wisconsin.

"Dollar General has betrayed the trust of millions of American consumers and imposed significant financial harm on communities and consumers who are already struggling to make their paychecks last," said Christopher Mufarrige, director of the FTC's Bureau of Consumer Protection.

Dollar General issued a statement in response to the lawsuit, saying it will "defend this litigation vigorously."

"As with other retailers and despite our policies and procedures, occasional price discrepancies can occur," the statement read. "When that happens, our store teams are instructed, empowered, and expected to make things right for the customer. In recent years, we have enhanced our price-accuracy processes and remain committed to continuous improvement."

Dollar General's statement said that it has cooperated with the FTC's investigation since February and has produced documents, data and sworn testimony. In September, the FTC sent a draft complaint and settlement offer. The company said it had sent a monetary counteroffer and was surprised when the FTC filed the lawsuit rather than responding to the offer.

The suit comes as shoppers look for discounts while they face elevated food and gas prices. Officials across the political spectrum also aim to show their efforts to protect consumers from high costs ahead of the midterm election next week.

#760. I took two iPhones on vacation. The Duo won me over.

“I think I’m going to switch back to my other iPhone.”

I’ll admit, I said this multiple times during my five-day vacation with the new iPhone Duo, after encountering frustrations with broken apps, unfamiliar gestures (a return to Touch ID, argh!), and camera concerns that had me lugging around two phones.

By the time the trip was over, I didn’t want to go back.

When I first unboxed the Duo, a loaner from Apple, I was genuinely intrigued. I felt a flutter of anticipation as I peeled back the factory seal wrap and booted up the device. In retrospect, this was probably the first signal that Duo was going to ultimately win me over.

As a tech reporter, I’ve received a lot of test devices over the years, so unfortunately, opening a package with a new gadget inside is no longer the thrill it used to be. But sometime in the days leading up to the Duo’s ship date, I found myself actually wanting one – I had started doing the math and comparing upgrade options that would allow me to afford the $2K the phone would cost. I started scrolling TikTok reviews of Duo cases. I hesitated not only because of the price but because moving to the Duo is a major change – especially coming from an iPhone 17 Pro Max. The phones could not be more different.

As I was about to head out on vacation when the Duo arrived, I decided to tote my 17 Pro Max with me too, as something of a safety net. I ended up using the Duo more, and grew comfortable with it over the course of the trip, despite some frustrations.

There’s always a psychological switching cost in changing phones, but particularly going from a high-end phone to a more experimental device like the Duo. For someone used to the iPhone’s long-perfected tech and thin rectangular shape, the Duo’s short, squat, and decidedly novel nature was somewhat off-putting at first.

“Where is all that screen real estate I used to have?” I lamented. “Will this thing even fit comfortably in my pocket?”

As it turns out, it does. Front and back pockets, in fact. Even while wearing a case. You can sit down with it in your back pocket, effectively squashing it, and it does not break. (I meant to be more careful, but I forgot to be, repeatedly, as I tend to carry phones in my back pocket when walking around. I sat on the phone countless times. It’s clearly not that fragile.)

I also dropped the phone (by accident!) at least three times. Twice from about table height and once when it got pushed off the side of the bed. It did not fall on soft carpet, but rather on concrete, tile, and wood floors. It didn’t break then either, and the screen didn’t crack. I wouldn’t call these professional drop tests by any means, but it certainly was a relief for someone fairly clumsy with their phone.

Of course, I did encounter some frustrations. I had to scroll more to read back through text threads because of the Duo’s small outer screen; unoptimized apps with numerous controls sometimes had key features tucked away in a “more” (three-dot) menu, leading to a hunt to find the option you needed; and, on multiple occasions, the Google Search app just froze.

Though not Apple’s fault, the latter turned out to be my biggest annoyance software-wise, given that Search is one of my go-to apps especially when traveling. On vacation, I open the app repeatedly to look up points of interest, restaurant reviews, distances between towns, weather, and more. But Google’s app would just stop working, and tapping its back button did nothing.

This seemed to be a problem specific to the Search app’s AI Mode, which these days you’re often dropped into without actually requesting it. (Even more reason to be annoyed by AI Search!) The workaround has been to force-close the app, so the next time you open it, your returned to the home screen with the search box. (It amazes me that, in a time when Google is facing a search decline because of AI, it wouldn’t have prioritized making its Search app functional on Apple’s latest device!)

In addition, many of the early reviews pointed out how many apps were not updated or redesigned for Duo at launch, which meant they had a telltale black bar on the side, cramping the limited screen real estate when the phone is closed even further. This is true, but personally speaking, I didn’t think it was a dealbreaker for making a switch to the Duo.

For one thing, companies have been pushing out their Duo-optimized updates fairly quickly. Those that have not – and there are still more than a few! – continue to be usable. They’re just kind of … ugly.

Google Maps was not Duo-optimized during the trip, yet I had no problem using it for driving directions, local search and discovery, route planning, and the like. If anything, the black-barred apps felt more like an aesthetic issue than a functional problem, even though they’re smaller than usual when the Duo is folded.

I expect all the major apps will eventually update. As for the older apps that never do? Well, it’s probably time to move on from apps no longer being maintained, I’d say, particularly in an era when older software is being exploited left and right by AI-enabled hacking tools.

However, I will agree with the other reviewers who complained about the lack of Face ID.

This is the Duo’s biggest drawback – not the screen, not the camera, not the lack of an Action button for launching your preferred AI assistant. It’s Touch ID.

It is much easier to look at your phone than to fiddle around with finger presses. While I easily avoided the need to use Touch ID to unlock the device – I am almost always wearing an Apple Watch, which serves as an alternative authentication method – I often stumbled when it came to using apps and Apple Pay.

Tap-and-pay is one of the areas where it’s immediately obvious how much smoother Face ID has made everyday tasks. Returning to Touch ID at the time of payment was cumbersome, given that you’re often holding shopping bags, a purse, and, in my case, a dog leash, in addition to the phone you’re using to pay with.

There’s a certain amount of what I guess I’ll call “muscle memory” that you have to overcome when you switch back to Touch ID. I found the easiest grip was to cradle the phone in your hand and use your thumb to authenticate. But where I repeatedly messed up was remembering to keep my thumb or finger on the Touch ID sensor long enough to authenticate. At times, my finger wasn’t situated quite right (I blame the dog pulling on the leash!), and I had to readjust. “I miss Face ID!,” I complained multiple times at checkout.

Though returning to Touch ID was something I got used to, it’s far from ideal. I can see this as being a real stopping point for some early adopters who would otherwise want to move to the Duo. It’s not an insurmountable problem, but it’s among the top reasons not to get the Duo now, in my opinion. (A future version is said to support Face ID.)

Another reason to be wary of the Duo? The camera. Much has been said about the Duo’s lesser camera system. The Duo has two rear cameras to my iPhone 17 Pro Max’s three. It has no telephoto lens. It has a 10x digital zoom versus the Pro’s 40x. The Pro has more advanced hardware for low-light photography and supports ProRAW, ProRes, and other advanced features.

Very simply put: if you are an iPhone photographer or a content creator, you shouldn’t consider the Duo as your primary device.

For, you know, us normal folks, the Duo’s camera is okay. Not great, but fine. What were you going to do with those photos you’re snapping anyway? Post them to Instagram Stories or Facebook? Text them to a friend? Look back on them fondly when they reappear in your photo widget on your Home Screen? Turn them into a Lock Screen wallpaper? None of these things requires a Pro-level camera.

Below are a few photos shot with the Duo versus others shot with the 17 Pro Max. None of the photos are using any special settings or filters; they are intentionally just average, non-professional point-and-shoot shots. Among my complaints are that the Duo’s image processing can, at times, make fine details, like the leaves on trees, look overprocessed. Meanwhile, distant landscapes can appear flatter or smoothed over — though, to be fair, the Blue Ridge Mountains are naturally hazy, so it’s hard to say how much of that is the camera. (Shots from the mountaintop were in low-light conditions — it was getting dark out.)

As for why to get the phone? This one’s easy; it’s a foldable. From Apple. First, foldables are whimsical in a way that slab phones will never be. They are toys for grown-ups!  Developers are also clearly having fun building for the new format, so there are lots of new apps to play with. Plus, it’s a great way to watch movies, particularly with that Duo Folio case with a kickstand.

Second, it’s a foldable from Apple. The company has taken its time to get it right, which comes through in the details, from the barely visible crease to the consistent aspect ratio between screens, allowing content to scale proportionally. It’s also a foldable in Apple’s software ecosystem, which matters for those who move between Apple devices and services and who use and pay for apps that are designed for iOS specifically.

But here’s a small confession: in day-to-day life, I didn’t unfold the Duo as much as I thought I would. (To be clear: I absolutely did unfold it! I just thought I would do it more!)

There are two reasons for this. One: unfolding the device in public invites conversation, and it feels sort of like a flex; I’m not always in a place where I have time to chat. I expect this problem to diminish in time as the novelty wears off.

That said, I still had roughly half a dozen conversations with strangers while I was on vacation, from fellow shoppers to servers to business owners. I even handed them the device at times (when they couldn’t easily run off with it!) and let them fold and unfold it. One person wanted to see the Kid Cue feature in particular, which displays playful Snoopy animations on the phone’s outer display to get children to look toward the camera when you take their picture. They were genuinely delighted by this.

Anecdotally, the consensus was that the unfolded screen was something of a marvel to most: “You can’t see the hinge!” “Is that a screen protector or is it just like that?” (The inner screen has a matte, nano-texture finish, reducing glare. Once explained, it was also praised as “pretty cool.” )

“What if you get sand in it?,” a former Samsung Galaxy Fold owner asked. Their older Android foldable had been ruined after a trip to the beach where sand got into the hinge, causing damage to the screen. (This was not uncommon at the time.) The Duo is rated IP68, the “6” signifying higher dust resistance, making it dust-tight. The Galaxy Z Fold 8 was rated IP48, by comparison. I’m not expecting it will have the same issues, though I do hope a reviewer takes it to the beach soon!

I am certain that at least one of the handful of strangers I spoke to had never actually seen a phone that folded before. The fact that Apple has now made one means this concept will no longer be niche; it will be known to the mainstream.

The other reason I didn’t unfold it as much as I thought is that doing so feels like the start of a more intentional screen time session. When I do unfold the phone now, it’s because I’m truly engaging with what I’m doing on the phone, like watching a show on Netflix or dragging two apps side-by-side in split-screen mode for multitasking. (I love reading news in Inoreader and having X open off to the side to see what people are saying about the news, but that’s a use case that won’t apply to everyone!)

The split-screen feature is easy to use, too – with the app open, you swipe up from the bottom, then drag it to the left or right edge. Target boxes on each side help you visualize where it can move to, which is helpful. (You can also long-press on an app icon to open a split view, but I don’t do this as much.)

But as something of a “mini” phone in terms of screen size when closed, I’m somehow less drawn to what I think of as time-wasting apps like TikTok, Instagram, or casual games.

I’ve been thinking about this, and I don’t believe it’s just the lack of screen real estate when the phone is folded, or the fact that vertical apps haven’t been designed for the Duo’s unfolded landscape screen. Those concerns make sense from a practical, usability standpoint, of course, but there’s something more to this behavioral change, I believe.

For years, phones have been getting bigger, taller, thinner, and more comfortable to hold. Consequently, they easily sucked us into their digital worlds. With the Duo, the design seems to interrupt the way smartphones effortlessly pull us in, begging us to open apps and scroll endlessly.

Rather, the Duo feels like a device you open and use when you have reason to do so, not something you reflexively turn to when you’re bored or have a few minutes to spare. Because the best app use cases are when the phone is unfolded, and unfolding is something you do with careful consideration (and careful hands!), device use becomes more thoughtful.

This is not a bad thing. It also aligns with the embrace of retro tech already underway, where knobs and dials and buttons make engaging with technology feel more deliberate.

And for that reason, despite its faults, the Duo won me over. It’s a phone that’s compelling and fun — a phone I enjoyed picking up, but also found easier to put down.

That may change, though. I recently experienced an alternative fiber called renoa, developed by a startup called Everbloom, that’s washable and promises to be more affordable. It could bring the cashmere experience, via a substitute material, to more products and people.

Renoa is made from sources of waste protein, including cashmere and wool scraps as well as down and feather “lint” leftover from the bedding industry. Everbloom processes the waste into a powder and blends it with a biodegradable biopolymer, and the combination is then extruded to the right length and diameter to mimic specific natural fibers.

Renoa was recently approved as the first new fiber classification in 25 years by the U.S. Federal Trade Commission, which regulates fiber labeling in the U.S.

Everbloom has been developing the new fiber for three years, and is currently in the process of scaling up. The startup developed its own AI model for material science, and a cashmere substitute is the first product.

Last month, I stopped by Everbloom’s headquarters in New York City, which sits 15 floors above 5th Avenue, kitty corner from Bryant Park. There, co-founder and CEO Sim Gulati walked me through a range of products made with its company’s cashmere-like renoa, including a crewneck sweater, a pair of knit gloves, and a scarf.

The sweater and gloves were soft and light, but the scarf was the most notable. It didn’t look like much, but it had been through the kind of hell and back that a pure cashmere scarf wouldn’t survive. It was a custom item that Everbloom had knit to test renoa, and Gulati had been wearing and washing it for months, including a trip through the tumble dryer before I saw it. It wasn’t pilled, and Gulati said it hadn’t shrunk, either.

Everbloom is currently working with a range of companies to get renoa into consumer products. The first are likely to feature renoa as a blended fiber, making up around 20% of the material in a cashmere sweater, for example. The first product is expected to hit the market in the second quarter of 2027 Gulati said.

The 80/20 cashmere-renoa blend that I felt was extremely close to the real thing, and Gulati said that additional finishing that accompanies commercial garments should eliminate any differences without compromising the washability and stability of the garment. After my visit, I kept thinking about various items in my closet that could benefit from those features. When renoa lands on shelves next year, I’ll be keeping an eye out for it.

#714. A 'canary in the coal mine': Measles resurgence exposes weaknesses in U.S. public health, experts say

For nearly two years, the U.S. has had to swallow an uncomfortable reality: A highly infectious disease it once eliminated is spreading again.

More than 4,400 measles cases and at least three deaths have been reported nationwide this year, marking the country's worst outbreak in more than three decades. The spike followed nearly 2,300 cases in 2025. But many public health experts argue that the return of measles isn't the only concerning development — it's also what the resurgence signals about America that's causing alarm.

Measles is often described as one of the most contagious viruses, and it is uniquely unforgiving of weaknesses in public health defenses. A safe and highly effective vaccine has existed for decades.

But the virus's spread exposes declining vaccination rates, eroding trust in health institutions and growing challenges in persuading people to protect themselves, their children and their communities, experts said.

Together, those vulnerabilities have made measles a revealing test of the country's public health defenses. For some experts, the resurgence of a disease the U.S. eliminated more than two decades ago is a warning sign of its ability to contain future threats, from other vaccine-preventable diseases to emerging pathogens.

"Measles is the canary in the coal mine for so many different reasons," said Jennifer Nuzzo, an epidemiology professor and director of the Pandemic Center at Brown University School of Public Health. "It's the canary in the coal mine for other vaccine-preventable illnesses, where we could see a return of things that used to harm kids, but also for very acute, no-notice dangerous infectious disease emergencies."

The U.S. is on track to lose its official measles elimination status during an upcoming review by the Pan American Health Organization, which will meet in Washington, D.C., in mid-November.

"It says a lot about a country if it is unable to control measles," said Nuzzo, who is one of 10 experts CNBC spoke with about the ongoing outbreak and its implications.

The U.S. has the tools to prevent measles from spreading. But pockets of low vaccination coverage are leaving the country vulnerable.

The measles, mumps and rubella, or MMR, vaccine is about 97% effective against measles after two doses, while a single dose is 93% effective, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Yet data aggregated by the CDC shows that vaccination coverage for that shot among U.S. kindergarteners fell to 92.4% in the 2025-2026 school year, which is below the 95% threshold public health officials generally recommend to prevent sustained transmission.

That translates to 280,000 kindergarteners who did not complete their MMR vaccination series, according to the CDC. Childhood vaccination exemptions in the U.S. also reached a record high 4.2% for incoming kindergartners during the 2025-2026 school year, up from 3.6% the previous year, the agency says.

"The bottom line is that not enough people are vaccinating their kids," said Brown's Nuzzo.

While some cases stem from international travel, much of the recent spread has been driven by local transmission in "too many" communities where vaccination coverage falls below 95%, she added.

Among states across the U.S. collecting MMR vaccine data, 67% of counties and jurisdictions now have immunization rates below 95%, according to a September investigation by NBC News.

Recent U.S. outbreaks have often occurred in "closely knit communities" where people have frequent contact and low vaccination coverage, said Dr. William Moss, an epidemiology professor at Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health.

But declining vaccination rates do not mean most Americans have turned against immunization. Polling suggests broad support for childhood vaccines. A July KFF survey found that many Americans sit in a more uncertain middle ground about the shots, leaving room for conflicting information to influence their decisions.

"This is not an edge-case issue," Nuzzo said, adding that the vast majority of parents still vaccinate their children.

Even if outbreaks are concentrated in a minority of communities, it can create a "dangerous situation" far from those areas, she said. Infants too young to be vaccinated and people who cannot receive immunizations for medical reasons can be particularly vulnerable when the virus spreads into communities with high vaccination rates.

Those risks come as the Trump administration reshapes federal vaccine policy.

President Donald Trump signed an executive order in August suggesting that the combined MMR vaccine be split into separate shots, while repeating false claims about its safety. Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has also overhauled a key federal advisory committee responsible for recommending childhood immunizations, though the changes are frozen amid ongoing litigation.

Several experts told CNBC that such moves risk further eroding confidence in vaccines and weakening efforts to protect children.

Public health infrastructure was also "really decimated by Covid," and the CDC in particular lost many experienced scientists and physicians "who had spent their lives working on vaccine-preventable diseases," said Scott Weaver, a virologist who is the research director of Galveston National Laboratory.

He added that the CDC, as of September, is also no longer immediately incorporating state-reported "measles-associated" deaths into its national published tally, making it harder to track outbreaks in real time. The agency lists three measles-related deaths in 2026, but Pennsylvania has reported at least five deaths.

"We have folks in federal health leadership right now that are throwing gasoline on the fire, and that is not helping the situation," said Jess Steier, a public health scientist and founder and CEO of Unbiased Science, an organization that aims to make complex scientific concepts understandable.

If the U.S. fails to materially improve vaccination rates, the measles resurgence could extend beyond ongoing outbreaks.

At "current" state-level vaccination rates, measles could become endemic again in the U.S., resulting in an estimated 851,300 cases over 25 years, according to a large-scale epidemiological model published in JAMA in April 2025 by researchers at Stanford and other institutions. Under that scenario, the model predicts the measles cases could lead to 170,200 hospitalizations and 2,550 deaths over that period.

The model, which simulated infectious disease spread in the U.S. at various childhood vaccination levels, relies on immunization data from 2004 to 2023 for that baseline scenario. The model assumes that diseases are introduced by someone who travels abroad and brings them back, most commonly a U.S. citizen who isn't vaccinated.

While many people are currently immune through vaccination, outbreaks can increase in size and frequency if immunization rates decline over a longer period.

Michael Osterholm, an epidemiologist and the director of the Center for Infectious Disease Research and Policy at the University of Minnesota, said he views the measles situation in Canada as a direct warning for the U.S.

Canada lost its official measles elimination status in November after a multi-province outbreak that generated more than 5,400 cases across 10 jurisdictions in 2025. Canada has a population of more than 40 million people.

"We have a population of 350 million. If you extrapolate, if we had the very same experience that Canada had over the next year or two, that would be up to 50,000 cases of measles in the U.S.," Osterholm said. "I think that's a potential future we have to be mindful of."

An even larger measles outbreak isn't the only potential problem.

Vaccination coverage for other childhood diseases has similarly declined. The share of U.S. kindergartners vaccinated against polio fell to 92.4% in the 2025-2026 school year, while coverage for diphtheria, tetanus and acellular pertussis, or DTaP, dropped to 92%, according to CDC data.

The U.S. has already seen a sharp rise in pertussis, or whooping cough. U.S. reported cases reached roughly 43,300 in 2024 and nearly 28,000 in 2025, up substantially from pandemic-era levels, according to the CDC. Brown's Nuzzo said the resurgence reflects, in part, fewer people getting vaccinated and the fact that protection from the pertussis vaccine wanes over time.

The simulation model published in JAMA also examined how falling childhood immunization rates could affect the future spread of several diseases previously eliminated from the U.S. through vaccines. Under current vaccination levels, the researchers found that diseases including rubella and polio were unlikely to become endemic in the U.S.

But in a hypothetical scenario in which childhood vaccination rates fell by 50%, the model projected 9.9 million cases of rubella, 4.3 million cases of polio and 197 cases of diphtheria over 25 years.

Rubella can cause devastating complications when an infection during pregnancy passes to a developing fetus, while polio can cause paralysis and death, said Dr. Nathan Lo, assistant professor of infectious diseases at Stanford Medicine.

The projections are not predictions that these outbreaks will occur, but they illustrate how much risks could grow if vaccination coverage deteriorates.

"What vaccines often buy you is not just the protection, but also less uncertainty," said Mathew Kiang, an assistant professor of epidemiology and population health at Stanford Medicine who also worked on the simulation model. "I think even more frightening is just the really wide uncertainty that we just wouldn't be sure of what could happen."

The measles resurgence is also raising questions about how effective the U.S. can be at detecting and responding to emerging public health threats before they spread.

"Public health is all about prevention," said Dr. Manisha Juthani, president of the Association of State and Territorial Health Officials and commissioner of the Connecticut Department of Public Health. She said the country must maintain its ability to detect threats, invest in surveillance, and recognize that infectious diseases do not respect national borders.

Those capabilities are crucial for curbing emerging pathogens, especially when scientists initially know little about how a new disease spreads, how severe it is or how to treat it. A pathogen could spread before vaccines or treatments are available, making early detection, clear public communication and coordination especially important.

Other experts argue that weaknesses in those capabilities could also have implications for the country's defenses against deliberate biological attacks.

In an April 2025 op-ed, Steph Batalis, a research fellow at Georgetown University's Center for Security and Emerging Technology, argued that the measles outbreak exposed a broader vulnerability in the U.S.' biodefense strategy and, specifically, a weakened ability to deter potential biological attacks.

She argued that the country's struggle to contain a known, vaccine-preventable disease "might only embolden those who see our public health vulnerabilities as an opportunity."

But Osterholm, who works extensively on bioterrorism, said low MMR immunization rates and rising measles cases do not necessarily indicate that Americans would refuse a vaccine or antibiotic during a biological emergency. The shots and drugs used to counter biological threats can differ from those routinely administered to the public, he said, and the prospect of a severe outbreak could prompt urgency among the public to seek protection.

That point highlights an important limitation: The measles resurgence by itself does not establish whether the U.S. could withstand entirely different or novel public health threats.

But as a disease the U.S. knows how to prevent spreads, experts say the stakes of addressing those vulnerabilities extend to whatever might come next.

A second wave of cyberattacks is suspected to have hit supermarket refrigerators across Europe, causing fridges to increase temperatures and spoil food at dozens of stores across Belgium, Ireland, Luxembourg and Sweden.

Supermarket giants Ahold Delhaize and Colruyt confirmed the hacks overnight, with one Belgian company that operates Delhaize’s systems saying that the hackers had changed the settings to defrost, per Reuters. Stores have experienced heavy food losses from the cyberattacks, forcing supermarkets to throw out goods that had defrosted or were otherwise no longer consumable.

The cyberattacks follow earlier refrigerator hacks across in France last month that caused food spoilage at hundreds of supermarkets by deliberately raising the temperature on the units. Security researchers said that the cause of the hacks were internet-connected refrigerators made by Danish company Danfoss, which was no longer providing security updates. Security company Claroty warned back in August that those refrigerator units were at risk of remote hacks and outages.

The freezer hacks underscore how cyberattacks can have real-world effects, including on the food supply chain, often as a result of weak or overlooked cybersecurity practices. In 2025, a cyberattack on food distributor giant UNFI resulted in food shortages and empty shelves at grocery stores and supermarkets across the United States.

U.S. Department of Defense officials are reportedly investigating whether freezer units on military bases were hacked by Iran-backed hackers. Iranian hackers were also blamed for cyberattacks targeting U.S. water providers earlier this year.

#688. Ninja’s first-ever microwave just replaced two of my kitchen appliances at once

I never thought I’d get excited about replacing my microwave, but the Ninja Crispi Microwave and Air Fryer has made a pretty convincing case for ditching two appliances and making room for one. Combining a microwave with a built-in air fryer, it’s designed to speed up everyday cooking, give leftovers their crunch back and, perhaps best of all, free up some valuable kitchen counter space.

Priced at $449.99, it’s certainly not a budget-friendly microwave. But this is no ordinary machine for heating leftover soup. Ninja has paired a 1,000W microwave with a 1,700W air fryer system, powered by its FusionCrisp technology, which combines the speed of microwave cooking with the golden, crispy results you’d typically expect from an air fryer.

Think leftover pizza, chicken tenders, fries, and all those foods that tend to turn sad and soggy in a traditional microwave. Instead, the idea is to get food that’s piping hot and juicy on the inside, with a deliciously crisp, golden finish on the outside. In other words, no more choosing between speed and texture.

Of course, microwave and air fryer combinations aren’t entirely new. Brands such as Smeg have already explored the concept, while plenty of modern microwaves come with a range of additional cooking functions.

What makes the Ninja Crispi Microwave particularly interesting, however, is its FusionCrisp technology, which is designed to bring both cooking methods together rather than simply offering two separate functions. It comes with a 5.5-quart glass cooking dish and a nonstick crisper plate, which sits on top when you want to air fry and crisp up your food.

Another part that makes it stand out is that there isn’t a rotating plate inside, opening up a lot more space to cook more dishes at once.

It’s not just about reheating leftovers. For example, if you want to make a pasta bake, the microwave can cook the pasta, while the air fryer crisps up the cheese on top.

There’s one thing to consider before buying it, though. Measuring 20.3 x 17.56 x 14.1 inches, the microwave is hardly compact. It takes up a fair amount of counter space, so while it could free up room if you’re replacing a separate microwave and air fryer, it’s not necessarily the ideal solution for a small apartment kitchen.

Then there’s the $449.99 price tag. That’s a considerable amount to spend on a microwave, particularly when a basic model can handle everyday reheating for a fraction of the cost. But if you’re someone who regularly reaches for both a microwave and an air fryer, the combination could make a lot more sense.
